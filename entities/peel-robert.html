Sir Robert Peel

Sir Robert Peel (1788–1850) was the dominant Conservative statesman of the middle years of the century, twice Prime Minister, and the author of the two great administrative and legislative achievements that remade the British state: the establishment of a uniform and disciplined police in London and the tariff and currency reforms that culminated in the repeal of the Corn Laws. His career was a long march from the rigid Torism of his early years to the free-trade liberalism of his last — a progress that split his party and ended in his fall, but that fixed the direction of English policy for a generation. Peel governed, as his contemporaries observed, less by the arts of party than by the power of his own mind and the authority of his administrative mastery.

Early career and the character of the minister

Peel entered public life as the heir of a manufacturing fortune, the son of a Lancashire cotton baronet, and rose rapidly under the old Tory system. He was Chief Secretary for Ireland, Home Secretary under Liverpool, and the author of the reform of the criminal law and of the metropolitan police — the “Peelers” of the new London police force that bore his name. His early politics were those of the post-war Tory ascendancy: hostile to parliamentary reform, and the mover of the repressive legislation of the postwar years. His conversion to Catholic Emancipation in 1829 — carried against his own past and his own party — first showed the pattern of his career: the statesman who would sacrifice consistency and party to what he conceived to be the necessity of the country. He resigned rather than oppose emancipation, and for years afterwards was a figure of suspicion to the men he had deserted.

The Tamworth Manifesto and the reformed era

After the Reform Act of 1832, Peel set himself to reconcile the Conservative party to the new constitution. His Tamworth Manifesto of December 1834, issued on forming his first, short-lived ministry, accepted the Reform Act as final and promised a policy of careful, moderate reform — a declaration that fixed the character of Conservatism for the Victorian age. His “Hundred Days” ministry of 1834–35 fell quickly, defeated on the ecclesiastical questions of the day, but Peel had established himself as the leader of a reconstructed party — the Conservatives, as the Tories now called themselves — capable of governing in a reformed Parliament. In opposition through the later 1830s he watched the Whigs struggle, and prepared himself and his party for power.

The great ministry, 1841–1846

Peel’s second ministry, formed after the general election of 1841, was the greatest administration of the period. He inherited a country in deep financial difficulty — annual deficits, deepening distress, and a revenue that would not meet the expenditure — and he addressed himself to the whole body of the country’s difficulties. Martineau’s account of the ministry emphasises the union of financial and commercial reform with the determination to apply principle to the state of the economy. Peel revived the income tax (abandoned since 1816) at sevenpence in the pound to meet the deficit, and set about a vast reduction of the tariff — remitting duties on raw materials and manufactured articles and simplifying the fiscal system. In Ireland he attempted to “kill Home Rule by kindness,” through the Maynooth grant, the new colleges, and the Encumbered Estates legislation. His government carried the Factory Act reforms, the Mines and Collieries Act, and the great legislative work that crowded the session.

Peel’s financial and commercial policy rested on the currency settlement as well as the tariff. In 1844 he carried the Bank Charter Act, separating the issue of the Bank of England from its banking department and fixing the note issues of the country at a legal maximum — the embodiment of the currency principle that every note beyond the bullion should be covered by gold. His speeches of May 1844, as Tooke recorded, insisted on the fixed metallic standard as the only sure regulator of the currency. The Act was the deliberate work of a minister who believed that sound money and free trade were the twin foundations of national prosperity.

The repeal of the Corn Laws

The culminating act of Peel’s career was the repeal of the Corn Laws. For years he had been moving toward free trade in private, convinced that the protective duties on grain benefited the landed interest at the expense of the people and impeded the commercial greatness of the country. The decisive occasion was the potato blight of 1845, which threatened Ireland with a terrible famine and made the free importation of food a national necessity. Peel, unable to carry his Cabinet and unable to secure the formation of a Whig ministry, returned to office resolved upon repeal. In January 1846 he announced his conversion, and carried the repeal of the Corn Laws in June with the support of the Whigs and Radicals against the mass of his own party. Disraeli rallied the Protectionists, and the ministry fell immediately afterwards on the Irish Coercion Bill. Peel’s final speech — in which he declared that he left a name “perhaps to be remembered in the pages of history” — closed the great administration, and the Conservative Party was split, not to recover a majority until the 1870s.

Retirement, death, and legacy

Peel spent the few remaining years of his life in the quiet authority of a statesman above party, giving his support to the Liberal governments that carried on his free-trade policy. He died on 2 July 1850, thrown from his horse on Constitution Hill, and the country gave him a funeral worthy of a national minister. Martineau’s closing tribute to his last position in the British legislature called it “the noblest that, in our period of time, could be held by any man” — the position of the statesman who had governed against his own party for the good of the country. Peel’s legacy was double: the administrative and fiscal reconstruction of the British state — sound currency, free trade, a reformed tariff — and the lesson, learned and resented by his own party, that a minister might be greater than his following. He was the architect of the settled free-trade and gold-standard policy that the Victorian age inherited, and his name stood, with Gladstone’s, as the founder of the modern Conservative tradition of fiscal and administrative reform.
