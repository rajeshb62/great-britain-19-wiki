Parliamentary Reform

Parliamentary reform in the United Kingdom between the Union and the mid-century meant the remaking of the House of Commons — the destruction of the rotten boroughs, the transfer of representation to the new manufacturing towns, and the widening of the franchise from the narrow body of electors of the old system toward a larger, and eventually a mass, electorate. It was the central constitutional question of the first half of the nineteenth century, agitated in the 1790s and again after the war, denied by the long Tory ascendancy, and at last carried by Earl Grey’s ministry in 1832 in the teeth of a national crisis. The Reform Act of 1832 settled the borough franchise on the £10 householder, opened seats to the industrial towns, and left the working classes — as Chartism was to insist — still outside the pale of representation.

The unreformed constitution

The House of Commons that entered the nineteenth century was the creation of an older England. The distribution of seats had been fixed in an age when the South, the agricultural counties, and the old corporate towns had carried the weight of population and wealth; by 1830 the industrial revolution had shifted population and commerce to the North and the Midlands, yet the representation had not followed. Nomination boroughs — seats owned outright by a patron — survived almost untouched: Old Sarum, a grassy mound with no inhabitants, still returned two members; Gatton was the standing example of a “pocket borough” bought and sold with the soil. Meanwhile Manchester, Birmingham, Leeds, and the other great manufacturing towns sent no member at all. The franchise itself was an incoherent patchwork of ancient customs — freemen, burgage-holders, potwallopers, scot-and-lot payers, £10 householders in some boroughs and resident householders in others — with no principle of uniformity behind it. Molesworth’s opening survey traced the long history of the question, from the Long Parliament and Cromwell through the elder and younger Pitt’s abortive schemes and the repressive legislation of the 1790s, and closed with the distress of 1829–30 that made reform a national demand.

The long denial and the prelude to crisis

Reform had been demanded and denied for a generation. The French Revolution had discredited it in the eyes of the governing classes and brought on the repression of the 1790s and the Six Acts after the war. The long Tory ascendancy under Liverpool had resisted every motion; the liberal Tories — Huskisson, Canning, and their school — had reformed commerce and administration but left the constitution untouched. Catholic Emancipation, wrung from Wellington’s ministry in 1829, broke the old alliance against change: it proved that the constitution could be altered when the crisis was sharp enough, and it alienated from the government a section of its own supporters. The death of George IV in June 1830 and the accession of William IV brought a general election; the July Revolution in France frightened the timid and encouraged the reformers; and agricultural distress, rick-burning, and the machine-breaking of the “Captain Swing” riots spread alarm through the southern counties. When Wellington declared in November 1830 that the existing system possessed the full and entire confidence of the country, and that he was not prepared to bring forward any measure of reform, his ministry fell, and Earl Grey — who had advocated reform since the 1790s — was summoned to form a government.

The Reform Bill crisis, 1830–32

Grey made reform a Cabinet question, and on 1 March 1831 Lord John Russell rose to introduce the first Reform Bill. The details of the scheme — the disfranchisement of the rotten boroughs, the enfranchisement of the new towns, the £10 householder franchise in the boroughs — were debated through a crisis that brought the country to the verge of convulsion. The Bill passed its second reading by a single vote, 302 to 301, on the night the strangers’ gallery was crowded with the people; it was then defeated in committee on General Gascoigne’s wrecking amendment. The ministry dissolved Parliament, and the general election of April–May 1831 returned a great reform majority. The second Bill passed the Commons by large majorities, only to be rejected by the Lords in October 1831 by a majority of forty-one, the bishops voting almost solidly against. Popular fury followed: riots at Derby and Nottingham, the burning of Nottingham Castle, the destruction of the Mansion House and the Bishop’s Palace in the Bristol riots, the breaking of the windows of anti-reform peers. The Political Unions — disciplined reform organisations that had spread from Thomas Attwood’s Birmingham Union across the country — threatened to march on London.

The crisis came to a head in the “Days of May” 1832. The third Reform Bill was introduced; the Lords, under Lord Lyndhurst’s lead, carried a motion to postpone the disfranchising clauses. The Grey ministry resigned, and the King sent for the Duke of Wellington — who failed to form a government, unwilling to take office on a reform basis and unable to command the support even of his own friends. Grey was recalled with the King’s reluctant promise to create enough peers to carry the Bill. The threat of the new creation was enough: the Tory peers abstained in great numbers, the Bill passed the Lords, and the Reform Act received the royal assent on 7 June 1832. Molesworth noted the mass abstention of the peers and the decisive force of the threatened creation; the “leap” had been made, and the constitution was remade.

The Reform Act of 1832

The Act disfranchised fifty-six rotten boroughs and reduced thirty more to a single member; it enfranchised forty-two new towns and extended the county representation; and it established the £10 householder franchise in the boroughs — the occupier of a house rated at £10 a year became an elector. The result was a wholesale redrawing of the electoral map of Britain and a decisive shift of power from the landed and closed boroughs to the middle classes of the towns. The first Reformed Parliament assembled in January 1833 with the reformers triumphant — the Conservative party (as the Tories now called themselves) reduced to about 150 members. Martineau’s account of the passage stresses both the revolutionary danger narrowly averted and the moderate character of the settlement: the Act enfranchised the middle-class householder and the substantial tenant, but left the town workman and the agricultural labourer without a vote. The agitation that had carried the Bill had been in part a middle- and working-class alliance; the settlement rewarded the one and disappointed the other.

The consolidation of reform

The years that followed consolidated the change. The first Reformed Parliament, against the background of the cholera epidemic and the financial difficulties of the government, carried the great measures of the reform era: the abolition of slavery in the British colonies (1833, with the £20,000,000 compensation to the owners), the Factory Act of 1833 limiting the hours of children, the New Poor Law Amendment Act of 1834, the renewal of the Bank of England and East India Company charters, and the Municipal Corporations Act of 1835, which swept away the corrupt self-perpetuating oligarchies of the old borough corporations and established elected town councils for 178 boroughs. The parties reshaped themselves — Tories as “Conservatives,” Whigs as “Reformers,” with a distinct Radical or “Destructive” section — and Peel, in the Tamworth Manifesto of 1834, accepted the Reform Act as final and promised moderate reform. The old order of the Pittites was literally dying off, as Martineau’s great necrology of the volume observed, and a generation of reformers was taking its place.

The unfinished settlement

The Reform Act was not the end of the question. The working classes, excluded from the £10 franchise, drew from the settlement the lesson of Chartism: that the Reform Act had transferred power from the landed aristocracy to the middle class and left the workman still unrepresented. The People’s Charter of 1838 demanded universal male suffrage, equal electoral districts, the ballot, annual parliaments, the abolition of the property qualification, and payment of members. The Chartist movement, with its monster meetings, its National Petition, the Newport rising of 1839, the Kennington Common meeting of 1848, and its eventual collapse, kept the demand alive through the 1830s and 1840s even as the reformed Parliament refused it. The £10 settlement of 1832 stood, in Martineau’s and Molesworth’s common telling, as the great constitutional turning-point of the age — the moment when the old constitution was broken and the long argument over who should vote, and how, entered its Victorian phase. Further widening — to the borough workman in 1867, the county labourer in 1884, and the women and mass electorate of 1918 — lay beyond the horizon of this period, but the Reform Act of 1832 set the direction of the whole.
