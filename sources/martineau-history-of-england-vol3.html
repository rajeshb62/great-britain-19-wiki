Martineau — History of England, Vol. III: From Canning to the Consolidation of Reform (1827–1836)

Harriet Martineau (1802–1876) — History of England from the Commencement of the XIXth Century to the Crimean War. Vol. III. — 1864 Edition.

The third volume covers the turbulent decade from the collapse of the Liverpool ministry to the consolidation of the reform settlement. Martineau narrates Canning’s brief premiership, the Duke of Wellington’s ministry, Catholic Emancipation, the French Revolution of 1830, the Reform Bill crisis of 1830–32, the first Reformed Parliament, the abolition of slavery, the New Poor Law, municipal reform, Lord Melbourne’s administration, and the dismantling of the Orange societies. The volume presents a philosophic Radical perspective on a period that saw the death of the old Tory order and the birth of the reform era.

The text is 30,461 lines. Note: Book IV has no Chapter XI — the chapter numbering jumps from Chapter X (line 20540) to Chapter XII (line 22293). This appears to be an original feature of the text, not an OCR omission.

Content Summary

BOOK III — From the Close of Lord Liverpool’s Administration to the French Revolution of 1830 (1827–1830)

  • Chapter I — Opening of Parliament and Death of the Duke of York; Canning becomes Premier — Martineau reflects on 1826–1829 as years of apparent legislative stagnation masking deep political change. The King opens Parliament with Canning’s speech on Portugal. The Duke of York dies in January 1827 — Martineau provides a detailed character assessment and describes his funeral. Lord Liverpool suffers a stroke and resigns. A Corn Bill debate ensues with Canning proposing resolutions and Wellington opposing. The King summons Canning to form a ministry on March 27, 1827. Peel resigns over Catholic relief, Lord Eldon departs. The formation of Canning’s administration amid great political uncertainty — Huskisson, Lord Lansdowne, and Lord Carlisle join

  • Chapter II — Canning’s Administration, His Death, Goderich, Wellington — Parliament reassembles May 1, 1827. The session is dominated by vicious personal attacks on Canning by the Tories (Eldon’s friends). The Whigs offer support; Lord Grey delivers a notable speech. Canning’s declining health under the strain of office, his death in August 1827. Lord Goderich briefly heads the ministry but proves incapable — “the incapacity of Lord Goderich was the greatest that could exist in a man of honour.” The Duke of Wellington becomes Prime Minister in January 1828, ushering in a new political alignment. The formation of Wellington’s Cabinet with Peel at the Home Office

  • Chapter III — Wellington, Ireland, Catholic Relief Bill 1829 — The Battle of Navarino (October 1827) — the allied destruction of the Turkish-Egyptian fleet at Navarino Bay, a key event in Greek independence. Lord Anglesey as Lord Lieutenant of Ireland. Wellington’s administration and the growing crisis over Catholic emancipation in Ireland — the Clare election (Daniel O’Connell elected in defiance of the law), Peel’s conversion to the cause of Catholic relief. The passage of the Catholic Relief Act of 1829: Catholics admitted to Parliament and most civil offices, the suppression of the Catholic Association. The Protestant backlash and the Brunswick clubs. The Duke of Wellington deserted by half his friends

  • Chapter IV — Wellington’s Last Years, the Birmingham Political Union, Death of George IV — Wellington’s final years in power. Rising economic distress and popular agitation for reform. Thomas Attwood founds the Birmingham Political Union in January 1830 — a new phase of extra-parliamentary organisation coordinating the middle and working classes. The agricultural interest and the currency debates. The King’s health deteriorates; George IV dies in June 1830 after a reign of ten years. The coronation of William IV. This triggers a general election. Wellington’s stubborn opposition to reform alienates moderate Tories

  • Chapter V — Social and Political Conditions — Economic distress gripping the country: agricultural depression, trade union activity, the Swing Riots (rural machine-breaking and rick-burning in southern England — 387 Swing prosecutions in Berkshire, Surrey, Kent, Sussex, and Hampshire). The anxieties of landowners and manufacturers. The collapse of Wellington’s ministry in November 1830 after his famous anti-reform speech (“I am not only not prepared to bring forward any measure of this nature, but I will at once declare that… the existing system possesses the full and entire confidence of the country”). Lord Grey is summoned to form his ministry

  • Chapter VI — Law of the Press and Reform Agitation — Press prosecutions and the campaign for a freer press: notable libel cases, the growing influence of the Times and other newspapers. The efforts of reformers to secure a freer press and the government’s countervailing efforts. The aftermath of Catholic emancipation, the continuing political ferment in Ireland, Lord Anglesey’s role as Lord Lieutenant

  • Chapter VII — Foreign Affairs: Russia, Turkey, and Greece — Foreign policy: the Russo-Turkish War (1828–1829), the London Conference on Greek independence, the establishment of the Greek state under the London Protocol, the Treaty of Adrianople (1829), the diplomatic maneuvering between Britain, Russia, France, and the Ottoman Empire. Lord Anglesey’s administration in Ireland and intensifying Irish grievances

  • Chapter VIII — The Collapse of the Bourbon Monarchy in France — The final years of Charles X’s reign: the resignation of the Villelle ministry, the formation of the Polignac ministry (the “Prince of the Jesuits”), the summoning of the Chambers, the growing opposition to Polignac’s ultra-royalist policies, the dissolution of the Chamber of Deputies, and the July Ordinances (the ordonnances de Saint-Cloud) that triggered the revolution

  • Chapter IX — The French Revolution of 1830 — The Trois Glorieuses — the Three Days of July (27–29 July 1830) in Paris: the barricades, the defection of the troops, the flight of Charles X, the provisional government. Louis Philippe, Duke of Orleans, accepting the throne as “King of the French” — the revision of the Charter, the abolition of the hereditary peerage, the new electoral law. The impact of the French Revolution on British politics — intensifying the demand for reform, frightening conservatives

  • Chapter X — Domestic Life and Industrial Unrest — Working-class conditions, notable crimes (Burke and Hare murders — 16 murders for anatomical dissection, Hare turning King’s evidence, the Anatomy Act 1832 subsequently passed). The new General Post Office and postal reform. The spread of Political Unions from Birmingham across the country. The condition of the poor, early Factory Act agitation, the intellectual ferment of the Society for the Diffusion of Useful Knowledge (penny magazines, The Penny Magazine, The Library of Useful Knowledge)

  • Chapter XI — Necrology and the Death of the Old Order — The longest chapter in the volume, a book-length necrology. Martineau catalogues “Political Deaths” (George IV, Canning, Huskisson, Tierney), “Men of Business,” “Artists” (Sir Thomas Lawrence), “Men of Letters,” “Philosophers” (Sir Humphry Davy), and “Philanthropists.” Extensive biographical sketches arguing that the old order of Pittites and anti-reformers was literally dying off, making way for a new generation of reformers. The chapter serves as Martineau’s pivot between the age of reaction and the age of reform

BOOK IV — The Reform Era (1830–1834/35)

  • Chapter I — The Accession of William IV and the July Revolution — William IV’s accession in June 1830, the Regency question (what if the King died before Princess Victoria came of age). The new King’s popular manners, the dissolution of Parliament, the general election of 1830. The Liverpool and Manchester Railway opening and Huskisson’s death (September 15, 1830 — struck down by Rocket at Parkside). Intensifying O’Connell agitation in Ireland. Sympathy in Britain for the French Revolution of 1830. Agricultural distress, rick-burning. The fall of Wellington’s ministry and Lord Grey being called to form a government

  • Chapter II — Lord Grey and the Reform Bill — The formation of the Grey Ministry in November 1830 with Reform of Parliament made a Cabinet question. Retrospective on Grey’s long advocacy of parliamentary reform since the 1790s. The composition of the Cabinet: Brougham Lord Chancellor (his “singular character,” possessing more influence than any other single member of the ministry), Lord Althorp Chancellor of the Exchequer (acting leader of the Commons), Lord John Russell Paymaster of the Forces (the sharp-tempered, fearless advocate of reform). The King’s initial support. The presentation of the first Reform Bill by Lord John Russell on March 1, 1831

  • Chapter III — The Reform Bill Crisis — 1831 opens with machine-breaking, rick-burning. A Manchester manufacturer murdered by trade unionists. The prospect of civil conflict between anti-reformers and the discontented lower classes. The middle classes organise through Political Unions. The first Reform Bill passes its second reading by a single vote (302–301) on March 22, 1831. Defeated in committee on General Gascoigne’s motion. Parliament prorogued and dissolved, the general election of April–May 1831 producing a massive pro-reform majority. The second Reform Bill passes the Commons with large majorities but is rejected by the Lords (41 majority against) in October 1831. Riots at Derby and Nottingham — the burning of Nottingham Castle. The crisis of May 1832: ministers resign after the Lords refuse to yield, the King attempts to get Wellington to form a government (fails), Lord Grey recalled with the King’s reluctant promise to create enough peers. The Reform Act receives royal assent on June 7, 1832

  • Chapter IV — The Reformed Parliament — The general election of 1831 in detail — the role of non-electors in pressuring voters. The emergence of Political Unions as disciplined reform organisations. The “Waverers” and the threat of creating peers. The King’s appeals to the Lords to yield. The Reform Act’s provisions: disenfranchisement of 56 rotten boroughs, 30 reduced to one MP, enfranchisement of 42 new towns, county representation extended, £10 householder franchise in boroughs. The new constituency map of Britain. The first Reformed Parliament assembles (January 1833)

  • Chapter V — The New Poor Law and Other Reforms — After the Reform Act’s passage, Martineau turns to the great social questions. The cholera epidemic of 1832 (from Sunderland through the country — panic, the spread of cholera as a social and political event). The New Poor Law Amendment Act of 1834: the principles (the workhouse test, less eligibility, central Poor Law Commission), its machinery, its reception and opposition. The 1833 Factory Act limiting hours for children. The Bank Charter renewal (1833). The India Company’s Charter renewal (1833 — the Company’s trading monopoly ended, India opened to free trade). The Slavery Abolition Act (1833, effective August 1, 1834) — £20 million compensation to slave-owners

  • Chapter VI — Irish Church and Tithe Reform — The Irish Church question paramount in the post-Reform Parliament. Statistics: an Established Church of 853,000 members with four archbishops and eighteen bishops, supported by tithes from a population of 6.5 million Catholics. The Tithe War — Irish Catholics refusing to pay tithes, seizures of property by armed police and soldiers. The Coercion Bill (1833). The Irish Church Temporalities Bill (reducing bishoprics from 18 to 12, creating the Ecclesiastical Commission). The prosecution of O’Connell. The Appropriation controversy — whether surplus Church revenues should be applied to secular purposes (especially education)

  • Chapter VII — Municipal Reform — The reform of the Civil List under William IV: the King surrendering hereditary revenues for a fixed parliamentary grant. The reform of the pension list. The Municipal Corporations Commission (appointed 1833). The history of English municipal institutions. The Commission’s report exposing widespread corruption and self-perpetuating oligarchies in the old borough corporations. The Municipal Corporations Act (1835) — elected town councils established for 178 boroughs

  • Chapter VIII — Ecclesiastical Affairs and the Great Charters — The Bank of England Charter renewed (1833) — the secret parliamentary committee on banking, the recommendation to limit paper money to a single London issuing bank and require periodic publication of accounts. The East India Company Charter renewed (1833) — the Company’s trading monopoly abolished, India opened to free trade and Christian missionaries. The Ecclesiastical Commission (1835), the Non-residence Act (abolishing absentee clergy), the abolition of sinecures, the preliminaries of the Tithe Commutation Act

  • Chapter IX — Irish Church and Affairs of France — Criticism of the Whig Ministry’s failures in Ireland: “Catholic emancipation would not tranquillize Ireland while she suffered under the burden of ‘too much Church.‘” The tithe collection system backed by armed police, the 1832 Tithe Act, the proposed 1834 Act (lost), the Irish Ecclesiastical Commission, the Irish census, reductions in bishoprics, the Appropriation controversy. Affairs of France under the July Monarchy: Louis Philippe’s acceptance of the crown, the revision of the Charter, abolition of the hereditary peerage, the electoral law, the characteristics of the July Monarchy. The Belgian Revolution (separation from Holland, Prince Leopold becoming King of the Belgians). Disturbances across Europe: Brunswick, Saxony, Hesse, Baden, Switzerland, Italy. Spain (death of Ferdinand VII, the Carlist claim). Portugal (death of Don Pedro, the Queen’s marriage). Egypt and Turkey (Mehemet Ali). The Polish Revolution of 1830–31 (the revolt, the struggle, the defeat)

  • Chapter X — Religious Life and Controversy — A comprehensive survey of Britain’s religious crisis. Three parties in the Church of England: the Evangelical Party, the High Church/Tractarian movement (just beginning with the Tracts for the Times, 1833–41), and the Moderate Church Party. The death of William Wilberforce (1833) and Hannah More (1833). The opening of the universities to Dissenters (debated 1834–35). The admission of Quakers to Parliament (Mr. Pease, 1833). The continued exclusion of Jews from Parliament. Deaths of prominent religious figures: Robert Hall (the great Baptist preacher), Rowland Hill, Charles Wesley, Adam Clarke, Rammohun Roy (the Hindu reformer), Dr. Doyle (the Catholic Archbishop of Dublin). The schism in the Scotch Church (the beginnings of the Disruption leading to the Free Church in 1843). Edward Irving and the Catholic Apostolic Church. St. Simonism and the spread of socialist ideas. Budgets from 1831–34: the assessed taxes movement, the house-tax, the malt-tax, the surplus of 1834, the corn-laws debated. Poor Law for Ireland. The ballot question. Military flogging and the impressment of seamen

  • Chapter XII — The Progress of the Country and the Fall of the Grey Ministry — Popular discontents and the power of Trades’ Unions in 1834: the murder of a Manchester manufacturer, the tyranny of union leaders, the new combination system where trades supported each other’s strikes, the collapse of these efforts. The Dorsetshire labourers (the Tolpuddle Martyrs — transported for administering unlawful oaths under the Unlawful Oaths Act of 1797). The disintegration of the Grey Ministry: Lord Althorp’s resignation (over the Irish Tithe question raised by Mr. Ward’s motion), Lord Grey’s resignation in July 1834, Lord Brougham’s removal from the Chancellorship (Lord Lyndhurst succeeds), Lord Durham’s discontent, the Grey Banquet. Lord Melbourne’s first short-lived ministry

  • Chapter XIII — Affairs of France (continued) — A detailed study of the July Monarchy from 1830–1834. Louis Philippe’s personal history: his barefoot exile across the Alps, teaching mathematics in Switzerland, living in Philadelphia, his modest courtship. His acceptance of the crown, the revision of the Charter (especially the controversial Article 14 allowing emergency royal decrees). Disquiet in France, the suicide of the Duke de Bourbon (the last of the Condés). Disturbances in Paris, the abolition of the hereditary peerage, the new electoral law, the emergence of parties (Legitimists, Orleanists, Republicans). Press prosecutions. Insurrections: the Lyons silk-weavers’ uprising, the Paris rising of 1832 (the funeral of General Lamarque). The fortifications of Paris. The death of Lafayette (1834). The characteristics of the reign: Louis Philippe’s bourgeois monarchy, the consolidation of the new order

  • Chapter XIV — The Accession of William IV and the Progress of the Country — William IV’s genuine popularity: the King walking in the streets, visiting the theatre, greeted with “Reform” and “Glorious King” placards. The coronation on September 8, 1831 — quiet and economical (no banquet). Princess Victoria (aged 12) beginning tours of the country. An assault on the King. Popular ignorance and riots. The Anatomy Bill (1832, allowing doctors to dissect unclaimed bodies). Medical education reform. Notable criminal trials. “The Progress of the Country”: an eclectic catalogue covering the introduction of steam navigation in the East, mail conveyance improvements, the Drummond Light (limelight for surveying), polar discoveries, the Islington Cattle Market, the rebuilding of London Bridge (opened August 1, 1831), education reform, the British Association for the Advancement of Science, statistics of suicide, duelling, the loss of the steamship Rothesay Castle, the fire at the Dublin Custom House, the burning of the Houses of Parliament (October 16, 1834 — the two Houses of Parliament completely destroyed). A substantial necrology of the period’s deaths: political figures, men of science (Sir Humphry Davy covered more fully here), seamen, travellers, actors, musicians, architects, antiquarians, artists, authors, and philanthropists

BOOK V — The Consolidation of Reform (1834–1836)

  • Chapter I — The Three Parties — After the Reform Act, the parties adopt new titles: Tories become “Conservatives,” Whigs become “Reformers,” Radicals become “Radical Reformers” (or “Destructives” by opponents). A sophisticated analysis of party dynamics: the representatives of those with much to lose, those with much to gain, and the “umpire party” that interposes in crises. The Duke of Wellington’s continuing role though out of office. Sir Robert Peel’s position. The formation of Peel’s first ministry (December 1834), the dissolution of Parliament. Peel’s Tamworth Manifesto (December 1834) — accepting the Reform Act as final and promising moderate reform. The new Parliament assembles, Mr. Abercromby elected Speaker. Peel’s minority government weak from the start

  • Chapter II — Lord Melbourne’s Ministry — Peel’s “Hundred Days” ministry (December 1834–April 1835): the King’s Speech, angry debates, the malt-tax debate, Lord Londonderry’s diplomatic appointment controversy, ministers defeated on ecclesiastical questions. The Dissenters’ Marriages Bill, the Ecclesiastical Commission, the London University Charter, the decisive struggle over the Appropriation question. Peel’s government defeated and resigns in April 1835. Lord Melbourne forms his second ministry: Lord John Russell Home Secretary, Mr. Charles Grant Colonial Office. The new government’s Irish administration and its reform agenda

  • Chapter III — Irish Church and Municipal Reform — The two great questions of the Melbourne ministry. First, the Irish Church: the Appropriation question — whether to appropriate surplus Church revenues to non-religious purposes — Church-rates, the eventual surrender of the Appropriation principle by the ministry. Second, Municipal Reform: the Corporation Commission, a detailed history of English municipal institutions, the Commissioners’ report exposing the corrupt self-perpetuating nature of old borough corporations (closed governing bodies, misuse of funds, no accountability), the principle of the case, the passage of the Municipal Corporations Act (1835) after bitter Lords opposition

  • Chapter IV — Ecclesiastical Commission and Tithe Commutation — The Ecclesiastical Commission (established 1835): its composition, its work in redistributing Church revenues, the Non-residence Act (clergy required to live in their parishes), the abolition of sinecures. The Tithe Commutation Act (1836) — commuting tithes in kind into a money rent-charge. Popular education: Lord Brougham’s education scheme and the ministerial scheme of 1834. Attacks on the House of Lords and the growing demand for Peerage Reform. O’Connell’s tour of Scotland and England (1835). The emergence of Chartism: its various factions (Radical, Tory, Hungering, Factious Chartists), the growing working-class disenchantment with the results of the Reform Act

  • Chapter V — The Dissolution of the Orange Societies — The final chapter, a detailed investigation of Orangeism in Britain. The Orange Lodges: secret Protestant societies headed by the Duke of Cumberland (the King’s brother and Grand Master), Colonel Fairman as the key organiser. The extent of Orange infiltration into the army, the Church, and the peerage. The lodges organising political intimidation against Catholics and reformers. A suspected plot involving a military coup or replacement of Melbourne with Wellington. Detection through parliamentary inquiry: Mr. Hume’s resolutions leading to a Committee of Inquiry exposing the scale of the conspiracy. The Duke of Cumberland compelled to resign his grand mastership. Colonel Fairman flees or refuses to testify. The Orange Society dissolved, addresses to the King adopted. The book closes with the end of organised Orangeism in England and the consolidation of the reform settlement
