An Introduction to the History of the Church of England

Henry Offley Wakeman (Fellow of All Souls College, Tutor of Keble College, Oxford) offers a narrative interpretation of the development of the Church of England rather than a mere chronicle. His stated aim is to answer the question “How is it that the Church of England has come to be what she is?” by fixing attention on what has proved permanent and passing quickly over periods of interruption or deterioration.

The book is written from a High Church/Anglo-Catholic perspective, reflecting the Oxford Movement’s conception of the Church of England as a branch of the Catholic Church, reformed of medieval abuses but in continuous historical succession with the primitive and medieval Church.

Chronological Table (55 BC–AD 1896)

A detailed timeline from Caesar’s first invasion of Britain through the planting of the Church in Roman Britain, the conversion of the English, the Danish invasions, the Norman Conquest, the Reformation, the Civil War, the Restoration, the Evangelical Revival, the Oxford Movement, and the ritual controversies of the late nineteenth century.

Chapter I — The Church in Britain, A.D. 200–597

Wakeman opens by acknowledging that no one knows precisely when Christianity first reached Britain, though passages in Tertullian and Origen, written between 208 and 240, make it probable that a church existed in the Roman province by the early third century. It may have arrived with Roman soldiers building the roads and camps that still inspire wonder, or more likely in the wake of wealthy Gallic civilians who built villas at Silchester and Verulam, bathed at Bath, or dined on oysters at Chichester and Canterbury. Whatever its origin, it left few traces — enough, however, to sketch a “fairly vivid picture” of an infant Church on the extreme edge of Roman civilisation.

This was a poor and struggling institution, “mainly of the poorer Roman provincials,” deriving its existence, ritual, and orders from its richer neighbour, the Church of Gaul. Over two centuries under the Roman eagles, it produced no great man, built no great building, endured no serious persecution, sent out no missionaries, and was obliged to appeal to Gaul for help in internal difficulties. At the Council of Ariminum in 359, the British bishops were the only ones who accepted the emperor’s expense allowance — a telling measure of their poverty. They could not even overcome the Pelagian heresy by themselves but had to summon Germanus of Auxerre and Lupus of Troyes from Gaul. Nevertheless, it was a duly organised branch of the Catholic Church, possessing bishops at London and York (and probably Lincoln), and sending representatives to the great councils of Arles (314), Ariminum (359), Nicaea (325), and Sardica (347). The proto-martyr S. Alban sealed his faith with his blood in 304 during the Diocletian persecution, though Wakeman wryly notes his martyrdom owed more to “his interference with the course of Roman justice than to his religious opinions.”

Wakeman then turns to the legends that poetry and later ages wove around these scanty facts — S. Paul as apostle of the Britons, Bran the Blessed bringing Christianity from Rome, and above all the beautiful medieval romance of Joseph of Arimathea at Glastonbury, bearing the Holy Grail and planting a staff that burst into thorny blossom each Christmas. These legends, however touching, have “no foundation in fact.” Genuine religious revival came only with the closing years of the fourth century: S. Ninian’s mission in 397 and his stone church at Whithern in Galloway; the anti-Pelagian mission of Germanus and Lupus; and S. Patrick’s labours in Ireland. A new Church grew up in the fifth and sixth centuries, “loose and ill-jointed in organisation and government” but “instinct with personal holiness, inspired by missionary zeal, and imbued with a passion for learning.”

This was the Church that faced the Saxon and English heathen pirates from 449 onward. The Celtic Church could not rise to the task of converting its conquerors; it shrank back into the fastnesses of Welsh and Cornish hills, and over five-sixths of England the worship of Christ gave way to Woden, Freia, and Thor — a fact still stamped on the days of the English week. Only in the unconquered Celtic west and across the water in Ireland did the faith survive, while at Iona the great missionary college of Columba was already founded. The chapter closes with a turning point: the marriage of the Kentish king Æthelbert to Bertha, daughter of the Christian king of Paris, who brought with her the Frankish bishop Luidhard and restored the ancient church of S. Martin at Canterbury. Their presence paved the way, but the initiative for conversion came not from Gaul but from “distant Rome under the humble cowl of a monk.”

Chapter II — The Conversion of the English, A.D. 597–655

The story begins with a famous encounter: in 585, Gregory, abbot of a monastery on the Caelian Hill, saw Northumbrian slave-boys in the Roman market — their “white skins, blue eyes, and flaxen hair” so moved him that he sought permission to missionise the heathen English. His fellow-citizens refused to let him go. Years later, as pope, he could command rather than ask. He chose Augustine, prior of his own monastery, to lead the mission. In 596 the little band set out, but at Aix in Provence they were struck with “sluggish fear” and sent Augustine back. Gregory would not hear of retreat; arming Augustine with abbatial authority and smoothing his path with letters, he drove them forward. In the spring of 597, Augustine landed at Ebbsfleet in Thanet — the very spot where Hengest’s Jutes had first landed 150 years earlier on an errand of “conquest and rapine.”

The meeting with King Æthelbert was staged with memorable theatre: the king sat in the open air under a tree, Teutonic fashion, while a procession of forty men approached chanting litanies, bearing a silver cross and a painted image of Christ Crucified. Augustine, towering over his companions, set forth in simple words “how the tender-hearted Jesus by His own throes had redeemed the sinful world.” The king, a statesman already familiar with Christianity through his wife Bertha, promised protection but refused to convert without more thought. Converts came nonetheless, and by summer Æthelbert himself offered for baptism. Augustine was consecrated archbishop of the English at Arles on 16 November 597, and with that the infant Church of England began.

He established his cathedral in an old Roman church at Canterbury, rebuilding it on the model of S. Peter’s on the Vatican — Christ Church, still the mother church of English Christianity. Outside the walls rose the beginnings of what would become the rich monastery of S. Augustine. Gregory sent reinforcements in 601 — Mellitus, Justus, and Paulinus — along with a pall, that “narrow strip of white woollen material” whose symbolic meaning later tangled English churchmen in questions of papal authority. Gregory’s grand scheme for two archiepiscopal provinces at London and York, each with twelve suffragans, was never realised; stubborn paganism kept the southern see at Canterbury, and the northern province took centuries to gain real weight.

The great missed opportunity was the union with the Celtic Church. Augustine met the Welsh bishops under an oak in 603, demanding they accept Roman customs — the date of Easter, the tonsure, the rite of baptism. The Celts, whose Church had developed wholly apart from Rome and was sending missionaries from Ireland to Burgundy and Switzerland, would not surrender their ancient customs. The conference broke up “amid hot recriminations,” and both sides suffered. Augustine died in 604, having founded the sees of London and Rochester but little more. His work, though narrow, was solid: “nothing that Augustine did had to be undone.”

After his death came crisis. The pagan reaction under Eadbald (who relapsed into heathenism on Æthelbert’s death) was short-lived, and in 625 Paulinus was consecrated missionary bishop for Northumbria, accompanying the princess Æthelburga to her marriage with King Eadwine. The great gemot at Godmundingham in 627 is one of English history’s most famous scenes: the cynicism of Coifi the high priest, the thegnly parable of the sparrow flying through the lighted hall from darkness to darkness, and Eadwine’s baptism on Easter eve. For six years Paulinus evangelised on foot, a gaunt stooping figure with piercing eyes and long raven hair, baptising by the hundred.

Then came Penda of Mercia, the heathen champion who defeated and killed Eadwine at Heathfield in 633. Paulinus fled to Kent, and for a moment Christianity in the north seemed doomed. But Oswald, a nephew of Eadwine educated at Iona, defeated the Celtic king Cadwallon at Heaven-field in 635 and summoned a new bishop from the Scottish monastery. The first monk sent returned discouraged, but a voice cried out from the brethren: “Was it their stubbornness or your severity? Did you forget the apostle’s command to feed them first with milk and then with meat?” That speaker was Aidan, and he was at once acclaimed bishop.

Wakeman draws a masterful contrast: Roman Christianity was the civitas Dei — law, order, territorial organisation — capable of governing but inclined to confine. Irish Christianity was ascetic, personal, monastic, capable of winning souls but not of building institutions. The combination of both was needed. Aidan himself is drawn with evident love — a “second Francis of Assisi,” embracing holy poverty, travelling on foot, founding Lindisfarne as a second Iona, training twelve picked boys, and working in perfect harmony with the king-saint Oswald. Aidan died in 651, his heart broken by the murder of his friend Oswin. Four years later, at the Battle of Winwaed (655), Oswiu defeated and killed Penda. “The Church was everywhere victorious. At once she began to occupy the vacant country.”

Chapter III — The Organisation of the Early English Church, A.D. 655–735

With heathenism defeated, the Church faced a new challenge: unity and organisation. Christianity in England was a patchwork — Roman (Kent, Wessex), Irish (Northumbria, Mercia, the East Saxons), mixed (East Anglia), and purely Celtic (Cornwall, Wales, Strathclyde). Over five-sixths of Christian Britain, the authority of Rome was not acknowledged. Wakeman argues plainly: if the English Church was to be a great national force, it must be united and organised, and this could only happen “by subordinating Celtic to Roman Christianity.”

The decisive event was the Synod of Whitby in 664, summoned by King Oswiu. The immediate issue was the date of Easter. Colman of Lindisfarne argued from the tradition of S. John handed down through Columba. Wilfrid, young, brilliant, and trained at Rome, answered with “convincing logic” and then “fiery invective” — how dare “a few obstinate inhabitants of parts of two remote isles” set themselves against the whole civilised world and the authority of the blessed chief of the apostles? Oswiu clinched the matter with a jest: he would obey the rulings of that doorkeeper Peter, lest he find no one to unbar the gates of heaven. Behind the theology lay real questions — “isolation or unity, law or chaos, culture or ignorance, progress or backwardness.” Whitby meant that “the Church of England had determined to take her part” in the conquest of the barbarian world.

But unity of observance was not enough without unity of administration. That was the work of Theodore of Tarsus, appointed archbishop of Canterbury by Pope Vitalian in 668. Theodore was “a man of orderly mind, strong will, and untiring energy.” His first metropolitical visitation in 669 set the Church in order — filling sees, correcting irregularities, winning hearts (even the meek Chad submitted to re-consecration and went to Lichfield). In 673 he summoned the first provincial council of the Church of England at Hertford — “the precursor of the present Convocations” — passing canons on Easter, episcopal authority, clerical discipline, and monastic stability.

The great controversy came over Theodore’s plan to divide the impossibly large Northumbrian diocese. Wilfrid, bishop of York, who ruled from Humber to Forth, would not consent, so Theodore simply divided the diocese anyway. Wilfrid’s response was momentous: he appealed to the pope. Wakeman notes that Wilfrid did not intend to assert papal supremacy in the later medieval sense; he was simply a wronged man seeking justice from the highest tribunal he knew. Theodore ignored the papal decision and continued his work, dividing the Mercian see into Worcester and Leicester. Wilfrid returned in 680 with a papal bull — and was banished by king and Witan who would not submit to foreign dictation. He spent six fruitful years converting the South Saxons, the last English tribe to be gathered into the fold.

The reconciliation came in 685, and Wakeman gives a tender portrait of S. Cuthbert — shepherd boy, monk of Melrose, prior of Lindisfarne, hermit of Farne, and finally bishop-elect dragged weeping from his cell by king and clergy on their knees. Cuthbert’s two years of episcopal duty were followed by a hermit’s death on Farne in 687. Theodore died in 690, his work done: fourteen suffragan bishops acknowledged Canterbury’s rule. Wilfrid, after a second banishment and a second appeal to Rome, was finally restored to Hexham and Ripon at the Council on the Nidd in 706. He died in 709.

Wakeman concludes that the organisation of the Church is due mainly to these two men — and “as much to the disagreements which arose between them as to the individual capacity and energy of each.” Wilfrid united England to the western Church and won the last heathen strongholds; Theodore introduced law, discipline, and territorial episcopacy. The chapter ends with three parallel lines of growth: structural (the episcopal system), educational (schools at Canterbury under Hadrian and at York), and parochial/monastic (the parson’s charge and the religious house). The monasteries of Wilfrid at Ripon, Benedict Biscop at Wearmouth and Jarrow, Hilda at Whitby, and Aldhelm at Malmesbury were centres not of seclusion but of active learning — homes of Caedmon, first English poet, and of Bede, first English historian. Missionaries like Willibrord and Boniface carried English Christianity to Frisia and Germany. The chapter closes with the death of the Venerable Bede in 735 — dictating the last sentence of S. John’s Gospel to a weeping monk, then turning his face to his prayer-desk and breathing forth the Gloria Patri as his pure soul passed into the presence of God.

Chapter IV — The English Church from the Death of Bede to the Coming of the Normans, A.D. 735–1042

Wakeman opens this chapter with a frank diagnosis of decline. The first 150 years after Augustine were “the best and most interesting” of the pre-Conquest period. The Old English race was at its best in a great crisis — “it could win its country from the Celt, conquer the conquering Dane, defend its chosen king against foreign attack.” The Church could win kingdoms from heathenism, produce saints of every type, establish provinces and dioceses, schools and parishes, and weld Roman and Celtic Christianity into one fabric. But when the work of conquest was done, the work of consolidation proved harder. The “rough, coarse, passionate nature of our Old English forefathers began to reassert itself.” There was no serious heresy in England — but also “a singular lack of writings of all sorts, except chronicles” after Bede. The one intellectual of the period, Alcuin, left England early to serve Charles the Great. Gluttony, drunkenness, anger, sloth, and impurity were “the besetting sins” of the people.

Monasteries degenerated. Without a universal rule like that of S. Benedict, each house governed itself, and “gross abuses” crept in. The Council of Cloveshoo in 747 had to forbid monasteries from becoming “resorts of play-actors, musicians, and buffoons.” By Alfred’s accession in 871, he could not remember “one priest south of the Thames who could render his service-book into English.” The Church’s unity suffered too: the archbishopric of York was revived in 734; Offa of Mercia created a rival archbishopric at Lichfield in 787 (abolished at Cloveshoo in 803); the supremacy of Wessex meant political leadership lay where the Church was least efficient.

Then came the Northmen. From 794, when Jarrow and Lindisfarne fell, wave after wave of Danes ravaged England. Churches and monasteries were burned — Crowland, Peterborough, Ely — and King Eadmund of East Anglia was shot to death with arrows at Hoxall, becoming one of the Church’s most popular martyrs. By 871, the English were hemmed in the swamps of Athelney. From those morasses came “a hero and a saviour”: Alfred.

Wakeman’s portrait of Alfred is glowing — “the real founder of English national greatness,” “the pattern of Christian royalty,” a character of “more perfect balance” than any other in history. His religion “sanctified his statesmanship without narrowing it.” He translated Boethius, Gregory’s Pastoral, and Bede’s History; he imported scholars (Grimbald from St. Omer, John from Germany, Asser from Wales); he chose Plegmund, a hermit from Chester, as archbishop of Canterbury; he established a school for nobles’ sons at court. By the Peace of Wedmore (878), the Danes were turned from plunderers into settlers. “Superiority of religion and culture could not fail to tell.” By Æthelstan’s victory at Brunanburh in 937, all England was united under the descendants of Cerdic.

But the Danish invasions had done lasting damage: the great monasteries of the north were destroyed and their traditions “irretrievably lost”; the archbishop of York became isolated from Canterbury; bishops took up arms and neglected spiritual duties; the Church’s councils were no longer held; legislation passed through king and Witan rather than synod. The Church’s revival under Alfred raised the standard of learning and life, but the deeper problem remained — the lack of a unifying monastic discipline.

This was the work of the tenth-century reformers: Odo, Dunstan, Æthelwold, and Oswald. Odo of Canterbury had to go to Fleury in France to be professed as a Benedictine — there was not a single Benedictine house left in England. Dunstan, nephew of Archbishop Athelm, was “one of those rare natures on whom are lavished the choicest gifts of body and mind” — a true artist in metalwork, illumination, and music, combined with deep religious conviction. As abbot of Glastonbury (940), he introduced the Benedictine rule and made it “a sort of university on a small and rough scale.” The famous confrontation with King Eadwig (who was dragged back from a tryst to his coronation feast) led to Dunstan’s exile and then triumphant return under King Edgar. As archbishop of Canterbury (959–975), he was the virtual ruler of England — conciliating Danes and English, encouraging the Benedictine revival (forty new foundations in Edgar’s reign alone), and handling clerical marriage with “tender wisdom,” encouraging celibacy as a high ideal without imposing it as a universal law.

After Dunstan’s death in 988, the Danes returned under Swegen, and this time England met them with “cowardice” and “treason” — the “lurid figure” of Eadric Streona and the bribery of Archbishop Sigeric. The one hero was Ælfeah of Canterbury, who refused to use church treasures for his own ransom and was killed at a drunken feast at Greenwich in 1011, felled with ox-bones and battle-axes. Cnut, the Danish conqueror, proved a Christian king in the mould of Dunstan’s teaching — “one of the most striking instances in history of a man who learned to act on Christian principles when in power.” His letter of 1028, vowing “to lead a right life in all things,” is quoted at length as evidence of his sincerity. But his rule was purely personal, and after his death in 1035 his worthless sons fell apart. With the accession of Edward the Confessor in 1042, the way was open for the Norman Conquest.

Chapter V — The Norman Conquest, 1042–1087

Wakeman opens this chapter with the lingering reign of Eadward the Confessor, a king “weak in body, small in mind,” whose Norman sympathies set the stage for England’s transformation. Two parties contended for mastery: the patriots, headed by the great Earl Godwine of Wessex, “the bulwark of English patriotism and the pride of the English nation”; and the foreigners, whom Eadward imported from Normandy. Godwine, though overshadowing the throne, did so only to protect it. But Eadward, chafing against his minister’s iron will, built a Norman party around favourites like Robert of Jumièges, whom he raised to Canterbury.

Wakeman paints the Normans as Europe’s leading people — conquerors of Sicily, saviours of Rome, patrons of Bec and Jumièges — and notes that their zeal for the papacy contrasted sharply with England’s insular lethargy. The English Church, he says, was deficient in moral leadership and orderly government. A chance brawl at Dover between Eustace of Boulogne and the townsmen gave Robert his pretext: Godwine was outlawed in 1051, and Normans ruled England. But their triumph lasted barely a year. Godwine and Harold sailed up the Thames in 1052 to a triumphant welcome; the Normans fled, Robert and Ulf cutting their way through London streets, sword in hand.

Godwine died soon after, and Harold succeeded as England’s virtual sovereign — more cultured and chivalrous than his father, but deficient in cautious wisdom. Wakeman judges Harold harshly in ecclesiastical matters: he set his face against monasticism, violated canon law, and promoted Stigand to Canterbury, an archbishop who could obtain no pall from a legitimate pope and performed no archiepiscopal functions. This “schismatic” alliance, combined with Harold’s fatal oath to William after being shipwrecked on the French coast in 1064, turned European opinion against England.

The climax is sharply drawn: Eadward’s death, Harold’s victory at Stamford Bridge over Harald Hardrada, and then the fatal field of Senlac. Norman craft and archery prevailed; with Harold’s death “old English simplicity, old English isolation, old English exclusiveness passed away.” Wakeman emphasises that William claimed to reign as Eadward’s lawful heir, not a conqueror — a fiction that preserved English institutions while substituting Norman persons. The chapter closes with Lanfranc’s appointment to Canterbury in 1070 and the sweeping reforms: Norman bishops replacing English, sees moved to fortified cities, churches rebuilt, clerical celibacy enforced (at least on paper), and the landmark separation of ecclesiastical from civil courts — a writ that made canon law distinct from common law and set the stage for two centuries of struggle between Church and State.

Chapter VI — The Struggle between Church and State, 1087–1176

Wakeman declares that the Norman Conquest transformed the English Church more profoundly than even the Reformation would. Within fifty years the Church became “cosmopolitan instead of insular, feudal instead of national, papal instead of independent.” Where once bishops were appointed by king and Witan, and Church law blended with civil, now Rome gained a foothold, legates deposed an archbishop, and the clergy became a separate judicial order.

The narrative centres on two archbishops: Anselm and Becket. Under William Rufus and his minister Ranulf Flambard, feudal logic was ruthlessly applied to the Church: bishoprics were sold or kept vacant while the king seized revenues. After Lanfranc’s death in 1089, Canterbury lay empty four years. When the Red King fell ill in 1093, he forced the archbishopric on Anselm, who protested they were yoking “a wild untameable bull and an old and feeble sheep.” Anselm’s struggle with Rufus at the Council of Rockingham (1095) saw the bishops ready to condemn him, but the lay barons refused — “He has to govern Christian religion in this land,” they said — and the commons rallied to the archbishop. Anselm went into exile in 1097, returning under Henry I in 1100.

The investiture controversy dominates the latter half. Henry I, though more politic than Rufus, was no less masterful. Anselm, having participated in the Lateran council’s condemnation of lay investiture, could not in conscience accept ring and staff from the king. A deadlock ensued. Henry eventually yielded in 1107: he surrendered the symbols of spiritual authority while retaining the oath of homage. Wakeman calls this a “victory of character over law,” arguing that Anselm’s moral resistance preserved English liberty itself.

The chapter builds toward its great set-piece: Thomas Becket. After fifty years of relative peace under Henry II’s efficient but imperious administration, the archbishop — once the king’s chancellor — transformed into an unbending opponent of royal encroachment. The Constitutions of Clarendon (1164) sought to bring the Church under royal control: criminous clerks would be tried in Church courts but sentenced by the king’s court; appeals to Rome would require the king’s leave. Becket accepted, then withdrew his consent, fleeing to exile at Pontigny. The narrative of his return in 1170 and murder in Canterbury Cathedral is vivid — the knights bursting in at vespers, Becket’s defiant “Here is the archbishop — no traitor, but a priest of God,” the blow to his skull, the brains scattered on the floor.

Wakeman’s judgment is nuanced. Becket was no saint in the mould of Anselm — proud, vengeful, theatrical — but a martyr nonetheless: “a witness to the right of the Church to independence of the civil power, an advocate of liberty against overweening authority.” Henry knelt in penance at his tomb in 1174; the Constitutions were withdrawn. Yet the privileges Becket won — benefit of clergy and papal appeals — would in time become shields for the guilty rather than protectors of the helpless. His shadow, “like Athena behind Diomed,” guarded the clergy long after their immunities had degenerated into scandal.

Chapter VII — The Church and English Liberty, 1176–1297

Wakeman frames this chapter around a paradox: the Church had won independence from the crown, yet remained dependent on the king’s physical force. “Not a synod could meet, not a service be held, not a sentence having any effect in this life could be enforced except by the king’s consent.” The Church’s power was purely moral — and in an age of all-mastering kings, that moral power needed reinforcement from Rome. This drove the Church into a tightening alliance with the papacy, which Wakeman describes as a Faustian bargain: the popes helped the English Church resist royal tyranny, but exacted their pound of flesh in money and jurisdiction.

The turning point comes with the disputed election to Canterbury in 1205. When the monks of Christ Church secretly elected Reginald, and then obediently elected King John’s nominee de Grey, Pope Innocent III set aside both and imposed Stephen Langton — an Englishman resident in Rome. John’s furious refusal led to interdict (1208), excommunication (1209), and finally the threat of deposition. In 1213 John surrendered, making England a papal fief. Wakeman calls this the realisation of Hildebrand’s dream — homage that William I had refused was now granted by the degenerate John.

But Langton, once in England, proved the pope’s match. He saw the king’s will governing the law as the root evil; the remedy was that the law should govern the king’s will. At St. Paul’s in August 1213 he produced Henry I’s charter, and on June 15, 1215, the Great Charter was signed at Runnymede. Innocent III annulled it and excommunicated the barons, but John’s death and the succession of the child Henry III allowed Langton and the regent Pembroke to restore the Charter’s principles.

The remainder of the chapter traces the eighty-year consolidation of English liberty. The Church contributed the principle of representation; bishops like Langton, Edmond Rich, and Robert Grosseteste led opposition to tyranny; the Friars — Franciscans arriving in 1224 — ministered to the poor and became Oxford’s leading teachers. Yet the papacy, embroiled in its struggle with the Empire, became increasingly rapacious: Gregory IX demanded a tenth of movables, his legate Otho a fifth of ecclesiastical revenues, Innocent IV a third. Systematised provisions placed Italian non-residents in English benefices; annates drained the Church’s wealth. Grosseteste’s prophetic denunciation of the Roman court before Innocent IV — “the cause, the fountain, the origin of all this is this court of Rome” — marks the breaking point.

The chapter climaxes with Archbishop Winchilsey’s resistance to Edward I over Boniface VIII’s bull Clericis laicos (1296), which forbade taxing the clergy. Edward outlawed the clergy and seized their lands; but the barons, encouraged by clerical resistance, joined the opposition. The Confirmation of the Charters in 1297 placed the national purse under Parliament’s control. Wakeman closes with a soaring tribute to the thirteenth century as the age “which expresses itself most truly in S. Dominic, S. Francis, and S. Thomas Aquinas, which sums itself up most perfectly in Dante” — an age of noble ideals, Gothic aspiration, and the birth of English liberty, midwifed by the Church.

Chapter VIII — The Decay of the Medieval Church, 1297–1485

The papacy, at its zenith in 1300, collapsed within years. Boniface VIII died miserably in 1303; by 1305 the popes had moved to Avignon, becoming for seventy years “an ornamental adjunct to the court of Paris.” The Great Schism (1377–1417) then split western Christendom between rival popes. The moral authority of the papacy died at Avignon, Wakeman argues — once men could not tell who the true successor of St. Peter was, the claim of a vicegerent of God on earth became absurd.

In England, the conditions that had made papal power welcome were reversed. Where once the English clergy were unspiritual and the king’s law capricious, now the clergy produced men like Grosseteste and Rich, and Parliament organised a national judicature. The papacy now sold its justice to the highest bidder. Parliament responded with a legislative assault: the Statute De Religiosis (1279) forbade land grants to religious corporations; Circumspecte Agatis (1285) confined Church courts to spiritual matters; the Statutes of Provisors (1351) and Præmunire (1353) struck at papal appointments and appeals to foreign courts. In 1366 Parliament repudiated John’s annual tribute to Rome.

But the rot was internal too. Clerical wealth and power bred envy and suspicion. The higher clergy lived as ministers of state, non-resident in their dioceses; bishops were appointed for administrative service, not spiritual leadership. The system of papal provisions continued, with Martin V after the Council of Constance brazenly appointing thirteen English bishops by provision and making his fourteen-year-old nephew archdeacon of Canterbury. Yet the anti-papal statutes rusted into disuse as the Wars of the Roses distracted the nation.

The chapter’s central intellectual figure is John Wicliffe of Oxford — brilliant, fearless, fierce in denunciation. He argued that clerical wealth and temporal power were incompatible with Christ’s teaching, attacked transubstantiation as a philosophical construct, and undertook the first complete English translation of the Bible. His followers, the Lollards, Wakeman portrays as “the Levellers of the Middle Ages — half fanatics, half communists” who attacked Church property, war, and the social order itself. The Peasants’ Revolt of 1381 discredited the movement; Archbishop Courtenay obtained Wicliffe’s condemnation as a heretic in 1382, though he died peacefully at Lutterworth.

Persecution followed. The statute De Heretico Comburendo (1401) provided for burning heretics; William Sawtre was burned that year. After Oldcastle’s rebellion (1414), a more stringent act empowered justices to search out offenders. Wakeman notes that perhaps fifty Lollards were executed under this act, but contextualises: “the penalty of death by burning… was not so considered at that cruel time.”

Amid the decay, one bright thread emerges: education. The Church founded the collegiate system — Merton College, Winchester (the first public school), Eton, All Souls, and Christ Church. Wakeman closes on a sombre note: the popes never exercised more unquestioned authority over the English Church than in the fifty years before the Reformation, as the statutes of Provisors and Præmunire lay dormant, and a century of civil war left neither king nor nation with energy to resist.

Chapter IX — The Inner Life of the Church in the Middle Ages

Wakeman opens this chapter with a methodological reflection: historians find it far easier to chronicle the public acts of an institution than its private, daily spiritual life, yet it is by her power over the individual soul that the Church must ultimately be judged. He therefore undertakes to describe the Church’s interior organisation as a spiritual community.

England and Wales were divided into twenty-one dioceses (eighteen in the province of Canterbury, three in York), containing some 8,000 parishes. The bishop was the centre of spiritual jurisdiction, assisted by archdeacons (disciplinary officers) and chancellors (legal experts). But the system was deeply flawed: dioceses were vast and uneven — Lincoln stretched from the Thames to the Humber — yet no suggestion was ever made to increase the episcopate. Bishops were chronically non-resident, often serving as crown ministers or foreign ambassadors. Confirmation was administered casually; ordinations were scandalously lax. Bishop Courtenay admitted 374 men to orders in a single day at Exeter in 1370; in 1337 the diocese of Worcester ordained 849 candidates. A constitution of the legate Otho in 1237 warns against ordaining “senseless persons, bastards, irregular persons, illiterates, and foreigners.”

The parish clergy were divided into rectors (full incumbents), vicars (substitutes), and curates. The distinction arose when monasteries absorbed parish benefices, taking the tithe and leaving a poorly-paid vicar to do the work. Archbishop Langton, following the Lateran Council of 1215, tried to remedy this by establishing vicarages. The priest’s main duties were sacramental: he said the canonical hours daily and mass on Sundays; thrice yearly he heard confessions and gave Communion; four times a year he instructed his people in the articles of faith, the commandments, the seven deadly sins, and the seven sacraments. The chief failing of the clergy was clerical marriage — technically concubinage under enforced celibacy — though Wakeman notes that the English clergy never sank to the continental level of corruption.

The chapter then surveys the religious orders. The Benedictines, introduced by Dunstan, were the dominant monastic order. The Cluniac reform (introduced at Lewes in 1077) represented a stricter Benedictinism, the Cistercians (founded by the Englishman Stephen Harding at Cîteaux in 1098) a still more ascetic ideal of simplicity and isolation — Tintern, Fountains, and Rievaulx are their most famous English houses. The Carthusians, with their rule of absolute silence and solitary confinement, produced only a handful of English Charterhouses. The friars — Dominicans (Preachers) and Franciscans (Minors) — arrived in the thirteenth century and transformed English religious life by plunging into the world as missionaries among the people. Wakeman traces their deterioration from voluntary poverty to professional begging, lamenting that by the fifteenth century the friar had become “a compound of mendacity, cunning, shamelessness, and vice.”

The chapter concludes with a lyrical account of medieval lay piety — the rhythm of the liturgical year, pilgrimages (especially to Canterbury and Walsingham), the use of primers (the medieval equivalent of the Book of Common Prayer), and a moving architectural description of a typical English parish church as it evolved from Norman austerity through Early English grace to Decorated richness. The church building itself, Wakeman argues, is an “epitomised history of the diocese in stone.”

Chapter X — The Church on the Eve of the Reformation, 1485–1529

Wakeman frames this chapter as a crisis of leadership. The Hildebrandine papacy had once offered Christendom unity, discipline, and spiritual guidance, but by the fifteenth century it had failed on every front. The pope had become a national Italian sovereign rather than the universal father of Christendom; the papal court at Rome was a byword for “official extortion and corruption”; and spiritual leadership had evaporated entirely. Wakeman paints a devastating portrait of the popes of the period: Alexander VI could offer Savonarola only the stake, Leo X could meet Luther’s perplexities with nothing but “Recant,” and Julius II, at eighty, “put on his armour and led his hordes of brigands to the storming of Mirandola.” Among the popes of this era, Wakeman writes, “nearly every quality is represented except that of religion.”

The chapter then turns to the better condition of things in England. The English Church had maintained enough independence to escape the worst continental corruptions. The indulgence system never reached the proportions that goaded Luther in Germany. The English humanist movement was led by devout churchmen: Archbishop Warham, Dean Colet, Sir Thomas More, and even Henry VIII himself. Erasmus was attracted to England by “the wit of More and the pension of Warham” — where Erasmus was, there was no danger of revolution.

Henry VII had pursued a cautious, economical policy, leaning on the Church and dispensing with parliaments. He handed to his son a power more uncontrolled than any English king had enjoyed since Henry II. The young Henry VIII, ascending the throne in 1509, seemed destined for greatness: handsome, athletic, learned, generous, surrounded by the wisdom of Fox, the judgment of More, and the genius of Wolsey. But Wakeman darkly foreshadows the degeneration: “If the restraints of discipline were once cast to the winds, and the lust of power and possession allowed full scope, what limit was possible to cruelty and tyranny?”

Wolsey dominates the second half of the chapter. He was “essentially a politician, not an ecclesiastic” — the first great English foreign minister in the modern sense. Though a pluralist and the father of an illegitimate family, he was not irreligious. As papal legate, he planned administrative reforms of the ecclesiastical courts. He suppressed smaller monasteries that had ceased to serve any purpose and used their revenues for his school at Ipswich and Cardinal’s College at Oxford. Wakeman presents Wolsey as the last and most splendid of the medieval Church reformers, standing at the end of a long line from Wilfrid and Dunstan to Wykeham and Colet.

The chapter closes with a crucial contrast: the Reformation on the continent was a hot rebellion against spiritual wickedness that overthrew the Church and manufactured new theology. In England, by contrast, the Reformation “began as a matter of policy, an affair of kings and ministers and parliaments” — constitutional and legal, not theological. Wolsey’s plans for organic reform were soon to be shattered by the king’s imperious will.

Chapter XI — The Repudiation of Papal Authority, 1529–1534

Wakeman begins by insisting that Henry’s divorce suit was “not the cause but merely the occasion” of the break with Rome. The real causes lay deeper: the long medieval struggle against papal extortion, the loss of papal moral authority, and the growing conviction among Englishmen that papal supremacy was foreign supremacy. “All that was wanted,” Wakeman writes, “was a leader and an occasion.”

Henry’s marriage to Catherine of Arragon had been happy for a decade, but by 1524 — with Catherine ageing and no male heir surviving — the king began to contemplate annulment. His scruples were probably genuine at first, but they were fatally complicated when he fell violently in love with Anne Boleyn. From that moment, “the strongest of human passions was brought to bear upon the most imperious of kingly wills.”

The legal difficulties were immense. Catherine had been married to Henry’s elder brother Arthur; Julius II had issued a bull of dispensation in the fullest possible terms, with a confirming brief to guard against technical objections. Henry was asking Clement VII to repudiate a recent act of his own predecessor — a blow to papal authority itself. Matters were made worse when imperial troops stormed Rome in May 1527, making Clement a prisoner of the Emperor Charles V, Catherine’s nephew. As long as the pope was politically dependent on the emperor, he could not grant Henry’s suit.

Wakeman traces the painful negotiations: Clement could not refuse to hear Catherine’s appeal without abdicating his office, yet as the emperor’s political vassal he could not decide against him. The real lesson Henry drew from this impasse was that papal supremacy “proved practically to be foreign supremacy” — intolerable for a sovereign nation. “Royal supremacy, however tyrannical in its exercise, was at least national.”

The fall of Wolsey in 1529 — convicted of præmunire for exercising legatine authority at the king’s own request — was the first act of the new drama. Then Henry turned on the whole clergy, forcing Convocation in 1531 to acknowledge him as “supreme head of the English Church and Clergy, so far as the law of Christ will allow.” The Annates Act of 1532 stopped payments to Rome and asserted England’s right to consecrate bishops without papal bulls. The Submission of the Clergy in 1532 forced Convocation to surrender its independent legislative power. Stephen Gardiner, the conservative bishop of Winchester, emerges as the defender of a national Catholicism — rejecting papal supremacy while opposing Protestant innovation.

The appointment of Thomas Cranmer as Archbishop of Canterbury in 1532 proved decisive. Cranmer, a learned lawyer rather than a theologian, devised the strategy of consulting the universities of Europe to show that the pope had no power to dispense from divine law. In May 1533 Cranmer pronounced Henry’s marriage to Catherine void and his marriage to Anne Boleyn valid. Clement VII responded by declaring Anne’s marriage null and affirming Catherine’s — the breach was complete.

Wakeman insists that this was no founding of a new Church. The Act in Restraint of Appeals (1533) declared “this realm of England is an empire,” and the Supreme Head Act (1534) gave the king visitatorial power over the Church. But all these measures were designed to restore the Church of England to its pre-Norman independence, not to create something novel. The men who carried them out — Gardiner, Bonner, Tunstall — were devout Catholics. The chapter closes with Wakeman’s measured reflection on the tragedy of the break: division is “after all a sin, and carries with it the consequences of sin,” though the historian declines to apportion blame between the two sides.

Chapter XII — The Royal Tyranny, 1534–1547

With the papal authority repudiated, Henry VIII emerged as “absolute and uncontrolled master of England.” Wakeman opens this chapter with a chilling description of the Tudor state: the law was wholly at the king’s command, Parliament was packed with his nominees, and the Church was defenceless against a king who could interpret the elastic formula of the supremacy however he wished. “To be accused by the crown was to be condemned by the law.”

The instrument of Henry’s tyranny was Thomas Cromwell — “a pure adventurer,” an Italianate Renaissance figure who had served Wolsey and now served the king with “plenty of ambition and no religion of his own.” For seven years Cromwell executed the king’s will. In 1534 the Nun of Kent, Elizabeth Barton, was executed for prophesying Henry’s death. The Succession Act required all persons to swear that Henry’s marriage to Catherine was invalid and to repudiate papal authority. Sir Thomas More and Bishop Fisher, who would swear to the succession but not to the repudiation, were imprisoned. The Verbal Treasons Act of 1534 made it high treason even to wish the king harm — any words questioning the supremacy could mean death.

The executions followed in ghastly procession. The Carthusian priors of the London Charterhouse were hanged in May 1535. Fisher, who had been made a cardinal by Paul III, was beheaded on June 22. Sir Thomas More — “the most attractive personality of an unattractive age” — followed on July 6, betrayed by the perjury of the solicitor-general Rich. “When the news got abroad,” Wakeman writes, “a thrill of horror seized Europe.”

Henry then turned on the monasteries. Cromwell, appointed vicar-general in 1535, claimed a jurisdiction superior even to the archbishops. A general visitation of the religious houses was conducted, and in February 1536 Parliament voted to suppress all houses with incomes under £200 a year — on the king’s say-so, without the actual reports ever being laid before the House. Parliament expressly commended the larger houses where “religion is right well kept,” but within three years they too were forced into “voluntary” surrender through intimidation and starvation. The Pilgrimage of Grace, a northern rebellion of 30,000 men in October 1536, was drowned in blood. The abbots of Hexham, Whalley, Furness, and Jervaulx were attainted and executed.

Wakeman condemns the dissolution in the strongest terms: “Never did a great institution fall by more unworthy hands or in a more unworthy manner.” The waste was “wanton barbarity worthy only of savage races” — books destroyed, buildings unroofed, hospitals closed. What was needed was “adaptation not confiscation.” For three centuries England has been trying to replace the capital thus wasted. The Church gained six poorly-endowed bishoprics; the poor were the direct sufferers.

The Act of Six Articles (1539) completed the tyranny, making denial of transubstantiation punishable by burning without opportunity of recantation — “the religious counterpart of the Verbal Treasons Act.” Wakeman notes that 500 persons were presented in London alone in the first year. The last years of the reign were a catalogue of judicial murder: the Countess of Salisbury, Anne Askew, the Earl of Surrey. “It is said that altogether during the reign of Henry VIII no less than 70,000 persons were put to death for offences against the laws.”

Wakeman closes with a measured judgment: the Tudor government was “constitutional in its forms, arbitrary in its results,” but for the individual it was simply terror. The same ruin awaited the noblest and the humblest. “Under such a government every man and every institution lived merely at the king’s will.” A learned Note B appended to the chapter scrutinises the evidence for monastic corruption, concluding that “the old belief that the general corruption of the monasteries was established to the satisfaction of Parliament… can no longer be held.”

Chapter XIII — The Growth of Protestant Influences in the Church, 1526–1549

Wakeman opens this chapter by tracing the penetration of Continental Protestant ideas into England, particularly the Zwinglian doctrines disseminated through William Tyndale’s English translation of the New Testament. Tyndale, far from giving a neutral rendering, prefixed aggressively anti-Church prefaces to his work and employed tendentious vocabulary — substituting “congregation” for “Church” and “senior” for “priest” — that pushed the Swiss reformer’s agenda. The book flowed into England via Dutch trade routes, smuggled in bales of Flanders cloth, and fell on fertile ground: the old Lollard hostility to priestcraft, widespread dissatisfaction with the Church’s condition, and Renaissance-bred eagerness among university men to restate doctrine in light of renewed scriptural and patristic knowledge. By 1529, a growing number across all classes had been affected by Zwinglian teaching, while many more stood ready to follow if the king willed.

But Henry VIII did not so will. Wakeman portrays Henry as a theologically learned, personally devout king who remained “firmly and conscientiously attached” to his Catholic upbringing. He loathed Lutheranism and still more opposed Zwingli’s anti-sacramental doctrines. Wakeman emphasises that Henry never tolerated any form of Protestantism. Throughout his reign, he meted out the same punishment to Lutherans, Zwinglians, Calvinists, and Anabaptists alike: death at the stake. At least thirty persons were burned for Protestant opinions in Henry’s reign, including Bilney (1531), Frith and Hewitt (1533), Lambert (1538), Barnes, Jerome and Gerard (1540), and Anne Askew (1546). Wakeman insists these were purely religious executions, not political ones — Frith, Lambert, and Anne Askew were no threat to Henry’s government; they died simply because they held Eucharistic beliefs the king judged false.

Nevertheless, Henry was willing to remedy defects in the Church, provided the crown’s authority was unimpaired. Wakeman identifies three principles guiding his cautious reforms: (1) absolute royal control over religious belief and practice; (2) the progressive introduction of English into worship — the English Bible, English lessons, and Cranmer’s English Litany (1544); and (3) abolition only of proved abuses, preserving Catholic doctrine while stripping away medieval exaggerations like indulgences and superstitious image-worship. The chapter catalogues the resulting formularies: the Ten Articles (1536), the Bishops’ Book or Institution of a Christian Man (1537), the Great Bible (1538), and the carefully composed King’s Book or Necessary Erudition (1543), which Wakeman calls “by far the most carefully considered” of Henry’s doctrinal statements.

With Henry’s death in 1547 and Edward VI’s minority, power passed to a privy council Wakeman condemns in scathing terms: “Never did the chief rule in England fall into hands of men of meaner mould.” At their head was the rapacious Protector Somerset, who plundered abbeys, churches, and episcopal estates with shameless greed. Associated with him were Rich, Audley, Paget, and the future Northumberland. The single honest man among them was Thomas Cranmer, whom Wakeman characterises as a gifted academic hopelessly unsuited to high office — deferential to the point of servility under Henry, and entirely lacking independence under Somerset and Northumberland. Cranmer’s theology drifted: essentially Lutheran under Henry, he converted to a Calvinistic view of the Eucharist under Ridley’s influence by 1548.

The council pursued an aggressively Erastian policy, treating the Church as a mere department of state. They issued the first Book of Homilies (1547), ordered the destruction of images and stained glass through the 1547 Injunctions, imprisoned Gardiner and Bonner for opposition, and in 1549 produced the first Prayer Book, enforced by the first Act of Uniformity. Wakeman analyses this Prayer Book at length, praising it as “instinct with the spirit of the Catholic Church,” based on the Sarum Use, simplified and translated into English, with a strong emphasis on Scripture-reading and congregational participation. He notes that the book completed Henry’s work rather than beginning the council’s — Gardiner and Tunstall were willing to use it, but the council immediately began undermining it, ordering the disuse of traditional ceremonies and vestments. The only serious protest was the Devonshire rising, which Wakeman notes was as much against governmental rapacity as religious change.

Chapter XIV — Alterations in Religion, 1549–1559

Wakeman begins this chapter by noting the legalisation of clerical marriage in 1549, a change that while morally beneficial in one respect brought with it the grinding poverty that would plague the clergy for generations. He then recounts how the council, emboldened by suppressing the Devonshire rebellion, turned on Bishop Bonner: he was ordered to preach at Paul’s Cross, his sermon denounced, and he was deprived of his see by royal commission without any ecclesiastical trial — the first of many illegal deprivations.

Hardly was Bonner imprisoned before Somerset himself fell. In October 1549, the Protector was ousted by Warwick, soon Duke of Northumberland — a pure self-seeker whom Wakeman portrays as a probable Catholic at heart who leagued with Protestants out of ambition. Under Northumberland, religious interests became entirely subordinate to political ones.

The chapter documents the accelerating changes of the period. A new Ordinal was published in 1550, retaining essential elements of the Sarum rite while drastically simplifying ceremonies. The episcopate was reshaped: Heath of Worcester was imprisoned for refusing the Ordinal; Ridley received London; and altars were systematically destroyed, beginning with Ridley’s unauthorised destruction of the altar at St Paul’s. Wakeman notes that the existing altars were protected by the Prayer-book’s parliamentary authority, but revolutionary politics did not stop at trifles. Gardiner was finally deprived of Winchester in 1552, replaced by Ponet, who surrendered the see’s endowments to the crown.

The destruction of altars was the first direct governmental step against the doctrine of the Real Presence. Wakeman explains that Cranmer, in his famous 1548 parliamentary speech, had declared himself (in the delighted interpretation of his Swiss correspondents) effectively a Calvinist on the Eucharist. A more radical Prayer Book was produced in 1552, visibly more Protestant than its predecessor: it omitted the prayer for the departed, changed the altar to a table, ordered communion in both kinds, and replaced the words of administration with more didactic formulae. Wakeman judges the 1552 Book the product of “unconstitutional violence” rather than “constitutional consideration,” noting that Convocation was not consulted and bishops who protested were illegally deprived. The Forty-two Articles of Religion were imposed by the same methods in 1552, “a series of documents in which the reforming doctrines were stated in their most uncompromising form.”

Edward VI’s death in 1553 brought a dramatic reversal. Lady Jane Grey’s nine-day reign collapsed, and Mary Tudor ascended the throne, determined to restore Roman Catholicism. The Reformation legislation was repealed, the Mass restored, and married clergy expelled en masse. Wakeman reports that about 800 clergy were driven from their livings; a few were “discreetly hidden” by their bishops. Then came the burnings: nearly 280 persons, including Cranmer, Ridley, Latimer, and Hooper, were executed between 1555 and 1558. Wakeman notes that Mary’s marriage to Philip II entangled England in Spanish interests, culminating in the loss of Calais — a disaster that broke Mary’s heart and caused her death in November 1558.

Elizabeth’s accession in 1558 opened a new era. The Acts of Supremacy and Uniformity (1559) re-established the Royal Supremacy under the title “Supreme Governor” rather than “Supreme Head” (a distinction Wakeman treats as constitutionally significant), and revived the 1552 Prayer Book with three important modifications: the omission of the “Black Rubric,” the combination of the two communion sentences, and the retention of the Eucharistic vestments. All but one bishop (Kitchin of Llandaff) refused the oath and were deprived, creating an episcopal vacuum that was filled by the consecration of Matthew Parker at Lambeth in December 1559. The chapter concludes by surveying the dire state of the Church Elizabeth inherited: depleted universities, impoverished clergy, vacant cures, and a shortage of educated ministers. Parker and his bishops did what they could with large ordinations and temporary expedients like reviving the minor order of Lector, but Wakeman starkly notes that “the English Church found herself in a greater state of spiritual and intellectual destitution at the beginning of the reign of Elizabeth than she had experienced since the coming of the Normans.” The constructive work was completed with the Thirty-nine Articles (1571) and the Second Book of Homilies, establishing the Prayer Book as the standard of doctrine and worship.

Chapter XV — The Growth of Puritanism, 1559–1604

Wakeman opens this chapter by describing Elizabeth’s first decade as one of “infinite peril for both Church and State,” saved by Philip II’s procrastination more than her own diplomacy. While loyalty to Elizabeth’s person grew into enthusiasm, opposition to her ecclesiastical system hardened among a significant section of the nation. The reason was the religious complexion of the episcopate itself: with the exception of Parker and a few older bishops, the new appointees — Grindal, Sandys, Parkhurst, Cox, Jewel — had returned from exile in Zurich, Frankfurt, or Geneva as committed adherents of Calvin and Bullinger. They merely tolerated the Prayer Book, hoping to alter it. Since there was no other source of clergy, their Calvinist views permeated the ministry.

Wakeman vividly describes how the destruction of medieval libraries and textbooks left a theological vacuum that Calvin’s Institutes filled. “English theology, as we now know it in Hooker and Andrewes and Jeremy Taylor, was not yet written,” he notes; Calvin’s work became “the acknowledged textbook of the English universities, the mould in which the religious opinions of the English clergy for half a century were formed.” The resulting theology was harsh: it taught total human corruption, predestination and reprobation, denied the Real Presence, and substituted the congregation of the elect for the visible Church.

The chapter traces the first open division among the reformers — the Vestiarian controversy — over clerical dress. Sampson and Humphrey at Oxford refused to wear any distinctive dress, supported by Beza of Geneva, while the bishops, on Bullinger’s advice, reluctantly conformed to the queen’s wishes. From these disputes emerged the Puritans, who objected to vestments, kneeling at communion, the sign of the cross in baptism, and the ring in marriage, and who preferred presbyterian to episcopal government. Wakeman notes that neither conforming nor nonconforming Puritans thought of leaving the Church: “To be a member of a sect within the nation was the very last thing that Puritan or Anglican desired to be. Ascendancy, not toleration, was the aim.”

Elizabeth and Parker attempted to enforce a minimum of observance through the Advertisements of 1566, which mandated the surplice, kneeling, the use of a font, and distinctive clerical dress. Thirty-seven London clergy suffered deprivation rather than comply, and some began holding meetings in private houses. Puritan strength in Parliament and at Court (Leicester was their patron) made enforcement difficult; a 1563 petition against disliked practices was lost in Convocation by a single vote.

The conflict escalated in 1572 when Thomas Cartwright launched an attack on episcopacy itself, followed by the publication of the Book of Discipline (1580), which established a parallel presbyterian structure of “classes” and synods operating alongside the legal Church system. Wakeman calls this the point at which Puritanism revealed its true aim: not reform but abolition of the Church of England. “Calvinism postulates a presbyterian form of Church government just as certainly as the Catholic Church postulates episcopacy,” he writes — the two systems were incompatible.

Elizabeth responded firmly. She suppressed the “prophesyings” (devotional meetings) in 1577, driving Archbishop Grindal into forced retirement for supporting them. In 1583 she appointed Whitgift, a theologically Calvinist but disciplinarily anti-Presbyterian archbishop, who enforced subscription to the Supremacy, Prayer Book, and Thirty-nine Articles. The Martin Marprelate libels (1588) — a series of scurrilous attacks on the bishops — backfired, alienating moderate opinion. In 1593, an Act of Parliament banished nonconformists, driving the advanced Puritans (Brownists or Independents) to exile in Holland, while the Presbyterian Puritans fell back into conformity.

The chapter concludes with the accession of James I and the Millenary Petition of 1603, signed by some 800 clergy, requesting abolition of confirmation, the cross in baptism, the ring in marriage, and the terms “priest” and “absolution.” At the Hampton Court Conference (January 1604), the Puritan spokesmen — Reynolds, Sparkes, Chaderton, and Knewstubbs — pressed demands that went far beyond ceremonial accommodation: they sought to alter the Articles to teach Calvinistic predestination and reprobation. When Reynolds implied bishops should consult presbyters, James famously retorted, “A Scottish presbytery agreeth as well with monarchy as God and the devil.” The conference failed. The Canons of 1604 asserted the Church’s Catholic claims and required all clergy to swear ex animo to the Prayer Book and Articles. About 300 strict Puritans fled overseas.

Wakeman’s final judgment is pointed: “Dresses and ceremonies are in themselves indifferent. As the visible expression of doctrine they are in the highest degree significant.” The struggle was never really about cap and surplice, but about two incompatible religious systems — one Catholic, the other Calvinist — contending for the soul of England. The Hampton Court Conference marked England’s choice: the Church Catholic would remain the established religion, and if the Puritans wished to supplant it, they must do so “by force and by force alone.”

Chapter XVI — The Strengthening of the Church, 1604–1662

Wakeman opens this chapter by describing the intense pressure that Puritan and Roman Catholic attacks placed on the Church of England, forcing Englishmen “back upon first principles.” They had to ask what they meant when reciting the Creed and using the Prayer-book, and had to justify their simultaneous claim to Catholic heritage and rejection of papal supremacy. The Reformation movement in England, Wakeman argues, “produced no great characters and few great writers,” whereas “the Church revival of the seventeenth century, with all its faults, was an age of saints and of students.” Thrown back upon herself, the Church had to restate her theology in the light of the crisis, appealing to Scripture as the source of truth, to the consent of the Church Universal as the witness and interpreter of Scripture, and to reason as “the illuminator and informer of the mind and conscience.” This middle position between Rome and Protestantism demanded moral and intellectual effort and did not provide “an oracle for the solution of all doubts.”

The chapter traces the intellectual foundations of this revival. Richard Hooker’s Laws of Ecclesiastical Polity (1594) laid down philosophical principles that showed “where the intellectual basis of Puritanism was wrong.” Bancroft’s 1589 sermon at St. Paul’s claimed episcopacy as a divine order; Saravia and Bilson reinforced the doctrine of Apostolical succession. Wakeman then presents two towering figures. Lancelot Andrewes is portrayed as the foremost saintly bishop — gentle, wise, and detached from worldly ambition. His controversy with Cardinal Bellarmine made “that appeal to history which is the articulus stantis aut cadentis ecclesiae of the English Church.” Casaubon called Andrewes the embodiment of “true piety” and “uprightness of judgment.” Andrewes used full ceremonial — copes, lights, incense, the mixed chalice — in his own chapel while tolerantly requiring only a minimum of decency from others.

William Laud is treated at length and with balanced judgment. Wakeman acknowledges the prejudice and hatred Laud attracted but argues that truth “lies between the two extremes.” Laud was “the legitimate successor of Andrewes and Hooker” in his conception of the Church’s double character as Catholic and anti-papal. He condemned Calvinist reprobation because “it makes God, the God of all mercies, to be the most fierce and unreasonable tyrant in the world.” He won back converts from Rome and retained John Hales in the Church. Yet Laud’s tragedy was that he was “essentially a man of action” and an administrator rather than a persuader. His enforcement of discipline — the surplice, kneeling, the railing of altars, the High Commission’s moral policing — made him deeply unpopular. By identifying the Church’s cause with the king’s prerogative, “every act of royal misgovernment weakened the moral appeal of the Church.” The fall was inevitable: “No bishop no king” was the motto of the age, and both fell together. Wakeman records Laud’s diary entry upon impeachment and his scaffold speech: “This poor Church of England hath flourished and been a shelter to other neighbouring churches.” Mr. Gladstone is quoted: “Laud was the man who prevented the English Church from being bound in the fetters of an iron system of compulsory and Calvinistic belief.”

The chapter then narrates the Puritan suppression — the abolition of episcopacy, the Westminster Assembly, the Directory replacing the Prayer-book, the ejection of the clergy, and the ecclesiastical anarchy that followed. Cromwell’s Protectorate established toleration for most Protestants but excluded “papists, prelatists, and antinomians.” The Restoration in 1660 brought the Church back “as a matter of course.” Bishops emerged from prison and exile, and by the summer of 1661 the Church was reinstated in her temporalities. The Savoy Conference of 1661 revealed the irreconcilable division between Church and Puritanism. The Act of Uniformity (1662) made the revised Prayer-book the only legal service-book, and some two thousand ministers resigned rather than conform. Wakeman calls the settlement of 1662 “the last act of her Reformation,” solving both the relation to Rome and to Protestantism. The Church emerged “reformed on Catholic lines, and, freed from Puritanism, was able to discharge her own duties to her own people in her own way.”

Chapter XVII — The Beginning of Religious Toleration, 1662–1714

This chapter opens with the recognition that the settlement of 1662 made toleration a political necessity. Competing religious bodies — Roman Catholics, Presbyterians, Independents, Baptists, Quakers — could not be expected to vanish. Wakeman offers a strikingly unsentimental account of toleration’s genesis: it was not born as a moral principle but forced upon statesmen who “began with a bad grace to endure what they could not cure.” Toleration was “used first as a political expedient, and then raised to the dignity of a moral principle.”

Parliament, driven by revenge and fear, imposed a harsh penal code. The Corporation Act (1661) excluded nonconformists from municipal office; the Act of Uniformity imposed religious disabilities; the Conventicle Act (1664) made nonconformist worship illegal and permitted transportation for repeated offences; the Five Mile Act (1665) banned nonconformist ministers from approaching any town; the Test Act (1673) required all office-holders to receive Communion according to Church rites and declare against transubstantiation. Wakeman delivers a fierce condemnation of the Sacramental Test: “It kept out only the honest and high-minded. It dulled the religious instinct, and lowered irretrievably for generations the whole conception of the Sacraments.”

Charles II’s attempts at toleration — including the 1672 Declaration of Indulgence — failed before Parliamentary opposition inflamed by fear of Roman Catholicism. James II’s accession made the danger “instant and menacing.” The chapter vividly narrates James’s use of the dispensing power, his revival of the High Commission, his attacks on the universities (especially the Magdalen College affair), and his order that the clergy read the Declaration of Indulgence. The response of the seven bishops — Sancroft, White, Lloyd, Turner, Lake, Ken, and Trelawney — is one of the chapter’s dramatic high points. Their petition, their trial, and the thunderous “Not Guilty” verdict produced “a scene of wild excitement unparalleled in the history of English courts of law.”

The Revolution of 1688 is presented as a national movement. The Toleration Act of 1689 marks “the beginning of religious toleration in England” — though it still excluded Roman Catholics, Unitarians, and Jews, and left civil disabilities untouched. Wakeman calls it “at once the Great Charter of religious freedom, and the triumph of common sense.”

The chapter then turns to the Church’s internal life after the Restoration. Two great gifts survived the Puritan ordeal: the Prayer-book (“it became, as their enemies expressed it, their idol”) and episcopacy. The physical restoration of churches was remarkable — Sheldon spent up to £70,000 on public works, Hacket rebuilt Lichfield Cathedral, Seth Ward raised £25,000 for Exeter. Wakeman defends the moral tone of the Restoration, arguing that the court’s profligacy was a “poisonous exotic” from France and that a devout society existed alongside it — the names of Evelyn, Boyle, Nelson, and Lady Russell attest to “strict and devout lives.” The Caroline divines — Cosin, Bull, Pearson, South, Stillingfleet, the Cambridge Platonists — are praised for learning and devotion.

Yet the seeds of decay were present. The division between High Churchmen and Latitudinarians hardened along political lines. The expulsion of the nonjurors — Sancroft, Ken, Frampton, and about four hundred clergy — is lamented as a “very serious blow” that lost the Church “the very best of the English clergy and laity.” Party spirit tore at the Church: “Descending into the arena of party strife, the Church quickly lost her spiritual ascendancy, and became begrimed with the dust of the conflict. Victories at the polls were more sought after than the triumphs of the Cross, and Atterbury takes the place of Ken as the typical Churchman of his time.”

The chapter concludes on a more hopeful note with the foundation of the religious societies, the Society for Promoting Christian Knowledge (1698), and the Society for the Propagation of the Gospel in Foreign Parts (1701). The reign of Anne saw “a vigorous and intense spiritual vitality” in the Church — the surrender of the firstfruits, the building of fifty new churches, the flourishing of daily services. But the final sentence is ominous: as the bells rang out for George I in 1714, “they sounded the death-knell of her high ideals and her vigorous life for more than half a century.”

Chapter XVIII — The Ascendancy of Latitudinarianism, 1714–1760

Wakeman opens by posing the central riddle: why did the “lofty aspirations and bustling activity” of the Church under Anne give way to “the placid and somnolent quiet” of the mid-eighteenth century? How did the religious societies that flourished in 1714 give way to an age where John Wesley and his Oxford society were “jeered at as fanatics as they went to their weekly Communion at St. Mary’s”?

The answer lies in three interlocking causes. First, the repressive policy of the Whig government. From 1714 to 1760, no Tory politician entered the inner councils of the sovereign, and no High Churchman was made a bishop. Some bishops’ beliefs “approached nearer to those of the Unitarians than to the teaching of the Prayer-book.” Second, the suppression of Convocation from 1717 to 1850. After an attack on Bishop Hoadly’s latitudinarian writings, the government refused to allow Convocation to meet for business. This deprived the Church of “the only means by which they could learn to respect each other through the influence of free discussion.” Third, the Latitudinarian spirit itself, which “depressed enthusiasm” and insisted that “religion is agreeable to common sense.”

Wakeman is careful to acknowledge the genuine achievements of the Latitudinarians — Burnet, Tillotson, Stillingfleet, Tenison — who “produced a body of men second to none of the High Church bishops in intellectual power and practical influence.” In the controversy with Deists and Unitarians, their rational defence of Christianity “was not only effective but triumphant,” and Bishop Butler is given particular praise. Yet the limitations were fatal: “Men after all are not all head, and in most men the head is not the most important part of their organisation.” Latitudinarianism “removed God away from the human heart to enthrone Him among the clouds and the snows of an intellectual Olympus.”

The consequences were devastating. The religious societies died out by 1730. The S.P.C.K. and S.P.G. lost much of their former activity. “Since the time of Edward VI the universities had not been so corrupt and so inefficient and produced so few great men.” Pluralities and non-residence were “conspicuous throughout the century.” Bishops rarely appeared in their dioceses: Hoadly “never set foot in his diocese of Bangor during the six years that he presided over it,” and Watson of Llandaff settled in Westmoreland and “achieved such success as an agriculturist” that he regained his health and provided for his family.

Daily service in town churches was given up. In country parishes where the rector was a pluralist and no curate existed, only one service on Sunday was possible. The Eucharist was celebrated only on the three great festivals. At St. Paul’s Cathedral in London in 1800, “there were but six communicants at the only celebration on Easter Day.” Old fonts were removed to rectory gardens to serve as flower-pots. Churches themselves — “whitewashed walls, damp stone floors, high stiff pews with mouldy green baize cushions” — spoke of “apathy and exclusiveness.” Meanwhile, the rich built “huge palaces for themselves after the Italian manner” while the teeming industrial population “was allowed to grow up without churches, without schools, without religion.”

Wakeman does, however, trace one clear improvement: religious toleration gained acceptance as a moral principle throughout the century. The Occasional Conformity and Schism Acts were repealed in 1718. By the mid-century, Protestant nonconformists were effectively relieved of civil disabilities through annual indemnity acts. The penal laws against Roman Catholics were relaxed from 1778, though the Gordon Riots of 1780 showed the persistence of fanaticism. Wakeman notes the eventual repeal of the Test and Corporation Acts (1828) and Catholic Emancipation (1829) as the culmination of this process.

On the character of the clergy, Wakeman offers a measured verdict. Their faults “were rather those of the time than of the men.” They were “decidedly better than the laity,” exemplary in their lives, diligent in study, kindly in nature. The names of Warburton, Butler, Berkeley, Waterland, and Paley attest to their learning. Yet they lacked “the subtle power which sanctity and devotion alone can give.” “If the coal from off the altar did not touch their lips, if the sword of the spirit did not pierce their hearts, if the power of the Cross was not dominant in their lives, it was because these are the special gifts and rewards of ‘enthusiasm,’ and to be an ‘enthusiast’ in the eighteenth century was to be out of harmony with the age.” The chapter ends with the Church in a state of spiritual deadness, “lethargy, like a malarious fog,” creeping upon her heart — setting the stage for the Methodist revival that Chapter XIX will describe.

Chapter XIX — Methodism and the Evangelical Revival, 1738–1833

John Wesley and the formation of the Methodist society, 1729–1739 — The origin of Methodism in the Oxford religious society formed by John Wesley in 1729, the influence of William Law’s Serious Call upon Wesley’s early religious development, and the cardinal principle of ardent love of God as the basis of his religious life. The Aldersgate Street experience of 1738, when Wesley felt his “heart strangely warmed” while hearing Luther’s preface to the Epistle to the Romans, gave him a conviction of the reality of religion different in kind from the grace of baptism or ordination. From this moment he took “the whole of England as his parish,” setting out on preaching tours. The extraordinary effects of his preaching — strong men rolling on the ground in hysterical convulsions, thousands converted, chiefly among the lower classes who were largely outside the ordinary influence of the Church. The elaborate organisation of the Methodist society grew up under Wesley’s administrative talent: circuits of itinerant lay preachers, preaching houses, class meetings, band meetings, and the conference. Wakeman compares Methodism to a religious order (like the friars preachers or the Society of Jesus) but notes its crucial difference: it had no “rule” approved by the Church, no constitutional status.

George Whitefield and the Calvinist secession, 1740–1781 — Whitefield’s extraordinary natural gifts as an orator — a noble presence, a voice capable of every shade of emotion, the quick instincts of an actor — which drew congregations of ten or twenty thousand and moved even cynical men like Chesterfield. The theological parting of ways: Whitefield’s Calvinism (absolute corruption of man’s nature, election, justification by faith alone) versus Wesley’s Arminianism (the doctrine of election making God a tyrant and taking away moral responsibility). Lady Huntingdon’s support for the Calvinist side. The division hardened into two streams: Calvinistic Methodists (Lady Huntingdon’s Connection) and Wesleyan Methodists (Arminian).

Estrangement between Wesley and the Church — The fundamental structural conflict: Wesley’s society overrode all diocesan and parochial limits by the simple will of its founder. Wakeman argues this was “the very spirit of schism” — the Church’s territorial organisation (diocese, parish) was replaced by the society’s circuit system (circuits for parishes, superintendents for bishops, Wesley as pope). Wesley’s claim that the organisation of the Church was “a rope of sand” justified ignoring parochial limits. The registration of preaching houses as Nonconformist meeting-houses under the Toleration Act and the licensing of lay preachers as Nonconformist teachers hastened separation.

The breach completed, 1744–1795 — The first Wesleyan conference in 1744 defined the society’s relation to the Church: they would obey bishops “in things indifferent” and observe canons “as far as with a safe conscience they could.” Distinct acts of schism occurred from 1760 when Norwich lay preachers began administering Holy Communion. Grimshaw of Haworth declared the Methodists to all intents and purposes a nonconformist sect. Wesley’s own consecration of two superintendents for America in 1784 was an act of separation in deed though not in word. His pathetic appeal in 1789 — “In God’s name, stop! Be Church of England men still” — failed to prevent formal separation after his death in 1791. In 1795 the preachers were authorised to administer the Lord’s Supper, and separation was finally accomplished. Internal dissensions followed: the Methodist New Connexion (1797), the Primitive Methodists (1810), and ultimately nineteen different forms of Methodism registered.

Character of Wesley’s work — Wakeman’s estimate: John Wesley the greatest religious figure of the eighteenth century, the S. Francis of his age. The parallel drawn in detail — the direct call of God, the revival of personal religion through a society, the mission to the poor and outcast, the use of colloquial sermons, the religious use of hymns, the renunciation of pomp. The crucial difference: S. Francis’s sons, guided by Innocent III, remained devoted children of the Church; Wesley’s sons, under the constitutional anarchy of Archbishop Potter, found their natural sphere outside it.

The earlier Evangelical leaders — The paradox: Wesley, though guilty of acts of schism, was a zealous Churchman to the end, while his Calvinist friends (Grimshaw, Berridge, Venn, Romaine) remained beneficed priests of the Church of England despite sharing Wesley’s disregard for order. The explanation in their moderate Calvinism: basing religion on total depravity but using it to magnify God’s love in redemption, not His justice in condemnation. They found support in the Articles and Homilies, interpreted the Prayer-book by their own prepossessions, and never grasped the conception of the Church as a living visible society with apostolic succession. Their strength: they never asked to alter the formularies of the Church.

Work of the Evangelical party — The Evangelical party as the “salt of the Church of England” in the late eighteenth century. The gradual triumph of piety and character over prejudice: Fletcher at Madeley among his Shropshire colliers, Venn among the factory hands of Huddersfield, Romaine among the middle classes of Southwark. The dramatic story of John Newton, saved from the slave trade to become the friend of the tempted. Thomas Scott’s commentary on the Bible. The influence of Cowper’s verses and Hannah More’s writings in making Evangelicalism interesting to people of culture. Charles Wesley’s hymns and Toplady’s giving English religion its “unique consecration.”

Philanthropy and missions, 1781–1833 — The philanthropic awakening: Robert Raikes’s Sunday schools at Gloucester (1781), the first bishop for English-speaking races outside England (1784), the Religious Tract Society (1799), the British and Foreign Bible Society (1804). The Evangelical party’s greatest cause: the awakening of the national conscience against the slave trade. William Wilberforce — of good family, favourite of Pitt, speaker of singular taste and persuasiveness — brought social distinction to the cause. The Church Missionary Society founded by Charles Simeon and John Venn in 1799 for the evangelisation of Africa and the East. The abolition of the slave trade (1807) and the emancipation of slaves (1833) as the zenith of Evangelical renown. The Clapham Sect: Wilberforce, Thornton, Zachary Macaulay, and their circle.

Defects of Evangelicalism, 1800–1845 — For the first half of the nineteenth century Evangelicalism was the strongest power in the Church. To be religious meant to forswear dancing and the theatre, to keep Sunday strictly, to read Venn’s Complete Duty of Man or Wilberforce’s Practical View, and to be interested in foreign missions. But no sooner had Evangelicalism become popular than it ceased to produce great men. Remarkably few men of mark on the episcopate from the beginning of the century until Samuel Wilberforce’s consecration in 1845. Few abuses remedied until the Parliament of 1832 forced reform. The Evangelicals could not produce ecclesiastical leadership or organising power. Charles Simeon exercised magnetic individual influence but the party never attempted to grapple with the problems of industrial towns. Under their rule dissent increased alarmingly and the Church stood still. Their best men devoted themselves to mission work or proprietary chapels in fashionable watering-places. Wakeman’s judgment: they failed to lead the Church just when it most wanted leadership, not from incapacity for exertion but from ignorance of how to use their power.

The Church’s condition on the eve of the Oxford Movement — As the nineteenth century grew to adolescence, new ideas rushed in from all sides — politics, philosophy, science, art — while the Church of England remained “inert and lethargic, and, as men thought, dying, waiting for the trumpet tongue which might yet wake her from her sleep.”

Chapter XX — The Oxford Movement, 1833–1896

State of the Church, 1820–1833 — When George III died in 1820 the Church was not materially different from what she had been in 1760. The intense piety of the Evangelical revival had never succeeded in leavening the main body of English Churchmanship. The bishops were amiable scholars living in dignified ease, attending the king’s levee, voting steadily in Parliament for the minister who appointed them, occasionally driving through muddy country roads to hold a confirmation. Spiritual leadership was almost unknown. Church patronage in the hands of the landowning class was used to provide for younger sons. Faculty pews and rented sittings absorbed the best parts of churches, the poor edged out into corners. Non-residence and pluralities flourished undiminished. Ecclesiastical courts were bywords for delay. Convocation had not been permitted to transact business since 1717. Cathedrals were treated as interesting museums or picturesque survivals, some actually in ruins. In most parish churches the doors were open only four hours in every seven days. The Holy Communion had become metamorphosed by slovenly customs — the prominent position of the pulpit, ill-kept chancels, mean and dirty altars, all pointing to the inferior position of the sacraments compared to the sermon.

Character of the clergy — Some hunted, shot, fished and drank during the week and mumbled through perfunctory services on Sundays. In remote districts drunkenness among the clergy was not uncommon. But such men were not the majority. The bulk of the English clergy were educated, refined, generous, God-fearing men, living lives of simple piety, respected for friendly help and wise counsel. They had no high standard of clerical duty, an exaggerated fear of fanaticism, and a holy horror of cant. Their doctrinal position was Low Church: they never thought of claiming priestly powers of absolution and sacrifice. Their Eucharistic doctrine did not rise above receptionist views taught by Calvin. They considered Rome to be Antichrist. The fathers and schoolmen were put aside for Luther, Calvin, Scott and Milner. A few remaining High Churchmen — the true spiritual descendants of Laud, Ken, and Wilson — believed in the Divine institution of the Church and the Real Presence, but they were scholars not men of action, their influence hardly extending beyond their own circles.

Helplessness of the Church in the face of attack, 1830 — The sole remaining vigorous principle was the strenuous maintenance of the alliance with the State. When the Test and Corporation Acts were repealed, when Roman Catholics were permitted to enter Parliament, when the Reform Bill was imminent and political power was to pass to the middle classes who formed the backbone of dissent — the Church “could apparently do nothing to meet the crisis.” The industrial revolution, the vast colonial empire, the religious revolution that had torn away the followers of Whitefield and Wesley — through all this the Church reared “her impassive front among the storms which raged round her, sublime in her apathy, unchanged and apparently unchangeable.”

Prospects of a revival — Wakeman poses the question from Ezekiel: “Could these dry bones live?” The answer: nothing short of the Divine command could make the scattered remnants fit themselves together again.

Dr. Arnold’s scheme for a national Church — Arnold of Rugby brought an intensely religious nature and commanding personality to the task of reform. His disagreement with popular Protestantism: he attached great importance to the training of character and distrusted religious feelings and experiences. He believed the greatness of England depended on the religious character of its people, and demanded a national Church coterminous with the nation. But he expressly repudiated the theory of a Divine society founded by Christ. The priestly side of Church teaching — apostolical succession, the Eucharistic sacrifice, the power of absolution — he considered corrupt superstition. His scheme: exclude from formularies everything except central doctrines and permit any form of worship. It was insular, unworkable, and fell still-born. Yet his disciples went forth determined to bring consecration into every act of daily life, distinguished by high moral standards of work and duty. The Church owes him an “undying debt of gratitude” for reviving strong religious principle in days of apathy.

The Tractarians — While Arnold sought regeneration through an Erastian establishment, men at Oxford were meeting the crisis on opposite principles. In Oriel College common room gathered John Keble, John Henry Newman, Richard Hurrell Froude, and eventually Edward Bouverie Pusey.

John Keble — The acknowledged leader at the beginning of the movement. Brought up a High Churchman of the school of Andrewes and Ken. A prodigy: scholarship at Corpus at fifteen, double first at eighteen, fellowship at Oriel, the English and Latin essay before nineteen. A singular sweetness of disposition, real humility, boyish zest for enjoyment, the quick imagination of a poet. His Christian Year (1827) — instinct with the Catholic spirit, so carefully written that only one word required alteration in the light of subsequent controversy — prepared the ground for the Tracts for the Times. His great sermon on National Apostasy (July 14, 1833) at the Oxford assizes sounded the first note of public alarm.

Reasons for action, 1833 — The Reform Bill of 1832 and the advent of a Parliament eager to reform the Church from a utilitarian standpoint. The prime minister warning bishops to set their house in order. A bill for the suppression of ten Irish bishoprics by the civil power alone. “Erastian utilitarianism” rampant in political and journalistic circles. The Church authorities had no policy, no principles, no leadership. Nothing less was wanted than to teach Englishmen from the beginning the real character of the Church. A meeting at Hugh James Rose’s house at Hadleigh resulted in an address to Archbishop Howley expressing adherence to apostolical doctrine and polity, signed by 7,000 clergy and 230,000 heads of families.

The Tracts for the Times, 1833–1840 — The first tract appeared in September 1833, short papers dealing with definite points of Church doctrine and practice. The leading idea: to justify the sacramental side of the Church’s life. The revival of the nineteenth century was to be anti-individualistic, based on the Incarnate Life of our Lord continued through the Church and Sacraments. The power given to all mankind to become the sons of God through His spouse the Church was the leading principle of the revival. The tracts brought men face to face with questions long forgotten: “What is the Church? Has she divine credentials? Has she inestimable privileges at her disposal?”

Accession of Dr. Pusey, 1834 — Pusey, Professor of Hebrew at Oxford and Canon of Christ Church, “the most venerated man in Oxford,” threw the weight of his profound learning into the scale. A character lofty in its ideals and stern even to asceticism. At a university “real profundity of learning and strength of character will always meet with respectful recognition.” Forty-six tracts published in 1833 and 1834. The collected volume with Newman’s introduction spread the doctrines among all the parsonages in England. Success among younger men at Oxford was most marked. Newman’s afternoon sermons at St. Mary’s — severe in tone, searching in thought, insisting upon conduct as the evidence of faith — made the movement recognised as a great effort after moral perfection.

The Hampden controversy, 1835 — The ill-judged appointment of Dr. Hampden as Regius Professor of Divinity united the university against the government. Hampden had laid down principles in his Bampton Lectures that seemed subversive of the Christian faith. The Tractarians, as the most earnest and able of Oxford theologians, appeared as leaders of a united university in protest.

Leadership of Newman, 1835–1841 — Insensibly Newman stood out as the real leader. It was round his personality that the younger men gathered. His quick intellect gauged the trend of thought and saw what positions needed fortifying.

The Roman controversy and the Via Media, 1836 — Newman was obliged to deal with the Roman controversy. The claim of the Church of England to be the true Catholic Church was met by the more imposing claims of Rome. Newman’s Lectures on Romanism and Popular Protestantism enunciated the theory of the Via Media: the Church has lost its unity of government but below the surface there is real unity — a unity of faith in the creeds, of liturgical worship, of apostolic episcopate. The surface is broken into three great pieces owing allegiance to Rome, Constantinople, and Canterbury. The duty of English Churchmen is to vindicate the true character of their Church, trusting that her central position between Rome and Protestantism, especially with the world-wide extension of the Anglo-Saxon race, might make her “the pivot round which the ultimate reunion of Christians would turn.” At the time (1837) the theory met the strongest opposition from the popular religion of the day. In 1839, while studying the Monophysite question, a “terrible doubt” came to Newman: was the parallel between the Monophysite and Anglican positions too close to be agreeable? He recovered his equilibrium and prepared Tract XC.

Tract XC and the storm, 1841–1845 — The publication of Tract XC was the turning-point. Newman subjected the Thirty-Nine Articles to a severe analysis to ascertain the irreducible minimum of statement against Roman doctrine to which subscribers could be legally bound. His argument: no Catholic doctrine, and hardly any theological Roman doctrine, was condemned by the Articles — only popular exaggerations current when the Articles were drawn up. All Protestantism flew to arms. The Heads of Houses condemned the tract as a dishonest evasion. The issue of the tracts was stopped at the request of the Bishop of Oxford. Bishop after bishop condemned them in their charges. Newman bowed to the storm and withdrew to Littlemore.

The Roman party — W. G. Ward of Balliol, combining extraordinary dialectical skill and love of paradox with boisterous capacity for fun, threw himself into the fray with more zeal than discretion. He boldly claimed the right to hold all Roman doctrine and remain in the Church of England. He became the centre of Faber, Dalgairns, Morris, Oakeley — men with little affection for the Church of England, powerfully attracted by the logical system of Rome. A rift appeared: Ward’s party drifting Romewards, Keble and Pusey’s remaining firmly Anglican.

Newman’s secession, 1845 — The doubt from the Donatist controversy returned and did not pass away. The Jerusalem bishopric scheme (1841) — a joint bishopric with Prussian Lutherans — seemed to demonstrate hopeless want of principle. Pusey’s unjust suspension for preaching a sermon on the Eucharist (1843). Ward’s condemnation by the university (1844). The knowledge that only the veto of the proctors prevented Newman’s own condemnation. In the summer of 1845 Ward, Dalgairns, Faber, Oakeley, and Christie seceded. In October the long-impending blow fell: “Newman was lost to a communion, the vitality and truth of which he had done more than any man to prove.”

The widening of the movement into a general High Church revival, 1845–1865 — The catastrophe of 1845 shattered the Tractarian party but did not stop the Oxford movement. The staunch fidelity of Pusey, Keble, Isaac Williams, J. B. Mozley, and Dean Church reassured the timid. The movement changed character: it lost its intellectual and academical stamp, ceased to be concentrated round a few persons at Oxford, became less argumentative and more occupied with practical Church work and the training of souls. Men like Dr. Hook at Leeds, Gladstone in Parliament, Roundell Palmer at the bar, Justice Coleridge on the bench, though not wholly accepting all Tractarian teaching, threw themselves into the work of spreading its principles. The Guardian newspaper was started in 1846. Samuel Wilberforce as Bishop of Oxford began to organise his diocese and set an example of episcopal activity possible only in one who believed strongly in episcopacy and the Church. Churches were cleaned, services multiplied and made brighter with music. The Eucharist was celebrated more frequently and with greater reverence. Devotional meetings for clergy were held. New parishes were formed, new churches built and endowed. The rising appreciation of Gothic art was pressed into the service of the Church.

Church reform and the Ecclesiastical Commission, 1836–1850 — The Church Discipline Act of 1840 introduced simpler procedure into ecclesiastical courts. The Ecclesiastical Commission of 1836 fixed and equalised episcopal and capitular incomes, freeing funds for new parishes and poor benefices. Legal obstacles to colonial bishoprics were removed: Selwyn to New Zealand (1841), Gray to South Africa (1847), Broughton to Australia (1836). In 1850 Convocation was finally permitted to meet regularly — the wrong of 1717 redressed.

The Gorham case, 1850 — The Privy Council’s decision in the Gorham case was understood to declare that the Church of England did not teach baptismal regeneration. A fresh batch of secessions followed, including that of Archdeacon Manning. Wakeman’s analysis: the court had not gone that length, and if it had, it was only a parliamentary court with no authority to bind conscience in matters of faith. The trend of affairs more than the facts of the case brought about crucial decisions.

Difficulties of the High Church revival — Englishmen became possessed with the belief that every attempt to raise the Church above customary tradition was a step towards popery. High Churchmen lived in an atmosphere of suspicion. The revival of carved figures, the symbol of the cross in decoration, coloured altar frontals — all denounced as disloyal. The wearing of the surplice in the pulpit instead of a black gown caused disgraceful riots in different parts of the country.

The Eucharistic controversy, 1856–1871 — Opposition centred upon efforts to surround the Holy Communion with dignity and splendour. The doctrine of the Real Presence had never died out but had ceased to influence official theology. Dr. Pusey devoted his massive learning to its vindication. In 1856 proceedings against Archdeacon Denison of Taunton for two sermons on the subject; the condemnation was set aside as irregular. In 1871 the Rev. W. J. E. Bennett was attacked for unguarded language on the Real Presence; both the ecclesiastical court and the Privy Council found his language not punishable as inconsistent with the formularies.

The Ritualist prosecutions, 1850s–1890s — After the Bennett decision, it became clear that prosecuting High Churchmen for heresy was useless; it was easier to punish them as law-breakers for ceremonial. The ornaments rubric was invoked to justify Eucharistic vestments and other ceremonial. Bishops, mostly Evangelicals or Latitudinarians, declared war on ceremonial. A series of Privy Council decisions in every point adverse to the High Church party followed. Many decisions were based on historical blunders later exposed by scholarship. Archbishop Tait’s Public Worship Regulation Act (1874) established a new parliamentary court. Conscientious clergy refused to acknowledge it. Five were actually imprisoned. The scandal of sending clergy to prison for refusing to acknowledge the State’s right to enforce decisions in matters of faith became unbearable. Tait himself saw his mistake; on his deathbed in 1882 he left a legacy of peace and toleration. The Lincoln Judgment (1892) reconsidered and partially overruled earlier decisions. Since then no fresh ritual prosecution occurred.

The Church’s defence of the faith — The Church’s response to mid-century intellectual challenges: the scientific spirit, the critical study of other religions, the higher criticism of the Bible, the utilitarian spirit. The Cambridge school of biblical scholars and the Oxford school of historians provided the defence. Wakeman’s conclusion: future investigation is unlikely to impair the conclusions of the Cambridge school on the authenticity of early Christian documents, or the evidence for the Church’s regenerating influence brought into prominence by the Oxford historians.

Estimate of the High Church revival — The High Church revival brought to English religious life “largeness of view.” It sought to take the whole of man — intellectual, moral, aesthetic, social, as well as spiritual and humanitarian. It restored the true conception of the Church, laid the basis of Church order on historical fact, claimed the witness of the ages for its defence. It welcomed knowledge from whatever quarter as a further revelation of the mind of God. It consecrated the beautiful and ennobling in the arts. Wakeman’s historical judgment: the Church revival of the nineteenth century is seen to be “nothing more than the complete reaction against the Protestant movement of the sixteenth century” — a reaction that began with Hooker and Bancroft, was developed by Andrewes and Laud and Jeremy Taylor, and reached its full expression in the Oxford Movement.
