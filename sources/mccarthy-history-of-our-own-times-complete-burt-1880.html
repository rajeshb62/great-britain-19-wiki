A History of Our Own Times

Justin McCarthy’s A History of Our Own Times is a political and public narrative of the reign of Queen Victoria from the accession in 1837 to the general election of 1880. This one-volume Burt edition is useful for the 1800–1865 wiki because the first forty-seven chapters carry the story from 1837 through the death of Lord Palmerston in 1865.

For the period requested — from the close of the Crimean War to 1865 — the especially relevant chapters are XXX–XLVII. They cover the Second China War, transportation, the Indian Mutiny and the end of Company rule, the Orsini crisis, the Derby ministry, the Ionian Islands, the French commercial treaty and paper duties, Eastern questions, the American Civil War, the Alabama, Palmerston’s last electoral victory, the ebb and flow of parties, and Palmerston’s death.

Sources

Justin McCarthy, A History of Our Own Times: From the Accession of Queen Victoria to the General Election of 1880. New edition with an appendix of events to the end of 1880. New York: A. L. Burt, [1880s]. OCLC 843529.

Coverage

  • Chronological scope of the book: 1837–1880, with an appendix to the end of 1880.
  • Chronological scope relevant to this wiki: chapters I–XLVII, 1837–1865.
  • Post-Crimean-to-1865 coverage: chapters XXX–XLVII, beginning after the Congress of Paris and ending with Palmerston’s death.
  • Later chapters: chapters XLVIII–LXVII continue beyond the folder’s terminal date and belong chiefly to the 1865–1919 continuation, but are included in the raw source for context.

Why this source matters for the 1800–1865 wiki

McCarthy gives a continuous Victorian narrative from the beginning of the reign to the end of the Palmerston era. For this folder he is especially useful as a bridge source: he begins after the Reform Act generation, follows the early Victorian crises of Chartism, Ireland, free trade, church controversy, and imperial war, and then carries the story across the Crimean War into the decade that ends in 1865.

The source is particularly strong for:

  • parliamentary and party politics: Melbourne, Peel, Russell, Palmerston, Derby, Disraeli, Gladstone, Cobden, Bright, and the shifting relation between Whigs, Peelites, Conservatives, radicals, and Liberals;
  • public agitation: Chartism, Repeal, Young Ireland, the Anti-Corn-Law League, the Ecclesiastical Titles agitation, and the reform issue before the Second Reform Act;
  • imperial crises: Canada, Afghanistan, China, India, the Ionian Islands, and the Mediterranean/Eastern questions;
  • mid-Victorian political culture: the Great Exhibition, the literature of the reign, public scandal, press and paper duties, education, divorce reform, and the language of national honour;
  • 1856–1865 specifically: the Arrow dispute, Indian Mutiny, end of Company rule, Orsini crisis, Cobden-Chevalier Treaty, American Civil War, Alabama, and Palmerston’s final ministry.

Detailed chapter breakdown — chapters I–XXIX, 1837–1856

Chapter I — The King is Dead! Long Live the Queen! (starts line 215)

  • Opens with William IV’s death at Windsor on June 20, 1837, and the summons sent to Kensington Palace for Victoria.
  • Establishes the transition from the old monarch to the young queen and uses the accession to introduce the political atmosphere of the new reign.
  • Useful for pages on Victoria’s accession, monarchy at the beginning of the reign, and the ceremonial/political opening of the Victorian period.

Chapter II — Statesmen and Parties (starts line 988)

  • Surveys the leading statesmen at Victoria’s accession, beginning with Lord Melbourne as first minister.
  • Introduces the party world of the late 1830s: Melbourne, Peel, Wellington, Brougham, O’Connell, and the parliamentary conditions inherited from the Reform Act era.
  • Useful for pages on early Victorian parties, Melbourne’s ministry, Peel’s opposition, and the political inheritance of 1832.

Chapter III — Canada and Lord Durham (starts line 1687)

  • Treats the Canadian rebellion as the first major disturbance of the new reign.
  • Covers Lord Durham’s mission and the constitutional questions raised by colonial unrest in British North America.
  • Useful for pages on the Canadian rebellions, Durham Report, responsible government, colonial reform, and empire after 1837.

Chapter IV — Science and Speed (starts line 2740)

  • Places the opening of Victoria’s reign alongside railways, steam, industrial invention, and new applications of science and commerce.
  • Presents technological acceleration as part of the public character of the age, not merely a background condition.
  • Useful for pages on railways, steam power, industrial modernity, and the social perception of speed in early Victorian Britain.

Chapter V — Chartism (starts line 3309)

  • Moves from technological optimism to domestic unrest and working-class political agitation.
  • Covers the People’s Charter, the discontents behind Chartism, and the early Victorian fear that political reform had not settled the social question.
  • Useful for pages on Chartism, working-class politics, suffrage demands, and social pressure in the late 1830s and early 1840s.

Chapter VI — Question de Jupons (starts line 4156)

  • Covers the Bedchamber crisis and the difficulty of transferring government under a young queen whose household appointments had political meaning.
  • Shows Sir Robert Peel rebuilding Conservative strength while the Melbourne ministry weakened.
  • Useful for pages on the queen’s household, monarchy and party politics, Peel’s first attempt at office, and court politics in 1839.

Chapter VII — The Queen’s Marriage (starts line 4631)

  • Covers Victoria’s announcement of her marriage to Prince Albert and the parliamentary/public discussion surrounding it.
  • Introduces Albert’s position, public reception, and the place of the royal marriage in the early reign.
  • Useful for pages on Prince Albert, royal domesticity, the monarchy’s public image, and court culture.

Chapter VIII — The Opium War (starts line 5290)

  • Narrates the China opium dispute and the outbreak of the Opium War soon after Victoria’s accession.
  • Places trade, treaty rights, coercion, and British imperial policy in China at the centre of an early Victorian foreign-policy crisis.
  • Useful for pages on the Opium War, China trade, imperial coercion, and British treaty-port expansion.

Chapter IX — Decline and Fall of the Whig Ministry (starts line 5878)

  • Follows the weakening of Melbourne’s Whig ministry as public agitation and political pressure mounted around it.
  • Covers the circumstances leading to the return of Peel and the end of Melbourne’s government.
  • Useful for pages on Melbourne, Whig decline, Peel’s accession to office, and early Victorian ministerial instability.

Chapter X — Movements in the Churches (starts line 6555)

  • Surveys church controversies in the early reign, beginning with Oxford and the censure of religious teaching associated with the Tractarian movement.
  • Covers ecclesiastical agitation, religious controversy, and the political importance of church questions.
  • Useful for pages on the Oxford Movement, Tractarianism, church politics, religious controversy, and the Anglican establishment.

Chapter XI — The Disasters of Cabul (starts line 7139)

  • Treats the early Peel ministry’s foreign crisis in Afghanistan and the disaster at Kabul.
  • Covers the shock of imperial failure after the British retreat and destruction of the Kabul force.
  • Useful for pages on the First Anglo-Afghan War, Kabul retreat, imperial vulnerability, and the foreign-policy inheritance of Peel’s government.

Chapter XII — The Repeal Year (starts line 8625)

  • Centres on Daniel O’Connell and the declaration that 1843 would be the great year for repeal of the Union.
  • Discusses O’Connell’s character, mass meetings, Irish political mobilization, and the British government’s response.
  • Useful for pages on Repeal agitation, O’Connell, Irish nationalism, monster meetings, and the politics of Union.

Chapter XIII — Peel’s Administration (starts line 9605)

  • Surveys important domestic measures under Peel, including social legislation and administrative reform.
  • Uses measures such as restrictions on women and girls in mines to show Peel’s government as more than a Corn Law ministry.
  • Useful for pages on Peel’s domestic administration, early factory/mines legislation, fiscal policy, and Conservative governance.

Chapter XIV — Free Trade and the League (starts line 10246)

  • Covers the Anti-Corn-Law League and the intensifying campaign for free trade.
  • Frames the Corn Law issue as a central political controversy of Peel’s administration and of the 1840s.
  • Useful for pages on Cobden, Bright, the Anti-Corn-Law League, free trade, political economy, and manufacturing interests.

Chapter XV — Famine Forces Peel’s Hand (starts line 11383)

  • Connects the Irish famine to Peel’s decision to move toward Corn Law repeal.
  • Shows the pressure created when humanitarian emergency, food supply, and political economy converged.
  • Useful for pages on the Irish famine, Corn Law repeal, Peel’s break with Protection, and the fall of his ministry.

Chapter XVI — Mr. Disraeli (starts line 12115)

  • Introduces Disraeli as the speaker who rose suddenly into prominence during the anti-Peel revolt.
  • Covers his parliamentary style, personal distinctiveness, and position as a leader of opposition to Peel.
  • Useful for pages on Disraeli, Young England, anti-Peel Conservatism, and parliamentary rhetoric.

Chapter XVII — Famine, Commercial Trouble, and Foreign Intrigue (starts line 13043)

  • Covers the Russell ministry after Peel: Russell at the Treasury, Palmerston at the Foreign Office, Charles Wood at the Exchequer, Grey at the Colonies, and George Grey at the Home Office.
  • Places famine, economic difficulty, and foreign affairs together as the new government’s inheritance.
  • Useful for pages on Russell’s first ministry, famine administration, commercial crisis, and Palmerston’s return to the Foreign Office.

Chapter XVIII — Chartism and Young Ireland (starts line 13782)

  • Treats 1848 as the year of European revolutions and follows its British and Irish consequences.
  • Covers the Chartist demonstration, renewed working-class agitation, and the Young Ireland movement.
  • Useful for pages on 1848 in Britain, Chartism’s last great demonstration, Young Ireland, revolutionary fear, and public order.

Chapter XIX — Don Pacifico (starts line 15056)

  • Covers the Don Pacifico affair and the foreign-policy crisis around Palmerston’s defence of British subjects abroad.
  • Leads to parliamentary confrontation and Palmerston’s famous justification of his policy.
  • Useful for pages on Don Pacifico, Palmerstonian foreign policy, gunboat diplomacy, and the language of British protection.

Chapter XX — The Ecclesiastical Titles Bill (starts line 16101)

  • Covers the anti-Catholic agitation after the restoration of a Roman Catholic hierarchy in England.
  • Explains the Ecclesiastical Titles Bill and the strange intensity of religious-political alarm in 1850–1851.
  • Useful for pages on papal aggression, anti-Catholic politics, Russell’s ministry, and religious liberty in mid-Victorian Britain.

Chapter XXI — The Exhibition in Hyde Park (starts line 16996)

  • Treats the Great Exhibition of 1851 as a public landmark of the reign.
  • Covers the Crystal Palace, international display, industrial achievement, and the association of the Exhibition with Prince Albert.
  • Useful for pages on the Great Exhibition, Prince Albert, industrial culture, free trade display, and mid-century optimism.

Chapter XXII — Palmerston (starts line 17590)

  • Gives McCarthy’s extended character treatment of Lord Palmerston after Peel’s death left him one of the most prominent statesmen in Britain.
  • Covers Palmerston’s self-asserting foreign-policy style and his position within and beyond the cabinet.
  • Useful for pages on Palmerston, foreign-policy independence, ministerial discipline, and mid-Victorian liberal nationalism.

Chapter XXIII — Birth of the Empire; Death of “The Duke” (starts line 18947)

  • Places 1852 in a wider imperial and emotional setting, with continental events and British reactions shaping politics at home.
  • Includes the death of the Duke of Wellington and the public meaning attached to his passing.
  • Useful for pages on Wellington’s death, imperial expansion, 1852 politics, and the memory of the Napoleonic generation.

Chapter XXIV — Mr. Gladstone (starts line 20114)

  • Centres on Gladstone’s emergence as a major parliamentary and financial figure.
  • Covers the rivalry and contrast between Gladstone and Disraeli in debate.
  • Useful for pages on Gladstone, the 1852 budget debates, Peelites, finance, and parliamentary oratory.

Chapter XXV — The Eastern Question (starts line 20584)

  • Begins from Britain’s long peace and moves into the diplomatic crisis around Turkey, Russia, and the Eastern Question.
  • Covers the road toward the Crimean conflict and the assumptions with which Britain approached it.
  • Useful for pages on the Eastern Question, Ottoman Empire, Russia, diplomacy before Crimea, and Britain’s return to European war.

Chapter XXVI — Where was Lord Palmerston? (starts line 21941)

  • Asks how Palmerston positioned himself as the Eastern crisis developed.
  • Covers cabinet tensions, Palmerston’s warlike expectations, and the political meaning of his temporary resignation and return.
  • Useful for pages on Palmerston and Crimea, Aberdeen’s coalition, cabinet management, and the politics of war.

Chapter XXVII — The Invasion of the Crimea (starts line 23049)

  • Covers Britain and France entering the war as allies and the appointment of Lord Raglan and Marshal St. Arnaud.
  • Narrates the expedition to Crimea and the early military conduct of the war.
  • Useful for pages on the Crimean War, Raglan, St. Arnaud, allied command, expeditionary war, and British military administration.

Chapter XXVIII — The Close of the War (starts line 23996)

  • Opens with Palmerston’s unexpected arrival at the premiership in 1855.
  • Covers the last phase of the Crimean War, political pressure over mismanagement, and the movement toward peace.
  • Useful for pages on Palmerston’s first ministry, war administration, the fall of Aberdeen, and the Congress/Treaty of Paris.

Chapter XXIX — The Literature of the Reign: First Survey (starts line 24914)

  • Treats the close of the Crimean War as a major landmark in Victoria’s reign and pauses for a literary survey.
  • Reviews the literary achievements of the reign up to the Crimean-War divide.
  • Useful for pages on Victorian literature, public culture, and McCarthy’s own sense of periodization at 1856.

Detailed chapter breakdown — chapters XXX–XLVII, 1856–1865

Chapter XXX — The Lorcha Arrow (starts line 26615)

  • Opens after the supposed settlement of the Eastern Question at the Congress of Paris and shows a post-Crimean lull giving way to new conflicts.
  • Covers Russell’s national-education proposal, the Wensleydale life-peerage controversy, public frauds and poisoning trials, Cyrus Field’s Atlantic telegraph project, Lesseps’s Suez Canal proposal, and the Arrow dispute at Canton.
  • Follows the seizure of men from the lorcha Arrow, the expired British registration, Consul Parkes, Sir John Bowring, Yeh, the bombardment of Canton, Cobden’s censure motion, Palmerston’s dissolution, and the election victory of 1857.
  • Useful for pages on the Second China War, Palmerstonian foreign policy, Cobden and Bright, parliamentary censure, national honour rhetoric, and the politics of empire after Crimea.

Chapter XXXI — Transportation (starts line 27371)

  • Treats 1857 as the year in which penal transportation effectively ended.
  • Gives the background of transportation from earlier penal policy to the Australian colonies and explains settler resistance to continued convict arrivals.
  • Useful for pages on transportation, convict labour, Australian colonies, penal reform, and settler self-government.

Chapter XXXII — The Sepoy (starts line 27917)

  • Opens from the centenary of Plassey and introduces the Sepoy army as the immediate setting of the Indian Mutiny.
  • Explains the cartridge grievance, religious anxieties, military conditions, and the deeper fragility of Company rule.
  • Useful for pages on the Indian Mutiny, Bengal Army, cartridges, Company administration, and British assumptions about Indian loyalty.

Chapter XXXIII — The Hundredth Anniversary of Plassey (starts line 28657)

  • Follows the shock in Calcutta when news arrived from Meerut and Delhi.
  • Uses Plassey’s hundredth anniversary as a symbolic frame for the crisis of British rule.
  • Useful for pages on Meerut, Delhi, Plassey memory, panic in British India, and the symbolic chronology of empire.

Chapter XXXIV — Cawnpore (starts line 29090)

  • Centres on the events at Cawnpore and the appeals for aid that Sir Henry Lawrence at Lucknow could not answer.
  • Treats Nana Sahib and the massacres as episodes that shaped British public anger during the Mutiny.
  • Useful for pages on Cawnpore, Nana Sahib, atrocity narratives, Lucknow, and British revenge sentiment.

Chapter XXXV — Reconquest (starts line 29693)

  • Begins with the capture of Delhi and follows the transition from imperial crisis to military reconquest.
  • Covers Delhi, Lucknow, relief operations, command decisions, and the reassertion of British control.
  • Useful for pages on Delhi, Lucknow, Havelock, Colin Campbell, siege warfare, and the military suppression of the uprising.

Chapter XXXVI — The End of “John Company” (starts line 30114)

  • Describes British agitation over India while the Mutiny unfolded and news reached England.
  • Covers the political end of East India Company rule and the transfer of India to the Crown.
  • Useful for pages on the Government of India Act, East India Company, Crown rule, India Office, and Victorian imperial administration.

Chapter XXXVII — The Orsini Bombs Explode in Paris and London (starts line 30752)

  • Explains how the Orsini attempt against Napoleon III produced a British political crisis.
  • Covers French pressure over refugees, Palmerston’s Conspiracy Bill, parliamentary resistance, and the fall of his ministry in 1858.
  • Useful for pages on political asylum, Orsini, Napoleon III, Anglo-French relations, and Palmerston’s fall.

Chapter XXXVIII — On the True Faith of a Christian (starts line 31585)

  • Covers Derby’s second ministry and Disraeli’s difficult position leading a minority government in the Commons.
  • Describes Conservative survival tactics, parliamentary alignments, and the religious-political language of the moment.
  • Useful for pages on Derby, Disraeli, minority government, Conservative politics, and the post-Palmerston parliamentary balance.

Chapter XXXIX — The Ionian Islands (starts line 32360)

  • Discusses British possession of the Ionian Islands and the case for union with Greece.
  • Presents the Ionian question as a test of imperial interest, Mediterranean strategy, and local national feeling.
  • Useful for pages on the Ionian Islands, Gladstone’s mission, cession to Greece, and liberal imperial policy.

Chapter XL — The Tory Diogenes Rolling his Tub (starts line 32767)

  • Uses the image of Diogenes to frame Conservative attempts to keep politically active by handling reform.
  • Covers Disraeli’s reform manoeuvres and the difficulties of a Conservative ministry attempting a reform settlement.
  • Useful for pages on parliamentary reform before 1867, Disraeli’s tactics, Derby’s government, and party competition over reform.

Chapter XLI — The French Treaty and the Paper Duties (starts line 33558)

  • Covers Palmerston’s return to office, Cobden’s commercial treaty with France, and Gladstone’s finance.
  • Follows the struggle over repeal of the paper duties and the constitutional conflict between Commons and Lords over taxation and finance.
  • Useful for pages on the Cobden-Chevalier Treaty, paper duties, Gladstone’s budgets, cheap press, and Commons financial privilege.

Chapter XLII — Troubles in the East (starts line 34648)

  • Opens with the Queen’s Speech of January 1860 and renewed disturbances in China after the Treaty of Tientsin.
  • Covers the Peiho/Peking crisis and wider Eastern and Mediterranean diplomatic complications in Palmerston’s later ministry.
  • Useful for pages on the Second China War, Treaty of Tientsin, Peking, coercive diplomacy, and eastern policy after Crimea.

Chapter XLIII — The Civil War in America (starts line 35371)

  • Begins with the outbreak of the American Civil War after Lincoln’s election and Southern secession.
  • Explains British opinion toward Union and Confederacy, the cotton question, slavery, trade interests, and the official problem of neutrality.
  • Useful for pages on British neutrality, cotton famine background, slavery and secession in British debate, and Anglo-American relations.

Chapter XLIV — The Cruise of the Alabama (starts line 36111)

  • Covers the Confederate cruiser Alabama, British shipbuilding, neutrality law, and the controversy that later fed the Alabama claims.
  • Places the dispute in the wider Anglo-American tension of the Civil War years and links it to Prince Albert’s final counsel for calm in the Trent crisis atmosphere.
  • Useful for pages on the Alabama, Laird rams, Confederate commerce raiding, neutrality, and Anglo-American diplomacy.

Chapter XLV — Palmerston’s Last Victory (starts line 37174)

  • Opens with the 1862 International Exhibition after Prince Albert’s death.
  • Covers Palmerston’s late political strength, public popularity, and last electoral/parliamentary success.
  • Useful for pages on Palmerston’s final ministry, the 1865 election, exhibition culture, late Palmerstonian liberalism, and public confidence in the old premier.

Chapter XLVI — Ebb and Flow (starts line 38592)

  • Describes English politics as a recurring movement of action and reaction, with reform energy followed by fatigue and renewed quiet followed by agitation.
  • Covers the shifting movement of party feeling in the last Palmerston years.
  • Useful for pages on Liberal cohesion, Conservative recovery, reform fatigue before 1866–1867, and the atmosphere before the post-Palmerston transition.

Chapter XLVII — The Death of Lord Palmerston (starts line 39569)

  • Closes the pre-1865 sequence with Palmerston’s death and presents it as the end of a political era.
  • Connects Palmerston’s passing to the coming change in Liberal leadership and the renewed reform conflicts after 1865.
  • Useful for pages on Palmerston, the 1865 transition, Russell and Gladstone, and the end of mid-Victorian Palmerstonian government.
