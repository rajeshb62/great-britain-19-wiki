The History of England from the Year 1830, Vols. I & II

The History of England from the Year 1830, Vols. I & II

Jan 01, 197016 min read

The History of England from the Year 1830, Vols. I & II — by William Nassau Molesworth

Vols. I & II cover the period 1830–1854. Molesworth was a firsthand witness to much of what he records — he sat in the House of Commons as MP for various constituencies from 1853 onwards, and his History of the Reform Bill of 1832 is incorporated into the early chapters. He writes from a Liberal standpoint, sympathetic to the progressive causes of the age — parliamentary reform, free trade, religious liberty, colonial self-government — but with a genuine effort at fairness to his opponents.

The work is structured episodically: each chapter follows a year-by-year (or session-by-session) chronicle, weaving together parliamentary proceedings, foreign affairs, social movements, literary and scientific developments, and the condition of the people. Molesworth is particularly strong on economic legislation, fiscal policy, and the working of the reformed parliament.

Volume I — 1830–1837

Chapter I — Introductory

Molesworth opens with an extended survey of the unreformed constitution, tracing the history of parliamentary reform agitation from the Long Parliament and Cromwell through to the revival of the question in the eighteenth century. He reviews the elder Pitt’s reform proposals, the Yorkshire Association movement under the younger Pitt, the influence of the French Revolution on English radicalism, and the repressive legislation of the 1790s. The chapter covers the long Tory ascendancy under Liverpool, the Six Acts, the Queen Caroline affair, the liberal Tory reforms of Huskisson and Canning, and the crisis over Catholic Emancipation — the Clare election of 1828, O’Connell’s organising of the Catholic Association, the Wellington ministry’s reluctant concession of emancipation in 1829. The chapter closes with the agricultural and commercial distress of 1829–30, the July Revolution in France, and the general election of 1830 that brought the reform question to a head.

Chapter II — First Introduction of the Reform Bill

The resignation of the Duke of Wellington’s ministry (November 1830) after his declaration against any reform, and the formation of Earl Grey’s reform administration — the first genuinely Whig government in over forty years. Molesworth details the composition of the cabinet (Brougham as Lord Chancellor, Lord John Russell as Paymaster of the Forces, Lord Durham, Althorp, Melbourne, Lansdowne, Palmerston, Graham, Richmond). The chapter covers the “Captain Swing” disturbances — the machine-breaking and rick-burning that swept the southern and eastern counties — and the special commissions that tried the rioters. Parliament reassembles in February 1831; Lord John Russell rises on 1 March to introduce the Reform Bill, a speech Molesworth describes as “one of the most remarkable ever delivered in the House of Commons.” The great Commons debate that followed — the speeches of Sir Robert Inglis, Joseph Hume, Hunt, Wetherell, Palmerston, Peel, Stanley, Croker, O’Connell — occupies much of the narrative. Molesworth traces the reception of the Bill by the country: the Political Unions forming in Birmingham (under Attwood) and elsewhere, the press campaign, the public meetings, the addresses to the King, the petitions. The Bill passes its second reading by a single vote (302 to 301) — the famous “strangers’ gallery” night — only to be defeated in committee on General Gascoigne’s wrecking amendment.

Chapter III — Second Introduction of the Reform Bill

The dissolution of Parliament (22 April 1831) by royal proclamation — a dramatic gesture that sent members back to their constituencies with the Reform Bill as the sole question of the general election. Molesworth traces the election of 1831, the return of a great reform majority, and the re-introduction of the Bill by Lord John Russell. He covers the anomalies of the old system exposed by the debate — nomination boroughs (Old Sarum, Gatton), the “Pocket-Borough” system, the electoral influence of the aristocracy — and Alderman Thompson’s notorious “inadvertence” in mistaking which lobby he had voted in. The Bill passes the Commons through all its stages, aided by the Speaker’s casting vote. The coronation of William IV (September 1831) provides a brief interlude of pageantry before the Bill is carried up to the Lords.

Chapter IV — The Reform Bill Carried

The Lords’ rejection of the Bill (8 October 1831) by a majority of 41 — the Bishops voting almost solidly against. Molesworth gives full weight to the popular fury that followed: the Bristol riots (the burning of the Mansion House, the Bishop’s Palace, the Customs House), the breaking of windows of anti-reform peers, the attacks on the Duke of Newcastle’s property at Newark, the grim determination of the Political Unions to march on London if necessary. Lord Ebrington’s resolution of confidence in the ministry carries the Commons. The “Days of May” crisis — the Bill’s third introduction, Lord Lyndhurst’s motion to postpone the disfranchising clauses, the resignation of the Grey ministry, the Duke of Wellington’s unsuccessful attempt to form a government (his “d——d nasty question” of the Speakership), the refusal of Peel to serve with him, and the recall of Grey. The King’s reluctant promise to create enough peers to carry the Bill, the mass abstention of the Tory peers, the passage of the Bill through the Lords, and the royal assent (7 June 1832). Molesworth closes the chapter with a survey of periodical literature of the age and a notice of the death of Sir Walter Scott.

Chapter V — The First Reformed Parliament

The general election of 1832 under the new franchise — nearly 300 seats uncontested, the Conservative (now so called) party reduced to about 150 members, the reformers triumphant. Molesworth devotes careful attention to the first reformed Parliament’s legislative agenda: the Coercion Bill for Ireland, the Irish Church question (the appropriation controversy that would eventually split the Whig party), the Irish Tithe Bill, and the great financial measures of the government — the reduction of the malt-tax and house- and window-duties, the renewal of the Bank Charter, the renewal of the East India Company’s charter. The abolition of slavery throughout the British colonies (the Act of 1833, the £20 million compensation to slave-owners, the apprenticeship system). The Factory Act of 1833 (Lord Althorp’s bill, the limitation of children’s hours, the factory inspectorate). The debate over the ballot, triennial parliaments, bribery and corruption at elections. The session of 1834 — the dismissal of Lord Melbourne by William IV (the King’s resentment over the Irish Church appropriation clause), Peel’s brief first ministry (the “Hundred Days”), the Tamworth Manifesto, the destruction of the two Houses of Parliament by fire (16 October 1834). The chapter closes with the death of Coleridge.

Chapter VI — Corporation Reform

The general election of 1835 — Peel’s appeal to the country fails to produce a Conservative majority, the “Lichfield House compact” between the Whigs and O’Connell’s Irish Repealers, the defeat of Peel’s ministry on the election of the Speaker and again on the corporation reform question, Peel’s resignation, and Melbourne’s return to office. The Municipal Corporations Reform Bill — Lord John Russell’s speech, Sir William Follett’s wrecking motion, the Bill in the Lords, the introduction of the ratepaying franchise for local government. The Irish Church tithe question continues. The Orange Lodges controversy — Joseph Hume’s disclosures, the Commons address against them, the Duke of Cumberland’s role. The Budget of 1836, the registration of births, marriages, and deaths, the reduction of the newspaper stamp duty from 4d. to 1d., the extension of counsel’s right to address the jury in felony cases (the Prisoners’ Counsel Act). The New Poor Law of 1834 (the Poor Law Commission, the workhouse system, the Andover workhouse scandal). The Canadian question — Lord Durham’s mission, the Rebellion Losses Bill. British intervention in Spain (the Quadruple Alliance, the Carlist War). The Westminster election of 1837, and the death of William IV — Molesworth gives a character sketch of the King, his constitutional role in the Reform crisis, his bluff plainness, his conscientious if sometimes capricious exercise of the prerogative.

Volume II — 1837–1854

Chapter I — Victoria Regina

The accession of the young Queen Victoria (June 1837) — Molesworth records the universal goodwill and “almost romantic interest” that greeted the new reign. The dissolution and general election — Melbourne’s Whigs maintain their majority. The Canadian crisis deepens: Lord Durham’s mission, his ordinance, his repudiation, his resignation, his famous Report on the Affairs of British North America (1839) which laid the foundation for responsible colonial government. The Bedchamber Question (May 1839) — Peel’s demand for the dismissal of the Queen’s Whig ladies-in-waiting, the Queen’s refusal, Peel’s resignation, Melbourne’s return. Molesworth covers the social and administrative developments of the early reign: the introduction of penny postage (Rowland Hill’s plan, the Treasury competition for the penny black), the formation of the Committee of Privy Council on Education (the beginnings of state involvement in education), the stock of the British Museum, the trial of Courvoisier for the murder of Lord William Russell. Lord Durham’s death, the coronation (1838), the marriage of the Queen, the birth of the Princess Royal. The Eastern Question — Mehemet Ali’s revolt against the Porte, Lord Palmerston’s robust policy, the Straits Convention of 1841. The session of 1841 — the government’s Irish registration measure, the fixed duties on corn (the Whigs’ gradual conversion to free trade), the sugar duties debate, the timber duties question. The defeat of the government on a vote of no confidence, the general election, the return of Peel, the resumption and conclusion of the Eastern crisis.

Chapter II — The Income and Property Tax

Peel’s ministerial announcements — the resignation of the Duke of Buckingham from the cabinet. The great Corn Law debate of 1842: the fixed duty versus the sliding scale — Peel eventually carried his new sliding scale. The deficiency in the revenue — Peel’s plan to revive the income and property tax (abandoned since 1816) at 7d. in the £. Molesworth traces the debates at length, giving the arguments of all parties. The copyright question — Carlyle’s famous petition to the Commons, in which he described himself as “a writer of books.” The reform of the law of libel, the law of evidence, the Bankruptcy Act. The disaster in Afghanistan — the Army of the Indus, the occupation of Cabul, the rising of the tribes, the retreat from Cabul (January 1842), the destruction of the army at the Pass of Tezeen, the captivity of Lady Sale and the other hostages, Lord Ellenborough’s proclamation restoring the gates of Somnath. The employment of women and children in mines — Lord Ashley’s (later Lord Shaftesbury) revelations before the Mines Commission, the Mines and Collieries Act of 1842. Lord Ashley’s motion on the education of the poor, the government plan of education, Sir James Graham’s factory education clauses. The state of Ireland in 1843 — O’Connell’s monster meetings, the Repeal Association, the Clontarf proclamation. The Braintree Case and the Chartist agitation.

Chapter III — The Sliding Scale

The year 1844 — England at peace with all the world, the war in Scinde (Sir Charles Napier’s conquest, his laconic despatch “Peccavi”), the factory question — Lord Ashley’s Ten Hours Bill defeated in the Commons, the government’s alternative factory bill limiting children’s hours but leaving women’s labour unrestricted. Peel’s budget — the reduction of the tariff on 750 articles, the relaxation of the Corn Law sliding scale, the extension of the income tax to Scotland and Ireland. The Bank Charter Act of 1844 (Sir Robert Peel’s Act) — the separation of the issue and banking departments of the Bank of England, the limitation of the note issue, the beginnings of the modern central banking system. Lady Hewley’s charities — the Nonconformist question, the Dissenters’ Chapels Act. The railway mania — the railway legislation of 1844 (Gladstone’s Railway Act, the compulsory parliamentary train, the provisions for third-class passengers), the speculation and the approaching crash. Modifications of the bastardy clauses of the New Poor Law. The Rebecca riots in Wales (the destruction of toll-gates, the disguise of the rioters).The opening of letters at the Post Office — the Mazzini affair. The Irish crisis: O’Connell’s monster meetings, the indictment and trial of O’Connell, his conviction quashed by the House of Lords on a technical point of procedure. The Tahiti affair (the Pritchard incident, French intervention). The session of 1845 — Peel’s financial statement, the sugar duties debate, agricultural distress, the Maynooth Bill (the substantial increase of the grant to the Catholic seminary, the furious Protestant opposition, the passage of the bill), the “godless colleges” of the Irish universities scheme (Sir Robert Peel’s Colleges Bill). The Oregon boundary question with the United States, the potato disease (the first appearance of the blight), the resignation of Peel, the failure of Lord John Russell to form a government, and the resumption of office by Peel — the turning point that would lead to the repeal of the Corn Laws.

Chapter IV — The Anti-Corn-Law League

The rise of the Anti-Corn-Law League — its origins in Manchester in 1838, the founding of the Anti-Corn-Law Association, the role of Richard Cobden and John Bright, the fundraising of £100,000, the registration campaign (qualifying every freeholder in the manufacturing districts for a county vote), the propagation of free-trade doctrines through lectures, pamphlets, bazaars, and the Economist newspaper. The potato blight of 1845 devastates Ireland — the crisis that made repeal inevitable. Molesworth covers Peel’s recantation of agricultural protection (January 1846), Disraeli’s brilliant speeches rallying the Protectionists around Lord George Bentinck, the measure of compensation to the landed interest, the Irish Arms Bill, the defeat of the ministry on the Irish Coercion Bill, the resignation of Peel, his famous final speech, and the formation of Lord John Russell’s Whig ministry. The Sugar Duties Act, the continuing distress and crime in Ireland, the discovery of anaesthetics (ether and chloroform), the Secularist movement, legislation for Ireland, Fielden’s Ten Hours Factory Act (the limitation of the working day for women and young persons to ten hours), the educational vote, O’Connell’s last speech in the House of Commons, his death at Genoa on his way to Rome, the dissolution of the Anti-Corn-Law League, and the discovery of the planet Neptune.

Chapter V — The People’s Charter

The general election of 1847, the November session, Baron Lionel de Rothschild and the Jewish disabilities question — his election for the City of London and his repeated refusal to take the oath “on the true faith of a Christian,” the successive bills for the removal of Jewish disabilities, the long struggle between Lords and Commons. The suspension of habeas corpus in Ireland, the Encumbered Estates Act of 1849 (the sale of insolvent Irish estates, the transfer of land to new proprietors). The effects of the French Revolution of 1848 in England — the alarm of the propertied classes, the voluntary enrolment of special constables (the Duke of Wellington organising the defence of London), the presentation of the Chartist petition. Molesworth traces the Chartist movement from its origins in the 1838 People’s Charter (six points: universal suffrage, equal electoral districts, abolition of property qualification for MPs, payment of members, annual parliaments, the ballot). The Chartist Convention in London, Mr. Duncombe’s amendment to an address, the formal adoption of the Charter, Feargus O’Connor’s leadership and his oratory, the progress of Chartism, the National Petition with its millions of signatures, the “Sacred Month” (the general strike that never really materialised), the Newport insurrection of 1839 (John Frost, the march on the Westgate Hotel, the trials and transportation of the leaders), the Kennington Common meeting of 10 April 1848 (O’Connor, the special constables, the petition delivered to Parliament with its forged signatures), the Land Scheme of O’Connor, the eventual collapse of Chartism as a mass movement. The chapter also covers Mr. Hume’s motion for financial reform, diplomatic relations with the Papacy, the public health legislation (the Public Health Act of 1848, Edwin Chadwick’s work, the General Board of Health), the death of Lord George Bentinck, the repeal of the Navigation Laws (the opening of the British carrying trade to foreign shipping), Disraeli’s resolution on agricultural distress, and Irish and colonial legislation.

Chapter VI — The Great Exhibition

The year 1850 opens with agricultural distress continuing, the Budget of 1850 (Sir Charles Wood’s financial statement), the window-tax (its unpopularity and the agitation for its abolition), the proposed reductions of official salaries (the “economical reform” movement), the Gorham Judgment (the Privy Council’s decision on baptismal regeneration, the defection of Archdeacon Manning and others to Rome), the Judicial Committee of the Privy Council and its growing role, the university reform movement, the removal of the remaining Dissenters’ disabilities, the Sunday delivery of letters controversy (the Post Office’s attempt to stop Sunday deliveries, defeated by Chartist and working-class protest). Peel’s last speech (the debate on the Greek question, his defence of Lord Palmerston’s foreign policy) and his fatal fall from his horse on Constitution Hill, his death and funeral, the universal tributes to his memory. The Papal Aggression — the restoration of the Roman Catholic hierarchy in England by Pope Pius IX (the “Durham Letter” of Lord John Russell denouncing the “insolent and insidious” aggression), the Ecclesiastical Titles Bill (the prohibition of Catholic territorial titles, its passage and later repeal). Alderman Salomons and the Jewish disabilities question. The Great Exhibition of 1851 — Joseph Paxton’s Crystal Palace in Hyde Park, the machinery, manufactures, and works of art from all nations, the opening by the Queen, the vast crowds, the profit that funded the South Kensington museum complex. The foundation of Owens College, Manchester (the precursor to the Victoria University). A new Reform Bill, the dismissal of Lord Palmerston from the Foreign Office (his unauthorised approval of the French coup d’état), the Derby administration (the “Who? Who?” ministry of 1852, Disraeli’s first budget), the Aberdeen coalition ministry (Lord Aberdeen as Prime Minister, Gladstone at the Exchequer, Palmerston at the Home Office). The session of 1853 — Gladstone’s great financial statement (the extension of the income tax, the reduction of the tea and sugar duties, the succession duty). Molesworth concludes with the prosperous condition of the country, the rise of the Eastern Question (the dispute between Russia and Turkey over the Holy Places, the Menshikov mission, the Russian occupation of the Principalities), the destruction of the Turkish fleet at Sinope, the popular war fever in England, and the decision to send the Baltic and Black Sea fleets.
