A History of England from the Conclusion of the Great War in 1815, Vol. VI

A History of England from the Conclusion of the Great War in 1815, Vol. VI

Jan 01, 19709 min read

A History of England from the Conclusion of the Great War in 1815, Vol. VI — by Spencer Walpole

Vol. VI, from the expanded 6-volume edition (1905), covers the period 1853–1880. The volume contains five chapters (XXIV–XXVIII), focusing on the Crimean War, India, and the growth of the British colonial empire.

CHAPTER XXIV — The Crimean War (lines 1163–4311)

Period: 1853–1856

The Origins — The Holy Places and the Eastern Question: The war originated in a dispute between Russia and France over the custody of the Holy Places in Palestine — churches, shrines, and the keys to the Church of the Nativity in Bethlehem. Russia claimed the right to protect Orthodox Christian subjects of the Ottoman Empire under the Treaty of Kainardji (1774). Napoleon III, seeking prestige for his new regime, challenged this by asserting French claims on behalf of Latin Christians.

Menschikoff’s Mission (February 1853): Prince Menschikoff was sent to Constantinople as a special envoy. He demanded a sened (imperial decree) and a treaty recognising Russia’s right to protect Orthodox Christians throughout the Ottoman Empire. The British Ambassador, Lord Stratford de Redcliffe, counselled the Porte to reject these demands. The Czar Nicholas I, convinced that Britain would not fight, ordered the occupation of the Danubian Principalities (July 1853).

The Vienna Note: A compromise was drafted by the four Powers (Austria, France, Prussia, Britain) and accepted by Russia but rejected by the Porte on Stratford’s advice. Walpole is sharply critical of Stratford, arguing that he “fulfilled his instructions to the letter, but he so contrived that his employers were constantly getting deeper into a war policy.” The Czar’s interpretation of the Note, leaked through a Prussian newspaper, further inflamed tensions.

The War: The Turkish fleet was destroyed at Sinope (November 1853). Britain and France declared war on Russia (March 1854). The allied armies landed in the Crimea. Battles of the Alma, Balaclava (including the Charge of the Light Brigade), Inkerman. The Siege of Sebastopol, winter 1854–55. The sufferings of the army: disease, cold, mismanagement. Florence Nightingale and the sanitary commission. The death of Lord Raglan (June 1855). Sardinia joins the allies. The storming of the Redan and the Malakoff (September 1855). The Russians evacuate Sebastopol.

Peace: Austria’s ultimatum to Russia; the Congress of Paris (February–March 1856). The Treaty of Paris neutralised the Black Sea, guaranteed the integrity of the Ottoman Empire, opened the Danube to free navigation, and admitted Turkey to the European concert. Walpole’s verdict: the war set back European progress by fourteen years. The only permanent result was the freeing of the Danube and the weakening of Russia’s position in the Near East.

CHAPTER XXV — The Supremacy of the British in India (lines 4312–8001)

Period: c. 1750–1835

A historical survey of the rise of British power in India. Walpole argues that the growth of the British colonial empire is the “most striking and lasting monument of Anglo-Saxon supremacy,” but that the conquest of India provides the more thrilling story.

The Foundations: The East India Company’s early struggles against French competition (Dupleix, Clive). The Battle of Plassey (1757). The administration of Warren Hastings. The Regulating Act and Pitt’s India Act. Lord Cornwallis and the Permanent Settlement of Bengal. Sir John Shore. Lord Wellesley and the Subsidiary System — the war with Tippoo Sultan, the annexation of the Carnatic, the conquest of the Mahrattas.

The Early Governors-General: Lord Cornwallis (second term), Lord Minto, Lord Moira (Marquess of Hastings — the Nepaulese War, the Pindarees, the third Mahratta War), Lord Amherst (the first Burmese War), Lord William Bentinck (financial reforms, abolition of suttee, suppression of thuggee, educational reforms, Macaulay’s Minute).

The Civil and Military System: The Company’s army, the Civil Service, the administration of justice, the Ryotwaree system (Munro), the Land Settlement of Bengal, the sale of offices, the institution of a police force.

CHAPTER XXVI — Afghanistan, Scinde, and the Punjab (lines 8002–12027)

Period: 1835–1849

Bentinck’s Successors: Bentinck’s resignation; Metcalfe’s interim administration. The free press in India — Silk Buckingham’s case, Metcalfe and Macaulay’s reforms. Lord Auckland as Governor-General.

The Afghan Disaster (1838–1842): Russian influence in Persia, the Euphrates route to India, the siege of Herat, Alexander Burnes at Cabul, Vicovitch’s mission, Auckland’s decision to dethrone Dost Mahommed. The Tripartite Treaty with Ranjeet Singh (1838). The Army of the Indus advances, Ghuznee falls, Cabul is occupied. The unpopularity of the war in England. The disastrous winter of 1841–42: the rising, the murder of Burnes and Macnaghten, the retreat from Cabul and annihilation of the army. Sale’s defence of Jellalabad. Pollock’s and Nott’s advance. The destruction of the Cabul bazaar. The release of the prisoners.

Scinde (1843): Lord Ellenborough as Governor-General. The conquest of Scinde by Sir Charles Napier — the battles of Meeanee and Hyderabad. The controversy over Napier’s “iniquitous” annexation. Operations in Gwalior.

The Sikh Wars (1845–1849): The death of Runjeet Singh. The Sikh Army becomes supreme in the Punjab. The first Sikh War: Moodkee, Ferozeshah, Aliwal, Sobraon. The Treaty of Lahore. Sir Henry Hardinge as Governor-General. The Lawrences and the administration of the Punjab. The second Sikh War: the murder of Vans Agnew and Anderson at Mooltan, Herbert Edwardes, the battles of Chillianwalla and Gujarat. The annexation of the Punjab under Lord Dalhousie.

CHAPTER XXVII — Dalhousie and Canning (lines 12028–16374)

Period: 1849–1856

The Second Burmese War (1852): Lambert’s mission to Rangoon, the Court of Ava yields, the refusal of the new Governor to receive Captain Fishbourne, Lambert’s stringent measures, the Burmese fire on H.M.S. Fox, Dalhousie’s ultimatum, the expedition, the annexation of Pegu. The cost and controversy of the war.

Oudh and the Doctrine of Lapse: The State of Oudh, Wellesley’s policy, Bentinck’s threat of interference, Auckland’s treaty of 1837 (abrogated without notice to the Nawab). Hardinge’s warning. Sleeman’s tour and recommendations (1849). Outram’s report. Dalhousie’s ultimatum. The annexation of Oudh (February 1856). Walpole’s treatment reveals the moral and legal controversy: the British case for annexation (misgovernment, duty to the people) and the Indian case for forbearance (the treaty of 1801 constituted an independent state; earlier governors-general had accepted the Nawab’s sovereignty). Dalhousie’s own ambivalence.

Dalhousie’s Reforms: Railways, the electric telegraph, the Ganges canal, the postal system, educational policy (Wood’s Despatch of 1854). His retirement and death. Lord Canning succeeds him.

Persia and Herat (1856): The Persian occupation of Herat, the British demand for its evacuation, the Persian War (1856–57), its rapid termination. The difficulty of fixing India’s north-western frontier.

The Eve of the Mutiny: The state of the Indian army. The grievances of the Sepoys: the General Service Order of 1856, the refusal of the 38th Regiment to embark for Burma, the greased cartridge question (foreshadowed). The European and Native troop ratio.

CHAPTER XXVIII — Greater Britain (lines 16375–18929)

Period: c. 1800–1870

The Colonies and the Mother Country: Walpole argues that Britain’s colonial empire is the true monument of British greatness, though at the beginning of the century the colonies were conquered possessions governed by the restrictive mercantile system.

The Old Colonial System and its Fall: The Navigation Acts, the monopoly of colonial trade, the relaxation after 1822 (Huskisson’s reforms), the triumph of free trade, the repeal of the Navigation Acts (1849). The colonial system transformed: from monopoly to freedom.

The Slave Trade and the West Indies: Abolition of the slave trade (1807), slave emancipation (1833), the £20 million compensation, the apprenticeship system (1834–1838), the decline of the West Indian colonies after emancipation. The rise of alternative supplies of sugar from Cuba and Brazil.

Emigration and the Growth of Population: Malthus’ theory, the pressure of population in the British Isles, the famine in Ireland and the Highlands, the great emigration of the 1840s and 1850s. The colonies as the safety-valve.

South Africa: The Cape Colony (acquired 1806, ceded 1815). The Boers and their trek (the Great Trek, 1835–37). The conflicts with the Zulus and the Kaffir wars. The annexation of Natal (1843). The Orange Free State and the Transvaal. Sir Harry Smith and the Battle of Boomplaats (1848). Governor Sir George Grey. The problem of native policy and race relations.

Canada: The Constitutional Act of 1791. The rebellion of 1837 (Papineau, Mackenzie). Lord Durham’s Report (1839) — “two nations warring in the bosom of a single state.” The union of Upper and Lower Canada (1841). The achievement of responsible government: Lord Elgin, the Rebellion Losses Bill (1849), the burning of the Parliament House at Montreal. The Reciprocity Treaty with the United States (1854). Confederation: the Quebec Conference (1864), the British North America Act (1867).

Australia: Discovery by Captain Cook (1770). The first convict settlement at Botany Bay (1788). The crossing of the Blue Mountains (1813). John Macarthur and the wool industry. The Wakefield theory of systematic colonisation. The abolition of transportation to New South Wales (1840). The discovery of gold (1851, Bathurst and Ballarat). The Eureka Stockade (1854). Responsible government granted to New South Wales, Victoria, South Australia, Tasmania, and Queensland. The exploration of the interior: Burke and Wills, Stuart, Macdouall.

New Zealand: Discovery by Tasman (1642) and Cook (1769). The Maoris — their character, capacity for civilisation. Samuel Marsden’s missionary work, the Treaty of Waitangi (1840). The New Zealand Company under Edward Gibbon Wakefield. Colonisation of the North and South Islands. The Maori Wars (the Flagstaff War, the Taranaki War, the Waikato campaigns). The problem of native title and race relations. Self-government granted (1852).

Transportation: The history of convict transportation to Australia. The last convicts sent to Western Australia (1868). The end of the system.

Conclusion: Walpole closes with the achievement of self-government in the colonies and the question of whether they would eventually separate from the mother country. These issues, he notes, remain open. The volume closes with Britain at the height of its imperial power but facing new questions of colonial relationship and responsibility.
