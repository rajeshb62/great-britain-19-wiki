The History of England from the Year 1830–1874, Vol. III

The History of England from the Year 1830–1874, Vol. III

Jan 01, 197013 min read

The History of England from the Year 1830–1874, Vol. III — by William Nassau Molesworth

Vol. III covers the period 1854–1874. This is the most politically eventful of the three volumes, opening in the midst of the Crimean War and closing with the Conservative victory of 1874 that ended Gladstone’s first ministry. Molesworth, writing in the immediate aftermath of these events, was able to consult a wide range of parliamentary papers, contemporary newspapers, published correspondence, and memoirs — the volume reflects a more documentary approach than the earlier two, with extensive quotation from Hansard, state papers, and the leading reviews. The narrative remains firmly centred on Westminster politics and high policy, but Molesworth continues his practice of interspersing chapters on the material, intellectual, and social progress of the period.

Chapter I — Sebastopol

The chapter opens with an extended review of the characteristics of the period — the extraordinary material growth of England since 1830, the advances in science and literature, the changes in manners and opinion — before plunging into the military narrative. The Russian retreat from the Danubian Principalities, the allied landing at Eupatoria, the battle of the Alma (20 September 1854), the flank march and the investment of Sebastopol. The battle of Balaclava (25 October) — the Charge of the Light Brigade, Lord Cardigan’s cavalry, the thin red line of the 93rd Highlanders. The battle of Inkerman (5 November) — “the soldiers’ battle,” fought in a fog. Miss Florence Nightingale’s mission to Scutari, the organisation of the nursing service, the mortality at the Barrack Hospital. The winter of 1854–55 — the hurricane, the destruction of the portable huts and stores, the breakdown of the commissariat, the sufferings of the troops before Sebastopol, the correspondence of W. H. Russell in The Times exposing the mismanagement. On the home front: the session of 1854, another reform bill introduced and withdrawn, Gladstone’s budget increasing the income tax, the Reform Club dinner, the declaration of war, the departure of the Baltic fleet, the Patriotic Fund. The Roebuck committee of inquiry — the mismanagement revelations, the resignation of the Aberdeen coalition, the formation of Lord Palmerston’s first ministry (February 1855), the withdrawal of the Peelites (Gladstone, Graham, Herbert), the death of the Emperor Nicholas. The renewal of the bombardment of Sebastopol, the capture of the Malakoff (8 September 1855), the Russian evacuation of the south side of the fortress, the fall of Kars to the Russians after a heroic defence, and the negotiations leading to the Peace of Paris (30 March 1856). Molesworth closes with the declaration of peace, the subsequent thanksgiving, the territorial settlement (the neutralisation of the Black Sea, the independence of the Danubian Principalities), and the general sense that a new era had opened.

Chapter II — The Indian Mutiny

The chapter opens with the life-peerages debate (the Wensleydale peerage case, the Lords’ refusal to admit a life peer) and the peace rejoicings. The dispute with the United States over Central America and the recruiting question. The Chinese War — the affair of the Arrow, the bombardment of Canton, the debate in Parliament, the defeat of the government on Cobden’s censure motion, the general election of 1857, and Palmerston’s triumphant return. Molesworth then turns to India: the greased cartridges, the growing disaffection in the Bengal army, the outbreak at Meerut (10 May 1857), the seizure of Delhi, the massacre at Cawnpore (Nana Sahib, the surrender, the massacre of the women and children), the sieges of Lucknow (Havelock’s relief, Outram’s defence, Sir Colin Campbell’s second relief), the recapture of Delhi (the assault, the death of John Nicholson), the reconquest of Oude. The reception of the news in England — the shock, the anger, the demand for vengeance, and the debates over the causes of the revolt. Disraeli’s motion, the testimony of Indian witnesses before the Parliamentary committee, the abolition of the East India Company (the Government of India Act 1858, the transfer of power to the Crown, the creation of the India Office and the Council of India). The Orsini attempt on Napoleon III (January 1858) — the bomb thrown by Italian revolutionaries, the discovery that the bombs were made in England, the French colonels’ addresses to Napoleon demanding retribution, the Conspiracy to Murder Bill, Palmerston’s defeat, and the formation of the second Derby administration. The India bills, the competitive examinations for the Indian Civil Service (the Macaulay committee’s report, the introduction of open competition), the final removal of Jewish disabilities (Baron Rothschild at last taking his seat), the purification of the Thames (the continuing stench, the metropolitan drainage scheme), the laying of the Atlantic telegraph, and the renewal of the reform agitation.

Chapter III — The French Treaty

The Conservative Reform Bill of 1859 (Disraeli’s attempt at a Conservative measure of reform, Lord John Russell’s amendment on the franchise, the defeat of the government), the general election of 1859 (the “union of the Whigs and Radicals” under Palmerston), the defeat of Derby’s ministry, and the return of Palmerston with Lord John Russell as Foreign Secretary — the Palmerston-Russell duumvirate. Molesworth traces Richard Cobden’s role in the negotiation of the Anglo-French Commercial Treaty (the Cobden-Chevalier Treaty of 1860), Cobden’s view of free trade as a means to peace, the tariff reductions on French wines, silks, and manufactured goods, the reciprocal reduction of French duties on British coal and iron. The frequent changes of ministries in the 1850s (the “everybody against everybody” period), the Liverpool Financial Reform Association, the St. George’s-in-the-East riots (the ritualist controversy in the London church). The death and character of Lord Macaulay — his History of England, his literary fame, his Whig interpretation of history. Gladstone’s French Treaty budget of 1860 — the abolition of the paper duty (the “taxes on knowledge”), the extension of the income tax, the duties on hops and fire-insurance. Lord John Russell’s Reform Bill of 1860, Mackinnon’s motion on the representation, and the withdrawal of the reform bill. The Paper Duties Bill rejected by the Lords — the Commons’ resolution against the Lords’ right to reject a finance bill, the eventual repeal of the paper duties in the budget of 1861. Lord Palmerston’s reconciliations, the invasion panic (the fortification of Portsmouth, the Volunteer movement, Tennyson’s “Riflemen Form!”), international pugilism, the harvest, the church-rates controversy, the budget of 1861. The American Civil War — the affair of the Trent (Captain Wilkes of the USS San Jacinto stopping the British mail packet and removing the Confederate commissioners Mason and Slidell, the British demand for apology and reparation, Prince Albert’s intervention in the wording of the despatch, the release of the commissioners), and the death of the Prince Consort (14 December 1861) — Molesworth records the universal grief, the Queen’s withdrawal from public life, the debate over the Prince’s constitutional role.

Chapter IV — The Palmerston Ministry

The session of 1862 — the effects of the American Civil War on the British cotton industry (the cotton famine, the distress in Lancashire, the relief committees, the destitution of 500,000 operatives with remarkable patience and order), the new Law Courts project, the church-rate question again, the Exhibition of 1862 (the South Kensington building, the exhibits, the financial failure), Glaisher’s balloon ascent (the scientific exploration of the upper atmosphere), the Roupell forgery case (the exposure of the elaborate forgery by a young solicitor, the flight of the forger), the improvement in the condition of factory operatives under the factory acts, the Alabama claims question (the building of the Confederate cruiser in Birkenhead, its escape, the depredations on United States commerce, the eventual arbitration), and the co-operative movement (the Rochdale pioneers, the growth of co-operative stores). The garrotting panic of 1862–63, President Lincoln’s Emancipation Proclamation, the marriage of the Prince of Wales (the Princess Alexandra of Denmark, the festivities), Gladstone’s budget (the reduction of the income tax to 4d.), the Polish insurrection (the British and French notes to Russia, the diplomatic isolation of the British government), the Trent mail case and international law, law reform (the fusion of law and equity), the Volunteer corps (the 150,000 volunteers, the reviews at Buckingham Palace and Woolwich), the death of Thackeray. The Schleswig-Holstein question — the Danish war of 1864, the invasion of Schleswig by Austria and Prussia, Lord Russell’s diplomatic notes, the Conference of London, the collapse of Danish resistance, the decline of British influence in Europe. Gladstone on parliamentary reform — his celebrated speech declaring that “every man who is not presumably incapacitated by some consideration of personal unfitness or of political danger is morally entitled to come within the pale of the Constitution.” The Permissive Bill (the temperance movement’s attempt to allow local veto on the sale of liquor), the Sheffield flood (the bursting of the Dale Dyke dam, the loss of 250 lives), Garibaldi’s visit to England (the popular enthusiasm, the quiet discouragement by the government, the withdrawal to Caprera), the murder of Mr. Briggs (the first railway murder, the public excitement, the trial of Franz Müller). The session of 1865 — the union-chargeability bill (reform of the poor law rating), the Irish Church question, Gladstone’s budget (the reduction of the tea and sugar duties), Baines’s motion for reduction of the franchise, the preparation for a general election, the resignation of Lord Chancellor Westbury (the leakage of confidential information, the criticism by the Lords committee), the general election of 1865, Gladstone’s triumph in South Lancashire (his defeat at Oxford, his re-election elsewhere), the death of Cobden, the assassination of President Lincoln, and the death of Lord Palmerston (18 October 1865) — Molesworth closes with an extended character sketch of Palmerston, his long career, his robust patriotism, his popularity with the country, his efficiency as a war minister, his easy-going management of the House of Commons.

Chapter V — The Reform Bill of 1867

Lord Russell’s second ministry (the formation of the cabinet, Gladstone as leader of the House and Chancellor of the Exchequer, the new members, the cattle plague of 1865–66, the limited liability acts, the report of the reform commission, the session of 1866). Gladstone’s Reform Bill — the household suffrage in boroughs coupled with a £14 rating franchise in counties, the bill to reduce the county franchise to £14. The “Cave of Adullam” — the group of Liberal malcontents led by Robert Lowe and Lord Elcho, who opposed any further extension of the franchise, the brilliant speeches of Lowe, the defection of Horsman and others, the defeat of the government on an Adullamite amendment (the “tea-room revolt”), the resignation of Russell (June 1866), and the formation of the Derby-Disraeli ministry. The Reform Bill of 1867 — Disraeli’s “leap in the dark,” his acceptance of household suffrage in boroughs (the compounding question, the registered voters as the sole test), the passage of the bill through the Commons with Hodgkinson’s amendment (the removal of the compound householder clauses, the vast extension of the franchise in towns), the Salford by-election showing the new voters in action, the bill in the Lords, the royal assent. The Scottish and Irish Reform Acts followed. The Abyssinian expedition (Sir Robert Napier’s march to Magdala, the release of the British prisoners, the death of King Theodore), the Fenian outbreak (the rising in Ireland, the rescue of Kelly at Manchester (the “Manchester martyrs” executed), the explosion at Clerkenwell prison, the resulting alarm, the suspension of habeas corpus in Ireland). The resignation of the Earl of Derby (his ill-health), Disraeli’s first brief premiership (February to November 1868), the budget, the general election of 1868 (the first under the new household franchise in the boroughs), the defeat of the Conservatives, and Gladstone’s accession as Prime Minister with a majority of 120.

Chapter VI — The Gladstone Ministry

Gladstone’s first cabinet — “a ministry of all the talents” (Lowe at the Exchequer, Bright at the Board of Trade, Cardwell at the War Office, the Duke of Argyll, Lord Granville, Lord Clarendon, Lord Hatherley, Bruce at the Home Office, Fortescue in Ireland). The Irish Church Act of 1869 — the disestablishment and disendowment of the Church of Ireland, the commutation of the life interests of the clergy, the passage of the bill through the Commons and the Lords (the Lords’ eventual surrender after the Queen’s intervention). The Irish Land Act of 1870 — the three F’s (fixity of tenure, fair rent, free sale), the Ulster custom given legal force, the Bright clauses for the creation of a peasant proprietory. The Elementary Education Act of 1870 — W. E. Forster’s bill, the creation of school boards, the filling up of educational gaps, the Birmingham League (Chamberlain, the demand for compulsory free education), the Cowper-Temple clause (the prohibition of distinctive religious teaching in board schools), the denominational schools controversy, the rates and the school boards. The abolition of purchase in the army — Cardwell’s reforms (the War Office reorganisation, the short service system, the localisation of regiments, the abolition of flogging in peacetime). The Treaty of Washington (1871) and the Alabama arbitration — the British acceptance of the “three rules” of neutrality (the duty of a neutral to prevent the fitting out of cruisers in its ports, the extension of the blockade, the notification to belligerents), the arbitration in Geneva, the award of £3¼ million against Britain. The Ballot Act of 1872 — the introduction of secret voting at parliamentary and municipal elections, the end of the old open system of public declarations in the hustings. The Licensing Act of 1872 — the reform of the liquor licensing system, the regulation of public-house hours, the opposition of the publicans, the “Beer Act” riots in some towns. The Irish University Bill of 1873 — Gladstone’s attempt to create a federal university for Ireland incorporating the Catholic University, Trinity College, and the Queen’s Colleges, the opposition of the Catholic bishops and the Protestant interest, the defeat of the bill by three votes, the resignation of the cabinet, the refusal of Disraeli to form a government, the return of Gladstone to office. The session of 1874 — Gladstone’s proposed repeal of the income tax (his “drowning man” budget), the general election of 1874 (the first by secret ballot, the first major test of the new borough electorate), the Conservative victory (a majority of about 50), the defeat of Gladstone himself in Greenwich, and the close of the Gladstone ministry. Molesworth appends a concluding chapter on the characteristics of the period 1830–1874, surveying the material progress (the growth of railways, the improvements in navigation, the development of the telegraph, the expansion of the coal and iron industries), the intellectual advances (the progress of science, the works of Mill, Darwin, and Herbert Spencer, the development of the periodical press), the moral and social improvements (the reform of the criminal law, the condition of the people, the Factory Acts, the Public Health Acts, the Education Acts), and some concluding reflections on the prospects of the nation.
