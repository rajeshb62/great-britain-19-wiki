Engels — The Condition of the Working Class in England

Friedrich Engels (1820–1895) — The Condition of the Working Class in England. — Translated by Florence Wischnewetzky (American edition, 1886); republished in Penguin Classics, 1987, with a Foreword by Victor Kiernan, and the text checked against the German first edition (1845) for accuracy.

Engels wrote this book in late 1844 and early 1845, after a two-year stay in Manchester (1842–1844) where he was sent by his father to learn the family textile business. The book is a contemporary investigation of the English working class during the Industrial Revolution, based on Engels’s own observations in Manchester and other industrial towns, official reports (Factory Reports, Parliamentary Papers, reports of the Children’s Employment Commission), and contemporary writings. It was written in German for a German audience but describes conditions in England — then the most advanced industrial nation — both as a record and as a warning.

The text is 3,479 lines, extracted from the 1987 Penguin Classics EPUB (translation based on the Florence Wischnewetzky American edition of 1886, with stylistic revision by Victor Kiernan).

Content Summary

This Penguin edition includes a modern scholarly apparatus:

  • Foreword by Victor Kiernan (1987) — a biographical and historiographical introduction setting Engels’s book in its context: Engels’s background in the Rhineland, his arrival in Manchester, the sources he drew on, the strengths and limitations of his analysis, and the book’s enduring significance

  • To the Working Classes of Great Britain (1845) — Engels’s original dedication, addressed directly to the English working men, explaining his purpose in writing the book

  • Preface to the First German Edition (1845) — Engels’s original preface, explaining that he wrote the book for a German audience but based on English conditions, and noting his use of “Mittelklasse” (middle class) to mean the capitalist class

  • Preface to the English Edition (1892) — Engels’s retrospective preface, written nearly fifty years later, surveying how much had changed: the repeal of the Corn Laws, the passing of the Ten Hours’ Bill, the growth of the labour movement, the rise of trade unions, and the industrial monopoly of England now threatened by foreign competition
