Gilbart — The Laws of the Currency (Country Bank Notes since 1844)

James William Gilbart, F.R.S. (1794–1863) — The Laws of the Currency, as Exemplified in the Circulation of Country Bank Notes in England, since the Passing of the Act of 1844. — Journal of the Statistical Society of London, vol. 17 (1854), pp. 289–321.

Paper read before the Statistical Section of the British Association for the Advancement of Science at Liverpool, 22 September 1854; printed in the Society’s journal for December 1854. Gilbart, general manager of the London and Westminster Bank and author of The History and Principles of Banking, treats currency as a subject for statistical investigation rather than party politics. The inquiry is limited to country bank notes in England and to the fluctuations in their circulation after the Bank Charter Act of 1844 (7 & 8 Vict. c. 32). An appendix of statistical tables (licenses and bankruptcies, stamp duties, stamp issues, Gazette returns, authorized vs. actual issues, deposits, comparative monthly series, &c.) supports the narrative.

What the paper is and how it opens

Gilbart opens by welcoming the detachment of currency debate from faction and its transfer to statistics. He then walks the reader through the genealogy of English note issue before turning to the Act of 1844 and to what monthly returns show about “laws” of circulation—uniform seasonal patterns that differ for the Bank of England, English country banks, Scotland, and Ireland.

Origins of country notes and the growth of country banking

Country bank notes, he reminds the section, began as goldsmiths’ notes. After Charles I seized merchants’ deposits in the Tower mint (1640), and after apprentices proved unsafe custodians in the Civil War, merchants lodged money with goldsmiths who both borrowed and lent, took rents, advanced on the taxes to the Crown, and issued receipts that passed from hand to hand. Statutes long spoke of “bankers’ or goldsmiths’ notes”; Irish acts of 1709 and 1729 treated such notes like inland bills and punished forgery. After the Bank of England’s foundation (1694), its notes gradually superseded the goldsmiths’; banking separated from the goldsmith’s craft, though many London bankers still belonged to the Goldsmiths’ Company mid-century.

Authentic continuous statistics of country banking are scarce for the early period. Few country banks existed before the American war; they multiplied after that war, suffered in 1793 (twenty-two bankruptcies), and expanded rapidly after the Bank Restriction Act. From 1808 note-issuing banks took annual licenses; stamp duty on notes dates from 1804 and was raised in 1808 and 1815. Parliament barred notes under 20s. in 1775 and under £5 in 1777; Restriction (1797) reopened £1 and £2 issues, continued toward the 1833 charter expiry, until after the panic of 1825 government stopped stamps under £5 and required existing small notes to cease by 1829. Speculations before 1825 were blamed on country banks; the legislative answer abolished small notes, allowed banks of more than six partners beyond sixty-five miles from London, and drew the Bank of England into provincial branches.

Lancashire, bills of exchange, and Bank of England branches

Lancaster—especially Manchester and Liverpool—formed a special case: little country paper and few Bank of England notes; circulation ran mainly on bills of exchange endorsed from hand to hand. Large notes for customs duties and small notes for wages came from London; manufacturers and dealers settled among themselves on London-payable bills. After 1826, Bank of England branches at Manchester and Liverpool, and joint-stock banks that took Bank of England paper at a preferential discount rather than issuing their own, displaced much of the bill circulation. With weak private banks gone or merged, and notes under £5 abolished, a “secure” country circulation seemed achieved—until later panics shifted the indictment.

Accusations against country issues, 1836–1841, and bankers’ replies

In late 1836 another panic produced a charge not of unsafety but of excess, aimed especially at joint-stock banks. Unlike 1825, when circulation could only be guessed from stamps, Lord Althorp’s Act of 1833 (3 & 4 Will. IV c. 83) forced quarterly averages of weekly circulation into the Stamp Office and the London Gazette. Those returns showed country circulation up by early 1836; contemporaneous speculation and year-end panic were then blamed on the notes. In 1839 the complaint changed again: country circulation was ill-regulated—it did not move with Bank of England gold (nor even with Bank of England notes) as some political economists required. A Commons committee on banks of issue sat in 1840–41; the practical fruit was weekly returns (4 & 5 Vict. c. 50).

Gilbart sets out the country bankers’ earlier answers (including their 1833 memorial to Earl Grey): London failures were relatively worse; great fluctuations began with influential merchants in great cities and with Bank of England issues, not in country channels (Liverpool failures of a few houses ran to seven or eight millions where country notes never circulated); country issues promoted domestic production and had less power to throw the exchanges; forcing miners and manufacturers into direct contact with exchange-regulating operations without the cushion of local circulation would be hazardous.

The Act of 1844 and the choice of maximum

When Peel returned to office and the Bank charter came up in 1844, the statute “to regulate the issue of bank notes” answered mainly the charge of excess. No new bank of issue might be created in the United Kingdom. Each existing English bank’s maximum average over four weeks was fixed at its average for the twelve weeks ending 27 April 1844; weekly averages were returned, and four-week excess brought a penalty equal to the excess. Increasing partners beyond six (for a small bank), certain unions, bankruptcy, or ceasing to issue extinguished the right forever. The Act did not compel country issues to track Bank gold: October 1844 gold about £12.15m, October 1847 about £6.75m, July 1852 about £21.85m, without corresponding legal contraction or expansion of country maxima.

The April 1844 twelve-week base was a compromise. Peel first proposed a two-year average; private bankers asked five years; joint-stock banks preferred the maximum of two years lest an average-turned-ceiling shrink issues further. Certified totals settled near £5.15m private and £3.50m joint-stock (205 private, 72 joint-stock banks). Country circulation had already fallen sharply 1839–1843 (from about £11.7m to about £7.7m). Bankers blamed dull trade and low corn prices; Gilbart also lists lasting causes: banks swapping own notes for Bank of England paper under advantageous contracts; railways returning notes faster; penny post (1840) and letter registration speeding inter-bank remittance of rivals’ notes to London; the spread of deposit accounts and cheques instead of pocket notes. Even without 1844, he thinks actual issues would not have recovered former peaks. A hard maximum on a seasonal series forces the average below the ceiling; the maximum was sliced among many banks each keeping a safety margin; the penalty equal to the whole excess made prudence keep issues well under the line.

How restriction was felt—and how deposits softened it

Restriction showed in attempted evasions (a circular under Sir Charles Wood; note-like cheques on penny stamps later stopped by stamp law); in fourth-week dips when banks pulled in their own paper and fetched £5 and £10 Bank notes from London to hold four-week averages; and in joint-stock banks that issued only at some branches and used Bank of England notes elsewhere. Branch Bank of England circulation rose as country issues fell (country about £11.7m with branches £3.5m in 1836; country about £7.7m with branches £6.5m in 1846). Yet the deposit system offset much pressure: once notes lodge with bankers and payments move by cheque and clearing, transfers replace note circulation. London joint-stock deposits had already passed £22m while Bank of England private deposits rose from about £5m to £12m; Gilbart believes similar deposit growth was general in the provinces without extinguishing private banks. Joint-stock admission to the clearing house and settlement by Bank of England cheques would economize notes still further.

“Laws of the currency”: seasonal circuits before and after 1844

From Commons monthly averages (Bank of England, English country, Scotland, Ireland), Gilbart had earlier derived “laws of the currency,” by analogy with laws of mortality—uniform recurrences in chronological figures.

  • Bank of England: four tides a year with the public dividends—January higher than February–March, April than May–June, July than August–September, October than November–December—because the Bank alone pays the dividends.
  • English country notes: one annual circuit—lowest about end of August, rising through November (slight December reaction) to a peak in April, then down again to August; governed by agricultural and local trade seasons, not by dividend dates.
  • Scotland: lowest in March, high in May and especially November (mortgage interest, annuities, deposit interest, servants’ wages, mortgage transfers); Scottish habit settles large and small payments in notes rather than London-style cheques; monthly pattern highly regular year to year.
  • Ireland: agricultural rhythm—rise with harvest and cattle and bacon trade, peak around January, then decline; expansion strongest in rural joint-stock districts and in the smallest denominations; annual level set by harvest and produce prices; Bank of Ireland in Dublin expands less than branches in towns, which expand less than agricultural issuers.

Before the 1841 banks-of-issue committee he drew the general moral: each of these circulations obeys laws peculiar to itself; they cannot be forced into one amalgamated system without wrecking the “beautiful system of country banking” that receives surplus capital in one district and releases it for trade and industry where wanted, expanding and contracting locally as trade requires.

After 1844, tables in the appendix show the same seasonal laws still governing English country issues. The Act capped the height of the wave; it did not rewrite the calendar of the tide.

Extinction of issues, topography, and actual vs. authorized circulation

Because lapsed issues cannot be revived, the ceiling itself erodes. Since 1844, thirty-seven private country banks had ceased to issue (eleven inside the sixty-five-mile circle, twenty-six outside; combined fixed issue about £536,793) and seven joint-stock banks (about £169,589)—among them houses at Devonport, Ipswich, Stockton, Leeds, Sheffield, Newcastle—cutting original certified issue from about £8.65m to about £7.94m. Topographically: inside the sixty-five-mile London circle there is no joint-stock bank of issue and no Bank of England branch of the provincial type under discussion; forty-seven private banks there hold about £1.30m authorized issue; outside, 120 private banks about £3.31m and sixty-five joint-stock banks about £3.33m. Average authorized size of private banks is nearly the same inside and outside the circle (about £27,700).

For actual circulation in 1853 (April, August, December), private banks run further below authorization than joint-stock banks, and private banks inside the circle further below than those outside—joint-stock actuals often in the high 80s–low 90s percent of authorization in peak months, private banks inside the circle nearer the mid-70s. Causes are assigned cautiously (competition, Bank branches, deposit banking, the penalty cushion). Gilbart notes that if the long policy is gradual extinction of country issues toward a single issuer, non-revival of lapsed rights serves that end; he doubts that London expansion of Bank of England notes can replace notes extinguished in a remote district, and he rehearses older evidence that depriving country bankers of note profit would raise charges on accommodation and close marginal rural offices.

Crisis of 1847, Scotland and Ireland under 1845, and small notes

He reviews how country circulation behaved through commercial pressure (including 1847) under the fixed maxima, and contrasts English rules with the 1845 arrangements for Scotland and Ireland. There a bank may exceed its authorized issue if it holds gold equal to the excess; in England excess is a full-value penalty with no gold (or Bank-note) offset. Bank of England notes are not legal tender in Scotland or Ireland (hence coin reserves there), yet English banks cannot lawfully expand against Bank of England notes the way Scottish and Irish banks expand against sovereigns. Unions of issuing banks may carry combined circulation in Scotland and Ireland; in England unions that push partners above six can destroy issue rights—though English banks are often small and would gain by union. When issues lapse, the Bank of England may take up to two-thirds of the extinguished amount; the Bank of Ireland may take the whole—another disparity Gilbart flags without defending.

The sharpest institutional difference remains power to issue notes under £5. In England that band is gold and silver coin. Amid the Russian war he notes the Russian silver ruble versus a heavily depreciated paper ruble as a caution against careless state paper, yet also treats English small-note prohibition as a reserve magazine: if foreign drain and war needs required it, reopening small country issues could release gold from domestic pockets—matter for statesmen, not for bankers alone, while Australian gold still meets foreign and domestic needs.

Closing recommendations

Gilbart’s practical programme, grounded in the statistical “laws” and in the comparative statutes, includes among other points: measuring the English maximum on a twelve-month average rather than four weeks; restoring issue rights, when Bank of England agency agreements expire (1856), to banks that had circulated Bank notes under those agreements; keeping aggregate country circulation from falling below the 1844 quantum by redistributing the roughly £706,000 already lost among issuing or non-issuing banks in the districts of the deficiency; allowing English banks, on the Scottish and Irish model, to exceed fixed issue against gold—and, gold being demandable for them, against Bank of England notes held as equivalent; letting banks of not more than six partners keep fixed issue in the same locality even if partners later exceed six, retrospectively covering unions since 1844; and allowing two or more issuing banks, whatever their partnership numbers, to unite and retain the sum of their issues. Notes under £5 he leaves to political judgment, with the war-reserve thought attached.

The appendix then prints the supporting tables—licenses and commissions of bankruptcy against country banks year by year, stamp duties on country notes, stamp quantities, Gazette and weekly circulation series, authorized and actual issues, deposit figures, and comparative monthly circulations that exhibit the seasonal laws for England, Scotland, and Ireland.
