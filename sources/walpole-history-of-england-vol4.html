A History of England from the Conclusion of the Great War in 1815, Vol. IV

A History of England from the Conclusion of the Great War in 1815, Vol. IV

Jan 01, 197011 min read

A History of England from the Conclusion of the Great War in 1815, Vol. IV — by Spencer Walpole

Vol. IV covers domestic history from the fall of the Whig Ministry (1841) to the triumph of Free Trade (1853), and foreign affairs to the Peace of Paris (1856). The volume contains six chapters (XVII–XXII) and continues the numbering from Vol. III.

CHAPTER XVII — The Effects of the Reform Act of 1832 (text starts line 1565)

Period: c. 1833–1841 (the post-Reform decade)

Walpole opens this volume with a sweeping reflection on the Reformed Parliament a decade after the Reform Act of 1832. He examines whether the fears of Tories and the hopes of Radicals had been fulfilled, concluding that both had been equally disappointed. The convulsion of 1832 subsided quickly; reformed Parliaments were not revolutionary. The characteristic features of the reformed House of Commons included: longer debates (the House sat 1,270 hours in 1833 vs. under 1,000 before reform), multiplication of Parliamentary papers (252 volumes in 1825–32 vs. 400 volumes in 1833–41), increased petitions (from 5,000 to 20,000 per year), and procedural reforms to limit debate on petitions.

Walpole provides a statistical tour of the period: Hansard swelled from 52 volumes for 1820–1830 (Series 2) to 59 volumes for 1830–1841 (Series 3). He discusses oratory in the reformed House — Peel, Russell, Stanley, Macaulay, O’Connell — and the changes in Parliamentary manners and private business. The chapter then broadens into the economic and social conditions of the time: extravagant speculations, joint-stock banks, limited liability, the commercial crisis of 1836, the general fall in prices after 1815, the distress of the working classes, the abject poverty aggravated by the Corn Laws, and the prevalence of disease in the slums.

Walpole describes the Poor Law of 1834 and the fierce agitation against it (Tory rural paternalism vs. Liberal Malthusianism), the rise of Robert Owen and his New Lanark model, Chartism in all its phases (the Charter, People’s Charter, Bull Ring riots, Newport march, Frost trial), and the Corn Law agitation. He examines the humanitarian reforms of the decade: mitigation of the criminal code, prohibition of dogs drawing carts (a reform for animal welfare inspired by Martin’s Act), prison reform, transportation reform (the probation system of 1842, modified 1847, abandoned), counsel for prisoners, abolition of imprisonment for debt, the soldier’s and sailor’s lot (distribution of medals and prize money, flogging, press gang abandoned). The chapter closes with a reflection on duelling — the decline from Lord Cardigan and Captain Tuckett’s affair through the legal and social repression of the duel — and a summary of the general characteristics of the period: “perhaps the most remarkable decade in English history” for humanitarian progress.

CHAPTER XVIII — The Formation of Peel’s Second Administration (text starts line 7598)

Period: 1841–1845

Walpole details Peel’s return to power in 1841, the difficulties he faced, and his first measures. The session of 1842 saw the renewal of the Income Tax (a temporary measure to cover the deficit), the relaxation of Protection, and the first of Gladstone’s budgets at the Board of Trade. Debates on the Budget revealed the growing divide between Protectionists and Free Traders.

The distress of the people in 1842 — strikes, the summer’s effects on prices, shrinking revenue — forced Peel to defend his policies against Tory attack (the Canada Corn Bill) and Liberal assault. Peel’s successive budgets (1842, 1843, 1844) show the gradual conversion to free trade. The conversion of Three-and-a-Half Per Cent. Stock, the alteration of Sugar Duties, and the pivotal Bank Charter Act of 1844 are examined in detail.

Walpole provides a history of banking in Great Britain: joint-stock banks, the Committee of 1836 on banking, the crisis of 1836, Peel’s views on banking, the Bank Charter Act (separating Issue and Banking Departments), the Act of 1845 for Ireland and Scotland, and the purging of the tariff (glass, auction duty, cotton, wool, sugar). The chapter documents Disraeli’s first attacks on Peel and the increasing distrust of the Prime Minister within his own party.

The second half of the chapter turns to infrastructure and invention: the railway mania and its consequences, the development of steam at sea, the tool-makers of the nineteenth century, the democratic effects of steam power, and the electric telegraph (from Thales and the Leyden jar through Galvani, Volta, Oersted, Ampère, and Faraday to the commercial telegraph). Walpole then returns to Ireland — the Poor Laws, bastardy, settlement, the employment of women and children in mines, the Factory Bill of 1843, the Factory Act of 1844, and the Ten-Hours Clause.

CHAPTER XIX — Ireland (text starts line 12926)

Period: 1841–1846

A focused chapter on Ireland, examining the English and Irish views of the Irish Question, the characteristics of English legislation towards Ireland, and the consequences of Catholic Emancipation. O’Connell’s Repeal Association, the “monster meetings,” the treatment of O’Connell in London, and the poverty of the Irish people — attributed to population increase, the absence of coal, the Relief Act of 1793, the potato, and absentee landlords.

Walpole covers the disfranchisement of the 40s. freeholders, the revival of Repeal agitation in 1842, the doctrine of nationality, Father Mathew’s temperance crusade, Peel’s Irish policy (Sugden and the Irish magistrates, the Arms Bill, coercion), Smith O’Brien, the prohibition of the Clontarf meeting, O’Connell’s trial and conviction (reversed by the Lords on a technicality), O’Connell’s death and character.

Peel’s constructive Irish measures: Irish railways, Charitable Bequests Act, changes in Irish government, and the Maynooth Grant. The chapter describes the anger of the country at the increased grant to Maynooth and Gladstone’s resignation over the issue. Middle-class education in Ireland, the Devon Commission on land tenure, Stanley’s abortive Irish Land Bill of 1845 (withdrawn in the Lords). The potato disease of 1845, its consequences, Peel’s conversion to repeal of the Corn Laws, the Cabinet meeting, the Edinburgh Letter, Peel’s resignation, Russell’s failure to form a ministry, Peel’s resumption of office, the mutiny of the Dukes, the fury of the Protectionists (led by Lord George Bentinck), obstruction in the House of Commons, the passage of the Corn Bill, and ultimately Peel’s fall.

CHAPTER XX — The Formation of the Russell Administration (text starts line 17368)

Period: 1846–1852

Russell’s administration formed June 1846. Its composition, the alteration of Sugar Duties, and the condition of Ireland form the opening. The Famine: measures for dealing with it, their failure, the discontinuance of relief works, the organisation of relief committees. The Famine in Western Scotland and the Western Highlands. The Irish Poor Law, the Encumbered Estates Act, and Bentinck’s proposal for assisting Irish Railways.

The Budget of 1847, the dissolution of Parliament, and the Commercial Crisis of 1847 — the Bank Charter suspended. Renewed outrages in Ireland, fresh Coercion measures, Somerville’s Irish Land Bill. The chapter then shifts to the European Revolutions of 1848: Smith O’Brien’s abortive rising in Ireland, suspension of Habeas Corpus, the rebellion’s collapse. Chartism in 1848: Feargus O’Connor, the collapse of the Chartist movement.

The financial and legislative history of 1848: the Panic of 1848, four successive budgets, the Disabilities of the Jews, Disraeli becoming leader of the Protectionist Party, Bentinck and the Sugar Duties. Returning prosperity, the state of Ireland in 1849 (the Battle of Dolly’s Brae, the Queen’s visit), the Rate in Aid Bill, the Irish Poor Law Amendment Bill, differences between Lords and Commons.

The Navigation Acts — their repeal in 1849. Agricultural distress in England, the Budget of 1850, Peel’s support of the ministry, and Peel’s death (after a fall from his horse on Constitution Hill). Walpole’s assessment of Peel’s character closes the chapter.

CHAPTER XXI — Religious Movements (text starts line 22525)

Period: c. 1800–1850

A thematic chapter surveying the religious history of the nineteenth century. Walpole opens with the functions of history and traces the religious movement to pre-existing causes. The effects of the Reformation comparable to Constantine’s conversion — Christianity and Paganism, Milton and Homer. The growth of doubt as the chief consequence of the Reformation, the rise of Rationalism, Galileo, the telescope enlarging man’s ideas of space, Kepler and Newton, Descartes, doubt and Deism in the eighteenth century.

The Church of the eighteenth century, the effects of the French Revolution on religion, jealousy of Rome in England, the Bible Society, geology’s challenge to Scripture (the attack on the Establishment).

Walpole then examines the parties within the Church: the Party of Resistance and the Party of Reform. The Ecclesiastical Policy of Peel: the wealth of the Church and its unequal distribution, the Bishops and their patronage, non-residence, the Ecclesiastical Commission, pluralities, the Ecclesiastical Courts, Church discipline, the Nonconformists, the Universities, Church Rates (the Braintree Case), Unitarian Chapels, the controversy between Freethought and Religion.

The Oxford Movement: Oriel College, Newman, Richard Hurrell Froude, Wilberforce, the origin of the movement, Keble’s Assize Sermon (1833), the Tractarians, Tract XC and its condemnation, the Jerusalem Bishopric, Ward’s “Ideal of a Christian Church,” Pusey, the secession to Rome, excitement and agitation in the country. The Broad Church: Hampden — his appointment as Regius Professor and as Bishop of Hereford, the Gorham Judgment, the Durham Letter (Russell’s response to Papal Aggression). Convocation. General effects of the Oxford Movement: the observance of Sunday, Sunday travelling, Sunday letters, theatres in Lent.

The Scottish Disruption: The resemblance between the Ten Years’ Conflict in Scotland and the Tractarian Movement — the history of the Reformation in Scotland, the claim of the Scotch Church, Episcopacy in Scotland, patronage, persecution, scepticism, the Act of 1712, Robertson and Chalmers. Chalmers’ views on pluralities, the effect of the Reform Act, the Call, the Veto, the Chapel Act, the Auchterarder Case, Aberdeen’s Non-Intrusion Bill, the Marnoch Case, the Duke of Argyll’s Bill, the change of ministry in 1841, the Claim, Declaration, and Protest, and the Secession of 1843. Walpole closes with the connection between the Tractarian and Disruption controversies, noting that the Scotch Movement was marked by greater earnestness.

CHAPTER XXII — Foreign Affairs (text starts line 28047)

Period: 1841–1856

Walpole surveys Britain’s foreign relations from Aberdeen’s tenure at the Foreign Office (1841–1846) through Palmerston’s return (1846–1851). The United States: the boundary dispute and the award of the King of the Netherlands (rejected by the US and ultimately by England), the arrest and release of Greely, the Caroline affair, McLeod’s Case, the Right of Search, Palmerston succeeded by Aberdeen, Lord Ashburton’s Mission, the Ashburton Treaty (1842), the Oregon Dispute.

France: the Tahiti Incident — Thouars and Queen Pomare, Thouars’ action ratified by France, Pritchard restores Pomare’s authority, the annexation of the Society Islands, Pritchard’s arrest, excitement in England, the settlement. Algiers — French ill-success in Morocco, the bombardment of Tangiers, the Entente Cordiale.

Spain and the Spanish Marriages: The mission of M. de Salvandy, the British Minister at Madrid, the candidates for the Queen’s hand, Bulwer and Bresson, Guizot’s Memorandum of 27th February, Aberdeen’s Despatch of 22nd June, Palmerston’s return and his Despatch of 19th July, the marriages celebrated. Cracow and its annexation to Austria. Portugal: the revolt of 1846, Saldanha’s coup d’état, Wylde’s Mission, Dom Miguel, Palmerston’s offer to interfere.

Switzerland: the Jesuits, the Sonderbund, the victory of the Diet. Italy: the Election of Pius IX, his liberal policy disliked by Metternich but supported by Palmerston, Lord Minto’s Mission, disturbances at Milan.

The Revolutions of 1848: the fall of Louis Philippe — reform in France, the political banquet forbidden, Lamartine, the February Revolution, the fall of Guizot, Bugeaud in command, the abdication, the provisional government, the flight of Louis Philippe. Effects of the Revolution in England and in Austria and Italy. Austria’s overtures to England, Italy’s difficulties, the Reconquest of Lombardy by Austria, Piedmont’s appeal to France, British and French mediation.

The Hungarian Revolution: Revolution in Hungary, Magyar and Slavonic jealousy, the second Revolution in Vienna, the accession of Francis Joseph, increasing excitement in Italy (war resumed), Palmerston’s Italian Policy, Russia interferes in Hungary, the Hungarians defeated, Palmerston’s policy towards Hungary, Austrian reprisals. Hungarian refugees in Turkey, the demand for their extradition. Greece: the Greek Revolution of 1843, Mr. Finlay’s Claim, other claims, Don Pacifico, the British fleet sent to Greece, its operations, the mediation of France, the renewal of coercive measures and their success, Drouyn de Lhuys recalled. The Lords censure Palmerston, the Commons approve his policy. The Great Exhibition closes the volume.
