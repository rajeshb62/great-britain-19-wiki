The History of England from the Commencement of the XIXth Century to the Crimean War, Vol. IV

The History of England from the Commencement of the XIXth Century to the Crimean War, Vol. IV

Jan 01, 197019 min read

The History of England, Vol. IV — by Harriet Martineau

Vol. IV covers 1835–1854, beginning with the later years of the second Melbourne Ministry through Queen Victoria’s accession, the Peel ministry and the Repeal of the Corn Laws, and concluding with the outbreak of the Crimean War. The volume is divided into three Books:

  • Book V — “1835–1840” — Ireland, Church affairs, agricultural distress, death of William IV, accession of Victoria, Canada (Lord Durham), Jamaica, Chartism, the Bedchamber Question, the Queen’s marriage
  • Book VI — “1841” — Peel’s ministry, the Opium War, the Afghan disaster, the Corn Laws, the Anti-Corn-Law League, the Disruption of the Scottish Church, the Irish Famine, Peel’s fall
  • Book VII — “Last Years of the Peace” — the Irish Famine (continued), the financial panic of 1847, the Revolutions of 1848, the Papal Aggression, Palmerston’s foreign policy, the Great Exhibition, and the declaration of war against Russia

Book V — “1835–1840”

Book V covers the latter half of the 1830s, dominated by the second Melbourne Ministry, the accession of Queen Victoria, and gathering social and political storms — Irish agitation, Church crises in Scotland and England, the Canadian rebellions, Chartism, agricultural distress, and the first stirrings of the free-trade movement.

Chapter VI — Ireland under Lord Mulgrave’s Administration

Martineau opens with a reflection on the hopes and heartbreaks of Irish governance during the 1835–40 period. She describes the administration of Lord Mulgrave (later Lord Normanby) as an experiment in conciliation — an attempt to govern Ireland through justice and mercy rather than coercion. The chapter captures the high expectations of the time but also carries the weight of subsequent disappointment, as the hopes for Irish redemption proved premature. The chapter concludes that even the most strenuous administration of justice was ultimately too small an instrument for Ireland’s redemption.

Chapter VII — Church and State

This chapter examines the parallel ecclesiastical disturbances shaking England, Ireland, and Scotland — viewing them as another aspect of the same transitional struggle that produced the Reform Bill. Martineau is sharply critical of successive governments’ inability to comprehend or apply principles in ecclesiastical affairs. She focuses particularly on the Church of Scotland, where the Melbourne government’s mishandling of patronage and church accommodation questions set the stage for the Disruption. The English Tractarian movement and the Dissenters’ cry for separation of Church and State are presented as parallel symptoms of the same crisis.

Chapter VIII — Agricultural Distress & Parliamentary Business

With the great constitutional questions dominating Parliament, ordinary legislative business was crowded out. The chapter opens with the Marquis of Chandos’s motion in 1835 for repeal of taxes burdening agriculture — the first of many insistent demands that would grow into the great Corn Law struggle. The session of 1835 was declared “ingloriously remarkable” for how little was transacted. This chapter covers the routine yet consequential legislative skirmishes of 1835–37 against a backdrop of deepening rural hardship.

Chapter IX — Whig Struggles & Death of William IV

Martineau paints a vivid picture of the Whig ministry’s desperation in the spring of 1837 — blocked in the Lords, abandoned by Radicals in the Commons, unable to carry their Irish measures, and unable to secure a dissolution from the King. The ministers were believed to desire resignation, the King was believed to desire their removal, yet neither acted. The chapter captures the paralysis at the heart of government before the death of William IV on June 20, 1837, which unexpectedly broke the deadlock and opened a new era.

Chapter X — The Melbourne Ministry & Queen Victoria’s Accession

Lord Lyndhurst’s single “ray of comfort” — the character of the new young Queen — proved prophetic. The chapter details the remarkable collapse of Whig power: from a majority of 300 after the Reform Bill to a precarious 26. Yet the Melbourne Ministry was allowed another trial, a decision that proved fateful. Martineau traces how the young Queen’s popularity temporarily shored up a failing administration, and how the nation’s affections turned to the girl sovereign as the one bright spot in a dispiriting political landscape. Includes the general election on the demise of the crown and the continuance of the Melbourne ministry.

Chapter XII — Canada: Lord Durham’s Mission

Martineau treats the Canadian crisis with unusual severity, declaring that “all parties might be too happy to pass over” this chapter of history if possible, and that the disgrace of the Whig ministers and opposition members over Canada is inescapable. She details Lord Durham’s mission, his famous Report, the Rebellion of 1837–38, and the constitutional crisis that forced a fundamental rethinking of colonial governance. The retribution for incapacity, presumption, and disregard of constitutional principle — on all sides — was, she insists, not yet exhausted. Covers Lord Sydenham’s governance and Lord Durham’s legacy.

Chapter XIII — Jamaica & Colonial Constitution

Canada was not the only colony in crisis. The affairs of Jamaica reached a climax in the spring of 1839 with the question of whether its constitution should be suspended. Martineau reflects with admiration on the quiet conduct of the former slaves after emancipation, contrasting it with the behavior of their former masters. The chapter examines the hostility between the Jamaican Legislature and the Executive, the Melbourne government’s attempt to suspend the constitution, and the broader implications for colonial self-government and the post-slavery order.

Chapter XIV — Dark Times: Chartism, Distress & Torchlight Meetings

The gloom of 1839–40 was the deepest since the peace. Martineau traces the causes — as usual — to the state of the crops, but devotes deeper analysis to the social convulsions that followed: Chartist torchlight meetings, the Newport Rising, machine-breaking, and the near-revolutionary atmosphere among the working classes. She observes that the men who professed to “take no interest in politics” were the same who blamed the commotions on the innate wickedness of the lower orders, missing entirely the economic roots of the unrest. This chapter forms the hinge of Book V, connecting the political failures of the Melbourne years to the social explosion that was to reshape British politics.

Chapter XV — Beneficent Legislation

Amid the darkness, Martineau finds light in three great legislative achievements: the consolidation and reform of the criminal law (making it accessible to the people who lived under it), prison reform, and the establishment of penny postage. The Penny Post, associated with Rowland Hill, receives special attention as a democratizing force that drew the nation closer together.

Chapter XVI — Privilege of Parliament

One of the last major questions debated before the Melbourne Ministry fell was the privilege of Parliament — a seemingly arcane constitutional issue that had become a deadlock between the Court of Queen’s Bench and the House of Commons. Martineau treats it with appropriate gravity even as she notes the levity with which the public and press regarded it. The chapter examines the apparent incompatibility between the privileges of the Commons and the rights of the subject, concluding with the dissolution of the 13th Parliament and the approach of the general election of 1841.

Chapter XVII — The Coronation & the Young Queen

The closing chapter of Book V returns to the figure of the young Queen Victoria — the strongest and most genial interest of the period. After the “feeble old man” William IV, Victoria’s youth and gaiety were exhilarating. Martineau captures the Queen’s early popularity: her delight in being seen, her drives through thronged parks, her Guildhall dinner, her command “Let my people see me.” The chapter covers the coronation, the Queen’s marriage to Prince Albert (February 1840), and the birth of the Princess Royal (November 1840), closing Book V on a note of cautious hope.

Book VI — “1841”

Book VI is the longest and most dramatic book of the volume, covering Peel’s great ministry (1841–1846). It traces the growing free-trade movement, the Opium War, the disastrous First Afghan War, the Disruption of the Scottish Church, O’Connell’s Repeal agitation, and the climactic struggle over the Corn Laws that ended in Peel’s conversion to free trade and the splitting of the Conservative Party. It closes with a lengthy necrology.

Chapter I — General Election of 1841

The general election of 1841 was of extraordinary importance — not because one great question was to be settled as in 1831, but because something yet more important was at stake. The Whig ministers had staked their tenure on the free-trade question, proposing a fixed duty on corn, but they were three times mistaken: the people were not ready for the struggle, the Reform Bill’s provisions still favored the landed interest, and no one trusted the Melbourne government to handle the matter. The chapter sets up the central drama of Book VI: the rise of Peel and the gathering forces that would ultimately compel him to split his party.

Chapter II — New Administration

The country watched the distribution of offices with great eagerness. The first great hope was that Peel would unite the Treasury and the Exchequer, for the pressing evil was the state of the finances — annual deficits, deepening distress, working-class hopelessness. There was regret when Goulburn was made Chancellor instead, but confidence that Peel’s mind would preside over the department. The chapter outlines the formation of Peel’s cabinet — a united, able government — and the nation’s guarded optimism.

Chapter III — Policy of China: The Opium War

Martineau provides a sweeping geographical and historical meditation on why the interior of Asia had been peaceful for a century, before turning to the crisis that shattered that peace: the Opium War with China. She traces British ignorance of China, the opium question, Lord Napier’s failed mission, Commissioner Lin’s crackdown, and the military campaigns that followed — the capture of Chusan, the Treaty of Nanking, and the opening of treaty ports. The chapter is notable for its moral ambivalence: Martineau acknowledges the wrong and violence through which free trade was introduced to China, and warns that the consequences will haunt future generations.

Chapter IV — India: Afghanistan & Lord Ellenborough

Among the difficulties Peel inherited, none was more conspicuous than the state of Indian affairs. Under Lord Auckland, the Melbourne Administration had embarked on the disastrous Afghan policy — the invasion of Afghanistan, the installation of Shah Soojah, and the catastrophic British retreat from Cabool. Martineau recounts the First Anglo-Afghan War in harrowing detail: the rising at Cabool, the murder of the Envoy (Sir William Macnaghten), the retreat and annihilation of the British force, and the rescue of the prisoners. The chapter closes with Lord Ellenborough’s controversial proclamation and subsequent recall, followed by Sir Henry Hardinge’s appointment. Includes the Gwalior campaign and the exploits of James Brooke in Borneo.

Chapter V — Condition & Fate of Parties

When Peel met Parliament at the beginning of 1842, his party strength appeared greater than any administration in years. Yet something stronger than party support was at work: an inward change in the minister’s own mind, and outward changes in the elements that create human food. The chapter surveys the political landscape — the Conservative party, the weakened Whig opposition, and the growing influence of the “Young England” movement. But the real story is the undercurrent of economic distress that was forcing a fundamental re-examination of protective tariffs.

Chapter VI — Opinion of the Session: The Corn Bill of 1842

Peel’s government took the recess to prepare their financial measures thoroughly, maintaining profound secrecy about their intentions. The chapter details the Queen’s Speech, the introduction of the new Corn Bill with its revised sliding scale, and the financial scheme that included the reimposition of the income tax — a measure that aroused fierce opposition. Includes the Poor Law Renewal Act and the Copyright Law of 1842 (Lord Mahon’s bill).

Chapter VII — Central Subject of Debate: Social Reform

Parliament reassembled in February 1843 with no increased hopefulness — distress had deepened, revenue had fallen, and agricultural interests were as alarmed as the manufacturing classes. The central subject of debate was Lord Howick’s motion on the corn laws, but the chapter ranges widely over social reform: Lord Ashley’s Mines and Collieries Act (prohibiting women and children from underground work), the Government Factory Bill with its controversial education clauses, the early closing movement, and improvements in working-class dwellings.

Chapter VIII — Movement in Ireland: O’Connell & Repeal

The chapter opens with O’Connell’s declaration in January 1841 that the Whigs had lost the hearts of the Irish people. Martineau traces the monster meetings — Tara, Clontarf — the government’s ban on the Clontarf meeting, O’Connell’s arrest, the trials, the packed jury, the conviction, and the subsequent reversal by the House of Lords. She examines O’Connell’s decline, his death (1847), and the broader Irish reform efforts: agricultural schools, the Charitable Bequests Act, the Devon Commission, the Maynooth Grant, and the new Colleges. Peel’s efforts to “kill Home Rule by kindness” are given their due, even as the potato blight loomed.

Chapter IX — Church Patronage in Scotland: The Disruption

The ecclesiastical disturbances in Scotland had by now become of the gravest import. Martineau traces the problem back to the Act of 1711, which subjected the power of the presbytery to the control of the civil courts. She recounts the Auchterarder Case, the Strathbogie Case, Lord Aberdeen’s Bill, and the Great Disruption of 1843, when over 400 ministers walked out of the General Assembly to form the Free Church of Scotland. The chapter closes with a parallel examination of the Oxford Movement — Pusey, Hampden, Ward, Oakeley, and the Tractarian secessions to Rome.

Chapter X — Canada Corn Question & the League

The harvest of 1842 was abundant. But a more significant event was Lord Stanley’s letter granting favorable terms for Canadian corn — a step that acknowledged the principle of free trade between colony and mother country. The chapter traces the confused state of parties over the Canada Corn Bill, and the growing power of the Anti-Corn-Law League under Richard Cobden’s leadership. The League’s methods — registration, the freehold land scheme, the campaign against the game laws — are examined in detail as the movement gathered force for the climactic struggle of 1846.

Chapter XII — Antagonism in Europe

The mere rumor that the Sardinian government was jealous of Mazzini’s residence in England pointed to approaching troubles in Italy and across the despotisms of Europe. Martineau surveys the European landscape: Russia’s ambitions (the Caucasus, Servia, Cracow), the turmoil in Portugal and Spain, Switzerland, Hanover, Sweden, Turkey and Egypt (the Eastern Question), and the route to India. She pays particular attention to France — the war spirit over the right of search, the Tahiti affair, the Spanish marriages, the death of the Duc d’Orléans, the return of Napoleon’s remains, and Guizot’s ministry. Concludes with American relations: the frontier dispute, the Creole affair, the Ashburton Treaty, the Oregon question.

Chapter XIII — India & Colonial Affairs

The records of Indian affairs during this period show curious minglings of progress and conflict: railway companies alongside hints that the British might soon be compelled to interfere in the Punjab. The chapter covers the Sikh invasion of 1845, the battles of the First Anglo-Sikh War, and the settlement under Sir Henry Hardinge. It then surveys colonial and international affairs: the Sandwich Islands, Van Diemen’s Land, South Australia, New Zealand, and Canadian governance under Sir Charles Bagot and Sir Charles Metcalfe.

Chapter XIV — The Corn Question: 1845

At the opening of the 1845 session, the harvest of 1844 had been good, trade was brisk, and even Ireland was quieter than usual. Then came the portents — the bad weather, the potato rot in Ireland. Martineau traces the mounting crisis: the League’s intensified agitation, Lord John Russell’s famous letter declaring his conversion to free trade, the Cabinet councils, the announcement in the Times, Peel’s resignation, Lord Grey’s obstruction, and the climactic return of Peel to power, now committed to repeal.

Chapter XV — Further Remission of Duties: 1846

The Queen’s speech of January 19, 1846, expressed satisfaction in tariff reform and recommended further remissions. Martineau details the final legislative battles: the reduction of duties on raw materials, the corn duties, relief to farmers, and the passage of the great measure through both Houses. The chapter captures the agony of the Conservative Party splitting under Peel’s leadership, the dissolution of the League, the Irish Life Bill, and finally Peel’s resignation after the defeat on the Irish Coercion Bill. The closing passage is a magnificent tribute to Peel’s last position in the British Legislature — “the noblest that, in our period of time, could be held by any man.”

Chapter XVI — Deaths: Necrology

With the charm of a youthful Sovereign and a fresh royal generation came the mournfulness of seeing the old drop off. The chapter forms a lengthy necrology covering the deaths of the period: the Duke of Sussex, the Princess Sophia of Gloucester, Lord Ashburton, Lord Wharncliffe, Lord Denman, Lord Lyndhurst, Thomas Arnold, Sydney Smith, Thomas Campbell, James Montgomery, the painter Haydon’s suicide, Turner, Wilkie, George Stephenson, Sir John Herschel’s father, Brunel, and many more. It is both a memorial and a reckoning with the passing of the generation that had shaped the post-Napoleonic world.

Book VII — “Last Years of the Peace”

Book VII carries the narrative from Peel’s retirement in 1846 to the declaration of war against Russia in 1854. It covers the Irish Famine, the financial panic of 1847, the European Revolutions of 1848, the Great Exhibition of 1851, the Papal Aggression controversy, Palmerston’s foreign policy, and the drift toward war.

Chapter I — Ireland: The Famine

This opening chapter was written when the history appeared to be closing at 1846, before the final four years of peace could be foreseen. The chapter surveys the period from Peel’s retirement (June 29, 1846) to the outbreak of the Crimean War (March 1854). The first great national interest was the famine in Ireland — the failure of the potato crop, the government’s introduction of Indian meal, the inadequacy of relief measures, the emigration, and the terrible mortality. Martineau traces the Russell ministry’s struggle with the catastrophe.

Chapter III — Financial Panic of 1847

The prosperity acknowledged in 1852 had a special significance given the trials through which other countries — and Ireland — had passed. In 1847 came severe commercial distress and a financial panic such as it had been hoped the Bank Act of 1844 would never permit again. Martineau traces the causes: the railway speculation mania of 1845, the bad harvest of 1846, the Irish famine, and the deficient cotton supply. The Bank Act was suspended to allow the issue of extra notes, and the panic gradually subsided.

Chapter IV — Continental Agitations: Revolutions of 1848

Before the commercial embarrassment was over, the political trouble of the impending revolutions had begun. Martineau surveys the revolutionary wave of 1848: the fall of Louis-Philippe in France, the upheavals in Germany, Austria, Italy, Hungary, and the impact on British politics. She examines how the Chartist movement suddenly collapsed in the face of continental events — the Kennington Common meeting, the monster petition, and the government’s firm response that revealed Chartism’s hollow core.

Chapter V — Greek Controversy, Papal Aggression & Political Changes

During the brief remnant of the years of peace, the most stirring events received their impulse from abroad. The chapter opens with the Greek Controversy of 1850 — the Don Pacifico affair, Palmerston’s gunboat diplomacy, and the great debate in which Palmerston defended his policy with the famous “Civis Romanus sum” speech. It then traces the death of Sir Robert Peel (July 1850), and the explosion of the “Papal Aggression” controversy when Pius IX restored the Roman Catholic hierarchy in England. The chapter examines the resulting Ecclesiastical Titles Bill, the wave of anti-Catholic sentiment, and the fall of the Russell ministry.

Chapter VI — Political Disturbances: Palmerston’s Fall & the Derby Ministry

The chapter traces the condition of France after the coup d’état of Louis Napoleon, the recognition of the Second Empire, and the effect on English opinion. Lord Palmerston’s retirement (after his unauthorized endorsement of the coup), the formation of the short-lived Derby Ministry (February–December 1852), the general election, the fall of Derby, and the formation of Lord Aberdeen’s coalition ministry (Christmas 1852) — all lead toward the approaching war. The chapter closes with the “delusions of the Czar” and the beginning of the Eastern crisis.

Chapter VII — The United States

While political troubles perplexed Europe, deeply interesting events were happening in the United States — the spirit of the Southern slave-power ruling at Washington. The chapter examines the Mexican War (1846–48), which Martineau presents as an unprovoked aggression driven by slavery’s lust for fresh territory. She covers the Lopez filibustering expeditions to Cuba, the gold discoveries in California, the Oregon boundary settlement, and the Compromise of 1850. The closing of the book’s main narrative reaches its climax with the declaration of war against Russia in March 1854 — “a war accepted on behalf of a good cause, and there could scarcely have been a better or more natural close to the forty years’ peace.”

Chapter VIII — Arctic Expedition, Discoveries & National Advancement

This final chapter serves as a panoramic review of national advancement during the forty years’ peace. It opens with the Arctic Expedition of 1846 — Sir John Franklin and his comrades sailing in the Erebus and Terror, never to return. The chapter then surveys the great achievements of the age: the electric telegraph, photography (“sun-painting”), Lord Rosse’s telescope, the electric discoveries of Faraday, the steam revolution and railways, the Thames Tunnel, gun-cotton, chloroform and ether, the Nineveh marbles and Layard’s discoveries, the Great Exhibition of 1851 (the “Festival of Peace”), the Portland Breakwater, the Britannia Bridge, university reform, church dissensions, royal births and deaths, and a final necrology of the notable dead (the Duke of Wellington, 1852; Napier; Bentinck; George Stephenson; Wordsworth; Moore; Southey; Coleridge; James Watt’s legacy; Brunel). The volume closes with a reflective meditation on the meaning of progress — the slow, grand advance of the human race.
