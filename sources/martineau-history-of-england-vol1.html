Martineau — History of England, Vol. I: Introduction (1790–1809)

Harriet Martineau (1802–1876) — History of England from the Commencement of the XIXth Century to the Crimean War. Vol. I. — Porter & Coates, Philadelphia, 1887 (American Edition).

The first volume of Martineau’s comprehensive history serves as an introductory prelude covering the state of Europe before the 19th century, the Revolutionary and Napoleonic Wars (1793–1815), the Peace of Amiens, the rise of Napoleon, Pitt’s second ministry and the Third Coalition, Trafalgar and Austerlitz, Wellesley’s Indian campaigns, the Ministry of All the Talents and the abolition of the slave trade, and the troubled Portland administration (1807–1809) with the Copenhagen expedition, the Peninsular War beginning at Vimeiro and Corunna, the Duke of York scandal, and the Regency crisis. The volume closes with an extensive survey of cultural and intellectual life in the first decade of the century.

The text is 25,719 lines, OCR’d from the University of California library copy (ark:/13960/t83j85f5s).

All chapters reported in the Table of Contents are present in the raw text. (Chapters V of Book I and Book II are present — the OCR renders the Roman numeral “V” as the letter “Y” in running headers, which can cause false reports of missing text.)

Content Summary

  • Preface to the American Edition — a note on the republication, explaining that the original work ended in 1846 and has been extended to the Crimean War; also an editorial note on the division of labour between the original authors

BOOK I — Background to the 19th Century (to 1806)

  • Chapter I — The Balancing System — the state of Europe before the 19th century: the balance-of-power system and its history in the 18th century, Russia under Paul I, Austria, Prussia, England’s supremacy at sea, France after the Revolution, the rise of Bonaparte as First Consul. England’s proposal of peace to France and the rejection by Bonaparte, French successes in Italy and Egypt, the rupture between England and France, and the challenges of a new kind of warfare and politics

  • Chapter II — The New Century Opens (1801) — the state of England at the opening of the 19th century: Pitt’s ministry, the Catholic question dividing the Cabinet, the King’s first serious illness, the Northern Confederacy (Russia, Prussia, Sweden, Denmark), Nelson at Copenhagen (April 2, 1801), the Egyptian expedition and the Battle of Aboukir, the death of Sir Ralph Abercromby. Pitt’s resignation over the Catholic question, Addington’s ministry formed, peace negotiations with France, the Treaty of Amiens (March 1802), and the general sense of relief and hope at the arrival of peace

  • Chapter III — Ireland and Domestic Affairs — the Irish Union (1801) and its aftermath, continuing discontents in Ireland, French tampering with Irish malcontents, Robert Emmett’s conspiracy (1803), the murder of Lord Kilwarden during Emmett’s rising, coercion acts passed, currency troubles and the state of the Irish economy. During the precarious peace: the Bantry Bay mutiny, English travellers flocking to the Continent, the prosecution of Peltier for libel against Bonaparte, French aggressions in Italy and Switzerland, the renewal of war (May 1803), and the seizure of Hanover by the French

  • Chapter IV — The Addington Ministry — the Prince of Wales’s financial affairs and his disputes with the King, English detained in France at the rupture of the peace, first naval captures of French and Spanish ships, Colonel Despard’s plot for insurrection (hanged 1803), the execution of Governor Wall for murder, the state of party politics, the Grenville letter exposing the weakness of the ministry, the King’s second illness, the defence debates, the volunteer movement, and the fall of Addington’s ministry under accumulating criticism. Pitt returns as War Minister (May 1804)

  • Chapter V — The New Parliament and Pitt’s Second Ministry — the general election of 1804, Pitt’s second administration formed, the rupture with Spain (the seizure of the treasure fleet), the reconciliation of Pitt and Addington, the additional force bill for raising troops, Lord Melville’s impeachment for mismanagement of the navy (the Tenth Report scandal — Melville acquitted 1805, but politically ruined), Napoleon crowned Emperor, the formation of the Third Coalition with Austria and Russia, Nelson in the Mediterranean

  • Chapter VI — Napoleon and the War — Napoleon’s Imperial coronation, the additional force bill, the continental alliances against France, the catamaran expedition against Boulogne (failed attempt to destroy the invasion flotilla), relations with Spain and the seizure of Spanish treasure ships, Mack’s surrender at Ulm (October 1805), the French army in Vienna, Nelson’s pursuit of the combined fleet, the Battle of Trafalgar (October 21, 1805) and Nelson’s death, the Battle of Austerlitz (December 2, 1805) and the destruction of the Third Coalition, the Austrian treaty of Pressburg, Pitt’s illness and death (January 1806)

  • Chapter VII — Wellesley in India — Arthur Wellesley’s administration and military campaigns in India under his brother the Governor-General Marquess Wellesley, the subsidiary system described, the Mahratta confederacy and its five chiefs (Scindia, Holkar, the Peshwa, the Gaikwar, the Bhonsla), the Mahratta War, the Battle of Assye (September 1803) with the 74th and 78th Highlanders, the Battle of Argaum, General Lake’s campaign in Hindustan, the assault on Delhi, the restoration of the blind Mogul Emperor Shah Alam, the Battle of Laswarree and the destruction of the Mahratta artillery, the results of the campaigns consolidating British power, Cornwallis as Governor-General and his death, the recall of Marquess Wellesley

  • Chapter VIII — The Ministry of All the Talents (1806–1807) — the meeting of Parliament after Pitt’s death, the King’s well-known dislike of Fox, Lord Grenville and Charles James Fox forming the coalition ministry, the negotiations for peace with France (failed), the reprobation of the slave trade by the Commons on Fox’s motion, Wilberforce’s continuing parliamentary campaign, the colonial slave trade prohibition in 1806, the acquittal of Lord Melville on his impeachment, Fox’s illness and death (September 1806), the Battle of Maida (July 1806) in Calabria, the recovery of the Cape of Good Hope from the Dutch, Sir Home Popham’s unauthorised expedition to Buenos Ayres (captured and lost), the humiliation of Prussia after Jena and Auerstädt

  • Chapter IX — The Fall of the Talents — the dissolution of parties after Pitt’s death, the dissolution of parliament and the general election of 1806, the state of the army and navy and the country’s resources, the financial scheme of Lord Henry Petty (later Lord Lansdowne), the abolition of the slave trade (Act of March 1807, effective May 1), the re-emergence of the Catholic question — the proposal to admit Catholics to the higher ranks of the army and navy, the Irish Act of 1793 limiting Catholic eligibility, the King’s demand that the Cabinet pledge never to concede further Catholic emancipation, the King’s retractation of his promise and the dismissal of the Talents, the “No Popery” cry in the general election of 1807, and the return of an anti-Catholic majority

BOOK II — The Portland Administration (1807–1809)

  • Chapter I — The Portland Administration — the formation of the Duke of Portland’s nominal administration (the real power with Perceval, Canning, Castlereagh, Eldon, and Liverpool), the dissolution of Parliament and the general election of 1807, the King’s deep prejudices against Catholic relief, the character and influence of Lord Eldon Lord Chancellor and Lord Malmesbury, the changing tone of political discourse with Cobbett and Burdett emerging as tribunes of popular opinion, the weakness and internal disunity of the Cabinet

  • Chapter II — Copenhagen and the Peninsular Beginnings — Napoleon in northern Europe after Tilsit, the secret Treaty of Tilsit between France and Russia, the Copenhagen expedition (August–September 1807): the British demand that Denmark surrender its fleet, the refusal, the bombardment of Copenhagen, the surrender of the Danish fleet (18 ships of the line, 15 frigates), the moral controversy in England over attacking a neutral power, Napoleon’s Berlin and Milan Decrees establishing the Continental System, the Peninsular War begins with the French invasion of Portugal and Spain, Sir Arthur Wellesley sent to Portugal, the Battle of Vimeiro (August 1808), the controversial Convention of Cintra (allowing the defeated French to evacuate Portugal with their arms and plunder), the recall of Wellesley and the Board of Inquiry

  • Chapter III — The Duke of York and the War — the Duke of York as Commander-in-Chief, the scandal of his mistress Mary Anne Clarke selling army commissions through her influence, Colonel Wardle’s investigation in the House of Commons, Mrs. Clarke’s sensational revelations before the parliamentary committee, the Duke of York’s resignation as Commander-in-Chief, the trial of the Duke of York, the close of 1809 — a year that had exhausted the nation’s confidence in its military leadership

  • Chapter IV — The State of the Nation — the opposing views of the ministerial and opposition parties on the condition of the country, parliamentary debates on the conduct of the war and its cost, the financial strain of the war with falling revenue and rising deficits, the state of agriculture, manufactures, and commerce under the weight of war taxation, the condition of the working classes, the debates on the budget and the raising of supplies

  • Chapter V — The Walcheren Expedition — the disastrous Walcheren expedition of 1809: the largest British amphibious operation of the war conceived to destroy the French fleet at Antwerp, the landing on Walcheren island, the capture of Flushing, the failure to push on to Antwerp, the outbreak of Walcheren fever (malaria) that devastated the army, the evacuation and the loss of 4,000 men to disease, the parliamentary inquiry and the political damage to the ministry, Canning and Castlereagh’s mutual recriminations over responsibility

  • Chapter VI — The National Consultations — the general election after the scandals and disasters of 1809, the attempt to form a broader administration, Perceval’s ministry formed (October 1809) — a weak government without the leading figures of either party, the debates on the state of the nation, the parliamentary opposition led by Lord Grey and Lord Grenville, attempts to criticise the conduct of the war

  • Chapter VII — Governing the Country — the immense difficulty of government in 1810 with a hated ministry and a failing King, the Regency question emerges as the King’s health deteriorates, detailed account of George III’s final descent into mental illness (his last period of sanity ending October 1810), the medical and political crisis over the King’s incapacity, the Prince of Wales’s position (not yet formally Regent), the debates on restrictions on the Regent’s powers

  • Chapter VIII — The King’s Illness — the steady and final decline of George III into incurable mental illness, the growing hopelessness of his recovery (the King would never again resume government), the Regency Bill of 1811 debated at length — restrictions on the Prince Regent’s powers for a period of one year (including the power to create peers and grant offices), the Prince Regent’s attitude and his abandonment of his Whig friends, Spencer Perceval continues as Prime Minister with the Regency

  • Chapter IX — Napoleon’s Return and the Peninsular War — Napoleon’s return to Paris from Spain after the Austrian war, the Peninsular campaign renewed, Sir John Moore’s advance into Spain and the strategic situation, the French pursuit under Soult and Napoleon, the terrible retreat to Corunna in deep winter, the discipline and suffering of the army, the Battle of Corunna (January 16, 1809), Moore’s death and burial, the evacuation of the British army by sea, the national mourning for Moore and the recognition of his achievement in saving the army

  • Chapter X — Explorations and the Wider World — the history of a people in wartime — a cultural survey of the first decade of the century: Mungo Park’s second African expedition (his death on the Niger, 1806), Lord Macartney’s embassy to China, the state of scientific and geographical knowledge in 1809, the Royal Institution and the advancement of science, the Edinburgh Review and the Quarterly Review, the Lake poets (Wordsworth, Coleridge, Southey), the essayists (Hazlitt, Lamb, Sydney Smith), the state of the arts and literature at the close of the decade, the condition of the people after ten years of war
