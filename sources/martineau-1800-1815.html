Martineau — History of England, A.D. 1800-1815 (Introduction to the Peace)

Harriet Martineau (1802–1876) — History of England, A.D. 1800-1815: being an introduction to The history of the peace. — London: George Bell & Sons, 1878 edition.

[A separate single-volume work from Martineau’s larger History of England from the Commencement of the XIXth Century to the Crimean War (4 vols.). This volume was written as an introduction to her History of the Peace and covers the period from the opening of the 19th century through the Battle of Waterloo and the Second Treaty of Paris. It fills the 1807–1815 gap left by the larger work’s first volume, which only reaches 1809.]

The text is 33,563 lines, OCR’d from an Archive.org scan of the Harvard College Library copy (ARL: kat: T1431375).

Relationship to Martineau’s Larger Work

The existing source page martineau-history-of-england-vol1.md covers Vol. I of Martineau’s 4-volume work (1790–1809), with Book I (1800–1807) and Book II (1807–1809). That volume’s Book II ends with the Portland Administration’s collapse, Moore’s death at Corunna, and the cultural survey of ~1809. This separate volume carries the story forward to 1815:

  • Vol. I (4-vol. set) → ends ~1809 (Portland, Moore, Walcheren, Duke of York scandal)
  • This volume → covers ALL of 1800–1815, with Book II (Ch I–X) covering 1807–1815 in far greater detail than Vol. I, including the full Peninsular War (through to Toulouse), the War of 1812, Luddism, Perceval’s assassination, and Waterloo.

There is some overlap in Book I (1800–1807), where both volumes cover the same ground from Pitt’s resignation through the Ministry of All the Talents. Book II of this volume is where the principal new material lies.

Content Summary

BOOK I — 1800–1807 (pp. 1–248)

Chapter I: Balancing System [1800]

State of Europe at the close of the 18th century. Martineau opens with the European Balance of Power and the great powers — Russia, Austria, Prussia, England, France. The French Revolution shatters the old equilibrium. Napoleon Bonaparte’s rise to First Consul is presented as both symptom and cause of the new era. The narrative pivots to England’s condition under Pitt: sedition at home, financial strain from the war, naval mutinies, and the smouldering Irish crisis. A detailed portrait of the middle and industrial classes, the harshness of the criminal law, and the subsistence of the poor closes the chapter.

Chapter II: Pitt, Catholic Question, Copenhagen, Aboukir, Peace of Amiens [1801–1802]

Pitt’s resignation, Nelson at Copenhagen, Abercromby in Egypt, the Peace of Amiens. Martineau offers a damning assessment of British statesmanship, calling it a wonder the nation survived the intrigues about the throne. The Catholic Question fractures Pitt’s Cabinet; the King’s first serious illness throws government into crisis. The Northern Confederacy (Russia, Prussia, Sweden, Denmark) is formed; Nelson forces the Battle of Copenhagen (2 April 1801). Meanwhile Abercromby’s expedition defeats the French at Aboukir, though Abercromby falls on the field. Pitt resigns over the King’s refusal to concede Catholic Emancipation. Addington succeeds him and negotiates the Treaty of Amiens (March 1802). A fragile peace, widely mistrusted, is achieved.

Chapter III: Irish Union, Emmett Rebellion, Coercion [1801–1806]

Ireland under the Union, Robert Emmett’s rising. As the Union flag was hoisted on 1 January 1801, the Catholic Question remained unresolved. The Union ended the domination of the great Irish families but the religious quarrel grew more formidable. A mysterious paper, purportedly from Pitt, inflamed Catholic expectations of emancipation that were never fulfilled. The Emmett conspiracy: Robert Emmett’s plot, the outbreak in Dublin, the murder of Lord Kilwarden, and the rebellion’s swift collapse. Martineau dwells on the savage repression that followed. The Duke of Bedford’s viceroyalty and Irish currency troubles compound the distress.

Chapter IV: Precarious Peace, War Declared, Despard’s Plot [1800–1804]

The collapse of the Peace of Amiens, renewal of war, Colonel Despard’s conspiracy. The rejoicing at peace gave way to misgivings before the treaty was concluded. The Bantry Bay mutiny, Addington’s weakness, French provocations (the Peltier affair), and a spectacular Stock Exchange hoax precede the declaration of war. English travellers caught in France by the rupture, the first naval captures, the loss of Hanover, and Colonel Despard’s revolutionary conspiracy in London (he is executed with his accomplices). Governor Wall is also executed for past cruelties. The fragile Addington ministry is exposed.

Chapter V: Grenville Letter, King’s Illness, Addington Falls [1801–1804]

The intercepted Grenville letter, the King’s madness, Pitt’s return. A captured East Indiaman yields Lord Grenville’s frank correspondence revealing the state of parties and the conviction that Addington must go. The King’s anxieties and recurring illness are described with sympathy. Debates on the defence of the country expose the ministry’s paralysis. Addington resigns and Pitt returns as Prime Minister. The loss of West India ships to Admiral Linois and solemn war ceremonials in London mark the chapter’s close.

Chapter VI: Napoleon Emperor, Trafalgar, Austerlitz, Pitt’s Death [1804–1806]

Napoleon’s coronation, the great battles, the death of Pitt. Napoleon’s elevation to Emperor (May 1804, coronation 1 December). The Additional Force Bill, the Catamaran expedition against Boulogne, the seizure of Spanish treasure ships, war with Spain. Lord Melville’s impeachment over naval administration (the Tenth Report scandal). Then the great battles: Nelson’s pursuit of the combined fleet, Trafalgar (21 October 1805), Nelson’s death and character. On land: Mack’s surrender at Ulm, the French in Vienna, Austerlitz (2 December 1805) shatters the Third Coalition. Pitt, broken by the news, falls ill and dies on 23 January 1806.

Chapter VII: Wellesley in India, Mahratta War, Assaye [1801–1806]

Arthur Wellesley’s Indian campaigns. The Mahratta confederacy and its five great chiefs (Scindia, Holkar, the Peshwa, the Gaikwar, the Bhonsla). The Mahratta War: Wellesley in the Deckan, the astonishing victory at Assaye (1803) against overwhelming odds, Argaum, the destruction of the Mahratta infantry. General Lake’s campaign in Hindustan: the battle of Delhi, restoration of the blind Mughal Emperor, the battle of Laswarree. The war’s results — the break-up of the Mahratta empire, new treaties establishing British supremacy. Lord Cornwallis returns as Governor-General but dies soon after.

Chapter VIII: Ministry of All the Talents, Fox, Slave Trade [1806–1807]

The Grenville-Fox coalition, the abolition of the slave trade. With Pitt dead, the King reluctantly accepts a ministry under Lord Grenville with Charles James Fox as Foreign Secretary. Fox’s ill-fated peace negotiations with France fail. The reprobation of the slave trade in Parliament, Wilberforce’s campaign, the colonial slave trade prohibition. Fox’s illness and death (September 1806). The war in Europe: Maida in Calabria, the recapture of the Cape of Good Hope, the disastrous Buenos Ayres expedition. Prussia lies humiliated by Napoleon. The Slave Trade Abolition Act is passed (March 1807) — a moral triumph — but the ministry collapses under the Catholic Question.

Chapter IX: Fall of the Talents, Abolition, “No Popery” [1806–1807]

The dismissal of the Talents and Tory reaction. The King demands pledges against further Catholic relief; the ministry refuses and is dismissed. The Duke of Portland forms a nominal administration. The “No Popery” cry sweeps the general election of 1807, returning an anti-Catholic majority. The short-lived Grenville ministry — the “Ministry of All the Talents” — ends, having achieved the abolition of the slave trade. The country returns to Tory rule with the long war still unresolved. Book I closes.

BOOK II — 1807–1815 (pp. 249–537)

Chapter I: Portland Administration, Copenhagen, Tilsit, Peninsular Opening [1807]

A feeble ministry thrust into a European crisis. Martineau opens with a blistering indictment: the Portland Administration’s imbecility was admitted even by its friends. The Duke of Portland himself sat for hours drugged with laudanum; the Cabinet contained Hawkesbury and Castlereagh, “two of the weakest men in public life.” Into this vacuum came Napoleon’s Berlin Decree and the Continental System. After Tilsit (July 1807), Napoleon and Alexander divide Europe. England responds by seizing the Danish fleet at Copenhagen — a bombardment and surrender Martineau examines with moral ambivalence. The Dardanelles and Egyptian expeditions are followed by the Russian declaration of war and the loss of the Swedish alliance. Napoleon invades Portugal; the Portuguese royal family flees to Brazil. The Spanish Bourbons are enticed to Bayonne. The Peninsular War opens.

Chapter II: Peninsular War, Moore’s Campaign, Corunna, Walcheren, Duke of York Scandal, Castlereagh-Canning Duel [1807–1809]

The disasters of 1808–1809. Moore’s heroic death, Walcheren catastrophe, the Cabinet tears itself apart. Napoleon’s entanglement with the Spanish Bourbons: Godoy’s intrigues, Ferdinand at Bayonne, the Madrid tumult, the Bourbon renunciation. Wellesley’s initial victories and the controversial Convention of Cintra. Sir John Moore’s daring advance into Spain, the terrible winter retreat to Corunna, Moore’s death on the field and burial. The Walcheren expedition — the largest British amphibious operation of the war, a catastrophe of mismanagement and fever that decimated the army. Naval successes under Collingwood and his death. At home: the Duke of York’s mistress Mary Anne Clarke sells commissions; a parliamentary inquiry forces his resignation. The rivalry between Castlereagh and Canning culminates in their duel on Putney Heath. Perceval becomes Prime Minister amid national gloom.

Chapter III: O.P. Question, Bullion Committee, Burdett in Tower, Regency Crisis [1810–1812]

Theatre riots, currency debates, a radical sent to the Tower, and the King’s final madness. The O.P. (Old Prices) riots at Covent Garden — theatregoers rioting for months against raised prices — illustrate the quarrelsome temper of a war-weary nation. The commercial crash, the Bullion Committee’s report on currency and the paper pound, early reform efforts under Romilly and Mackintosh. Sir Francis Burdett’s privilege dispute with the Commons: he defends the right to publish parliamentary debates and is committed to the Tower — a constitutional crisis that grips London. The Princess Amelia’s death (November 1810) triggers George III’s final descent into incurable madness. Parliament adjourns repeatedly; the Regency question dominates politics.

Chapter IV: Restrictions on the Regent, Perceval’s Assassination [1811–1812]

The restricted regency, negotiations fail, Perceval shot dead in the Commons. The ministers hope for the King’s recovery and limit the Prince Regent’s powers; the Whigs expect to return to office. The Prince grows estranged from his old Whig allies. When the restriction expires, he invites Lords Grenville and Grey to form a government — then reneges after their refusal to accept patronage limitations. Perceval continues as Prime Minister. On 11 May 1812, a deranged bankrupt named John Bellingham shoots Spencer Perceval dead in the lobby of the House of Commons. The shock and confusion are profound. Parliament provides for Perceval’s family. The country is adrift, the Peninsular War floundering, with no clear successor.

Chapter V: Luddism, Frame-Breaking, Luddite Acts [1811–1814]

The machine-breaking crisis. Commercial distress, Luddite risings, Lord Sidmouth’s repression. Nothing in living memory compared to the distress of 1811–12. The collapse of foreign commerce, the failure of manufacturing credit, wheat at 112s. a quarter. The factor system broke the relationship between employer and operative. Into this misery came Luddism: frame-breaking in Nottinghamshire, the destruction of machinery by stocking-weavers. The Luddite Acts made frame-breaking capital. Lord Sidmouth as Home Secretary deployed spies, troops, and mass prosecutions. The movement spread to Yorkshire and Lancashire. A plentiful harvest in 1813 and renewed continental trade brought a temporary lull, but by 1814 Luddism resumed as the agricultural crash brought down 240 of 700 country banks.

Chapter VI: Full Peninsular War — Talavera, Torres Vedras, Badajoz, Salamanca, Vittoria, Toulouse [1809–1814]

Wellington’s six-year campaign from Lisbon to Toulouse. Parliament resolves to persevere after Moore’s catastrophe. Wellesley lands at Lisbon on 22 April 1809 — he would not need his ships again until the pacification of Europe. Talavera (1809, after which Wellesley becomes Wellington), the grim defensive Lines of Torres Vedras, Busaco, the expulsion of the French from Portugal. Albuera, the sieges of Ciudad Rodrigo and Badajoz (stormed with terrible loss), Salamanca, the occupation of Madrid, the failure at Burgos, and the harrowing retreat. Napoleon’s Russian campaign shifts the balance. Wellington is made Commander-in-Chief of the allied armies in Spain. Vittoria (1813) breaks French power; Wellington invades France. The campaign of 1814 ends with the storming of Toulouse — fought after Napoleon had already abdicated, costing 8,000 lives from delayed news. Wellington enters Paris as ambassador.

Chapter VII: War of 1812, Treaty of Ghent [1812–1815]

The “bootless war” with America. Martineau is scathing: “as purposeless, as foolish, as unnecessary, as it was ill-managed, useless, and discreditable.” The Orders in Council and the Right of Search were the ostensible causes. The war ends without settling either. The burning of Washington (1814) is the most dramatic British success. The Mississippi expedition, the Battle of New Orleans (fought after peace was signed), and the capture of Fort Mobile. The Treaty of Ghent leaves everything as before the war — no better terms than those rejected in 1808. Martineau closes with the verdict that history should tear out “that page which must contain the narrative of the bootless war of 1812–15.”

Chapter VIII: Princess Charlotte, Press (Cobbett/Hunt), East India Charter, Finance [1812–1814]

The Regent’s unhappy family, the radical press, India, finance. As the King’s recovery becomes hopeless, attention fixes on the Princess Charlotte, heir to the throne, and her troubled relationship with her father the Regent. Charlotte’s suitors (the Prince of Orange), her flight to her mother, and her eventual marriage to Prince Leopold of Saxe-Coburg. A survey of the press: the Morning Chronicle under Perry, William Cobbett’s Political Register, the Hunts (Leigh and John) of the Examiner. The Printers’ Name Bill and prosecutions for seditious libel. The renewal of the East India Company’s Charter (1813) opens India to missionaries and establishes church establishment there. Bible societies, the prophetess Joanna Southcote, a Stock Exchange fraud, and the burning of the Custom House (1814).

Chapter IX: Napoleon’s Fall, Hundred Days, Waterloo [1813–1815]

Leipzig, the abdication, Elba, the Hundred Days, Waterloo. A month after Napoleon’s return from Russia, the remnants of the Grande Armée drift back to Paris. The renewed Allied compact, the spring campaign, the armistice, Austria’s declaration of war, Dresden, Leipzig (the Battle of the Nations) shatters Napoleon’s German Empire. The Allies cross the Rhine; Paris capitulates. Napoleon abdicates at Fontainebleau, attempts suicide, departs for Elba. The Bourbons return; the Treaty of Paris is signed. Then the Hundred Days: Napoleon’s escape, his march to Paris, the renewal of war. Quatre-Bras, Ligny, and Waterloo (18 June 1815). Martineau describes Wellington’s victory and Napoleon’s final exile to St. Helena. The Second Treaty of Paris (November 1815). Wellington’s farewell: “England is still at Peace.”

Chapter X: Cultural Survey, Steam Navigation, Necrology [1801–1815]

Steam, the arts, the reviews, science, and the dead of the period. Martineau traces the early development of steam navigation — Fulton’s Hudson River success (1807), Symington, Henry Bell’s Comet (1811) — and Boulton and Watt’s innovations. Chain cables, steam carriages, Trevithick. The Edinburgh Review (1802) and the Quarterly Review (1809) are discussed with ambivalence. The Edinburgh Review’s tyranny of literary opinion is contrasted with its service to liberal causes. Bentham, Herschel, Davy, Dalton, Wollaston, Leslie, Cavendish, Maskelyne represent science. The artists (Boydell, Morland, Barry, Opie, Hoppner, De Loutherbourg), the authors (Home, Cumberland, Percy, Porson, Dibdin, Dr. Burney), the clergymen (Horsley, Hurd), philologists (Horne Tooke), philosophers (Paley, condemned for shallowness), philanthropists (Granville Sharp), diplomats (Lord Macartney), and explorers (Mungo Park). The book closes with Park’s mysterious death on the Niger.

Necrology (1800–1815)

Key deaths noted in Chapter X: Nelson (1805, Trafalgar), Pitt (1806), Fox (1806), Sir Ralph Abercromby (1801, Aboukir), Sir John Moore (1809, Corunna), Spencer Perceval (1812, assassinated), the Duke of Portland (1809), Lord Collingwood (1810), Lord Melville (acquitted 1805 but politically ruined, his actual death not in this period), Princess Amelia (1810), George III (living at close of volume — he would die in 1820).
