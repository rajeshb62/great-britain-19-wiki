Martineau — History of England, Vol. II: From the Congress of Vienna to the End of Lord Liverpool’s Government (1815–1826)

Harriet Martineau (1802–1876) — History of England from the Commencement of the XIXth Century to the Crimean War. Vol. II. — 1864 Edition.

The second volume covers the decade of peace and unrest that followed the Napoleonic Wars, from the Congress of Vienna (1815) through the Liverpool administration down to the commercial crisis of 1825–26. Martineau traces the great political, economic, and social struggles of the period: the post-war depression, the Corn Laws, the radical agitation culminating in Peterloo, the Queen Caroline affair, the emergence of Canning, the Catholic question, and the speculation mania of 1825.

The text is 25,422 lines, OCR’d from the University of California library copy (ark:/13960/t0wp9wh71).

Content Summary

BOOK I — From the Settlement of Europe to the Accession of George IV (1815–1820)

  • Chapter I — The Congress of Vienna and the Peace — the settlement of Europe after Waterloo: the Congress of Vienna’s territorial rearrangements, the second Treaty of Paris, the Holy Alliance and the principles of the allies, the return of the Bourbons, Castlereagh’s navigation of the competing interests of Russia, Austria, and Prussia, Britain’s enormous war debt and the challenge of demobilisation, the state of parties at home — Eldon and Liverpool, the opposition led by Lord Grey, Lord Grenville, and Henry Brougham, the abolitionist movement, Paris in the autumn of 1815, commerce and territorial limits settled by peace, the general results of the peace settlement

  • Chapter II — The Prince Regent’s Speech and the New Parliament (1816) — Castlereagh’s triumphal reception in the Commons after Vienna, the Prince Regent’s speech praising the establishment of peace, the House of Commons of 1816 and the presumption of the government, the Property Tax debate — Brougham and Lord John Russell demanding rigid economy, Castlereagh’s infamous remark about the people’s “ignorant impatience” to be relieved of taxation, the defeat of the income tax, the civil list and the expenses of the royal family, the character and expenditure of the Prince Regent

  • Chapter III — Agriculture, Corn Laws, and Currency — post-war agricultural distress from the collapse of wartime prices, the Corn Law of 1815 (prohibiting import of foreign wheat until domestic price reached 80s.), the effects of the Corn Laws on the price of provisions, the burden of tithes and poor rates on the land, the state of manufactures and commerce in the depression, the currency question and the controversy over resuming cash payments, Mr. Horner on currency and the Bullion Committee’s recommendations, petitions from the agricultural counties, Ricardo’s monetary theories

  • Chapter IV — Poverty, Discontent, and Sedition — the collapse of the wartime economy, high food prices, demobilisation of soldiers and sailors and the failure of employment, post-war poverty and its manifestations, frame-breaking in the Midlands, private benevolence and charitable efforts, the legacy of Luddism, early reform meetings and the government’s growing fear that all popular political organisation was seditious

  • Chapter V — The Rise of the Radical Movement — the emergence of mass radicalism in 1816–17, the development of a politically conscious middle class, parliamentary reform debates revived, William Cobbett’s writings in the “twopenny trash” Political Register, the Hampden Clubs and Major Cartwright’s provincial tours, the Spencean Philanthropists (followers of Thomas Spence who advocated the common ownership of land), the Spa Fields meeting of December 1816, Orator Hunt’s rise, the riot subdued, the real dangers facing the government, novelty of female reform societies

  • Chapter VI — The Barbary States and the Algerine Expedition — Lord Exmouth’s 1816 naval expedition against Algiers to suppress Barbary piracy and end Christian slavery, the bombardment of Algiers, the second Algerine expedition, the treaties with Algiers, Tunis, and Tripoli, the release of Christian captives, Britain’s Mediterranean naval power projection and the international effort to suppress piracy, a diversion from domestic affairs

  • Chapter VII — Social Improvement and Reform — the progress of social improvement in the darkest years of the post-war depression, Sir Samuel Romilly and the reform of the criminal law (his campaign to reduce the number of capital offences), the new police system emerging, gas-lighting in London’s streets, mendicity and vagrancy, Poor Law administration and the system of allowances (Speenhamland system), the workhouse, elementary education and the Education Commission, the founding of savings-banks, the Elgin Marbles purchased for the nation, Elizabeth Fry’s prison work, the Society for the Diffusion of Useful Knowledge, Sunday Schools, Robert Owen’s New Lanark experiment, the Bible Society and missionary organisations

  • Chapter VIII — The Spanish American Colonies — the revolutions in Spanish America (Bolívar, San Martín, O’Higgins), British policy toward the independence movements, the debates over recognising the new republics, English merchants financing the revolutionaries, the strategic interest in breaking Spain’s colonial monopoly, parties in Mexico, New Granada, Venezuela, the drought and earthquakes, Paraguay under Francia, the independence of Chili and Brazil, Castlereagh’s cautious neutrality contrasted with Canning’s later assertiveness

  • Chapter IX — The Conspiracies of 1817 — the wave of alarms in 1817, the outrage on the Prince Regent (the “popgun plot”), the reports of secret committees on the state of the country, the suspension of Habeas Corpus (February 1817), the March of the Blanketeers (Manchester weavers marching to London with blankets to petition), the Derby Insurrection (Pentridge Rising), Oliver the Spy — government agent provocateur, Jeremiah Brandreth’s rising and execution, the evidence of Samuel Bamford, the results of the trials, parliamentary protests by Romilly against the suppression of civil liberties

  • Chapter X — The Government’s Repression — systematic repression after 1817: Lord Sidmouth’s circular authorising magistrates to seize and detain persons suspected of publishing seditious or blasphemous libels, the flight of William Cobbett to America, the trial of William Hone for blasphemous parodies (his brilliant three-day self-defence before Lord Ellenborough, resulting in acquittal — three juries in three successive days acquitted Hone), the second and third trials of Hone, the Seditious Meetings Act, the general atmosphere of political repression

  • Chapter XI — Death of the Princess Charlotte — the death of Princess Charlotte (only legitimate child of the Prince Regent) in November 1817 after giving birth to a stillborn son, the nationwide grief and mourning, the dynastic crisis — no direct heir to the throne, the scramble among the royal dukes to marry and produce an heir, the abolition of sinecures, Charlotte’s significance as a symbol of hope for a happier monarchy

  • Chapter XII — India: The Pindarree and Mahratta Wars — British campaigns under the Marquess of Hastings, the Pindarrees (bands of freebooters ravaging central India), their leader Cheetoo, the ravages of the Pindarrees, the Pindarree War and extirpation of the robbers, the third Anglo-Maratha War (1817–1818), the Battle of Kirkee, the siege of Nagpur, the Battle of Mehidpore, the ravages of cholera-morbus, the Battle of Corregaum, the defeat and submission of the Peshwa Bajee Rao, the attack on Fort Talnair, the surrender of Bajee Rao, the restoration of peace, the happy effects of British rule as Martineau presents them, rapid improvements in administration and revenue, accessions of territory

  • Chapter XIII — The Session of 1818 — the general election of 1818 preceded by this session, the Prince Regent’s speech and the debates on the address, the state of the country — which side should be believed about the condition of the people, the reports of secret committees concerning seditious practices, the renewal of the Alien Act, the debates on resumption of cash payments, agricultural distress, the Bank Charter Act, the fall of the income tax (forced by overwhelming opposition), Peel’s first Factory Act (limiting hours of child labour in cotton mills — the beginning of factory legislation), the renewed discussion of the Poor Laws

  • Chapter XIV — The General Election of 1818, the Manchester Strike, and the Congress of Aix-la-Chapelle — This chapter is present in the text (line 10785). The general election of 1818: the Westminster contest (Romilly at the head of the poll with 5,339 votes, Burdett 5,238, Sir Murray Maxwell 4,808 after a riotous fifteen-day election — Maxwell severely injured by the mob, the Riot Act read and the military called out), the London city election, Brougham’s contest in Westmoreland against the Lowther family, 115 contested seats out of 380 constituencies, the Whig resurgence under Tierney’s leadership. The strike of Manchester cotton spinners (about 15,000) over wages from June 1818, the confrontation at a factory in Ancoat’s Lane on September 2 — soldiers fired, one killed, a coroner’s verdict of justifiable homicide, the strike breaks up. The Congress of Aix-la-Chapelle (September–November 1818): the evacuation of the allied army of occupation from France, the Duke of Wellington’s crucial role in smoothing the negotiations and his appointment as Master-General of the Ordnance with a seat in the cabinet, Robert Owen of New Lanark presenting his schemes to the Emperor Alexander, Clarkson sent by Wilberforce to lobby for abolition (unsuccessful). Deaths: Queen Charlotte (November 17), Sir Samuel Romilly (suicide November 2, four days after losing his wife, aged 61 — Lord Eldon wept openly in court at the sight of Romilly’s empty seat), Lord Ellenborough (December 13), Warren Hastings (August 20), Sir Philip Francis (December). The economic state of the country at the close of 1818: better harvest, but prices still at scarcity levels, growing bankruptcies. The revival of reform agitation: Samuel Bamford introducing women’s voting at radical meetings in Lydgate (Saddleworth), the Birch meeting where Benbow urged marching to London “at the point of the sword and pike” before fleeing to America, Cobbett’s letter from America ridiculing Jeremy Bentham

  • Chapter XV — The Close of the Session — the opening of the new parliament in January 1819, the Regent’s speech read by commission (mentioning the Queen’s death, the Aix-la-Chapelle treaty, and the commercial convention with the US), Sir James Mackintosh’s successful motion for a select committee on criminal law reform (reducing capital offences), debates on Catholic emancipation (a thin government majority), Scotch burgh reform, cabinet divisions over currency policy, Lord Liverpool’s letter to Eldon threatening resignation if financial measures failed, the state of parties — an administration increasingly divided within itself

  • Chapter XVI — The Reform Agitation and Peterloo (1819) — the pivotal chapter of the volume. The summer 1819 agitation: mass reform meetings, the election of “legislatorial attorneys,” female reform societies, radical drilling on Tandle Hill. Detailed account of the Manchester meeting of August 16, 1819: orderly processions from the manufacturing towns (Bamford’s Middleton contingent with laurel branches and a banner of “Unity and Strength”), the assembling of perhaps 60,000 people in St. Peter’s Field, Orator Hunt’s arrival in an open carriage, the magistrates’ decision to arrest the leaders, the disastrous charge of the Manchester and Salford Yeomanry (untrained special constables on horseback, many drunk), the intervention of the 15th Hussars, the resulting massacre with 11–15 killed and 400–700 wounded, the panicked flight of the crowd. Draws on Bamford’s memoir, Sir William Jolliffe’s account, and the parliamentary evidence

  • Chapter XVII — Aftermath of Peterloo — the conduct of the magistrates — the government immediately supporting their actions, the Prince Regent’s letter of approval, nationwide protest meetings, Sir Francis Burdett’s inflammatory letter to his constituents (prosecuted for libel, convicted and fined), the York trials of the radical leaders, the deep division in the cabinet over whether to charge with treason (Eldon arguing for it, the rest opposed — Eldon prevailed in some cases but not in the mass), Hunt’s trial (sentenced to 2½ years in Ilchester Gaol), the passage of the Six Acts (1819): the Training Prevention Act, the Seizure of Arms Act, the Seditious Meetings Prevention Act, the Blasphemous and Seditious Libels Act, the Newspaper and Stamp Duties Act, the Misdemeanours Act — the most repressive legislation of the post-war period

BOOK II — From the Cato Street Conspiracy to the End of Liverpool’s Ministry (1819–1826)

  • Chapter I — The Revival of Sedition and the Cato Street Conspiracy — the revival of sedition after Peterloo, the Cato Street plot (February 1820): Arthur Thistlewood and a small group of radicals plotting to murder the entire cabinet at a dinner at Lord Harrowby’s house, the government’s knowledge through spies, the dramatic arrest in the Cato Street stable (Thistlewood killed a police officer, Richard Smithers), the trial and execution of five conspirators (Thistlewood, Ings, Brunt, Tidd, Davidson — beheaded after hanging), Edwards the spy controversy (accusations of agent provocateur), the true character of Hunt revealed, the lessons learned by experience, public sentiment that the conspirators were as much victims of government provocation as genuine revolutionaries

  • Chapter II — The Queen Caroline Affair (1820–1821) — the unhappy marriage of George IV and Caroline of Brunswick, Queen Caroline’s return from her Continental exile to assert her rights, the King’s message to Parliament demanding a divorce, the introduction of the Bill of Pains and Penalties in the House of Lords (depriving Caroline of her title and dissolving the marriage), the “trial” with evidence of Caroline’s alleged adultery with Bartolomeo Bergami, Brougham and Denman’s brilliant defence (Denman’s famous “where is the injured husband?” speech), the enormous public sympathy for the Queen (mobs supporting Caroline, the King hissed in the streets), the abandonment of the bill after a narrow majority of nine in the Lords on the third reading, the Queen’s death (August 1821) and funeral (the procession forced through the City over the government’s objections, two men killed in the Hyde Park confrontation)

  • Chapter III — Domestic Events (1821) — the dissolution of parliament after George IV’s accession, the King’s visit to Ireland (the first by a reigning monarch since the Union — a success that temporarily quieted Irish discontent), the death of Henry Grattan, popular education, agricultural distress, continuing but reduced reform agitation, the Constitutional Association (the “Bridge Street Gang” for prosecuting libel), the death of Napoleon at St. Helena (May 5, 1821), the cautious steps toward trade recovery and liberalising economic policy

  • Chapter IV — The Liverpool Ministry in Transition — the weakening of Liverpool’s ministry in 1821–22: unpopular with the King, internally divided by the Catholic question, facing growing pressure for liberal reform — the strength and fragility of the ministry assessed, the retirement of Lord Sidmouth from the Home Office, Mr. Canning’s position (refusing to serve under Liverpool, out of office). The condition of Ireland, the Peterborough Church questions, the new Marriage Act, the death of Lord Londonderry (Castlereagh, August 1822 — suicide by cutting his throat in a paroxysm of overwork and paranoid delusions). Canning becomes Foreign Secretary, Huskisson at the Board of Trade, Peel at the Home Office — the transition from old Tory repression to liberal conservatism

  • Chapter V — Foreign Affairs Under Canning — the transformation of foreign policy from 1822: the princes of Europe and the Congress of Verona, Canning breaking from the Holy Alliance, his “system” of recognising de facto governments, the French invasion of Spain (1823) to restore the Bourbon monarchy, Canning’s opposition and the British refusal to participate, his great speech on the Spanish question, the recognition of the South American republics (Argentina, Colombia, Mexico — “I called the New World into existence to redress the balance of the Old”), British naval demonstration preventing European intervention in the Americas, the protection of Portugal from Spanish invasion, the death of John VI of Portugal, the death of Alexander I of Russia (1825), the last war with Algiers, the Burmese War, the Oregon question, the renewal of the Alien Act, the influence of foreigners in British political discourse

  • Chapter VI — Domestic Reform Under Liverpool — a new period opening after the accession of the liberal Tories to key posts, the Cabinet of 1823, Canning and Huskisson’s influence and the opposition of Eldon, Lord Chancellor Eldon and his resistance to change, the debt and the burden of taxation, commercial policy reform: Huskisson’s reduction of the Navigation Acts and the Reciprocity Bill (allowing reciprocal tariff arrangements with other maritime nations), the reform of the Combination Acts (allowing workmen to combine for wage negotiations), the Gaols Act, the sliding scale for corn duties. The Catholic claims: renewed debates and the growth of the Catholic Association. Negro slavery: the government’s resolutions on amelioration, Barbadoes and Demerara, the Smith the Missionary case (a missionary executed for not suppressing a slave rebellion), the close of the session

  • Chapter VII — Trade, Manufactures, and Labour — economic expansion and its problems: the evils of the smuggling system (along the Kentish and Sussex coasts — a massive illicit industry fuelled by high protective duties), the reform of the Combination Acts and its effects on labour organisation, the silk duties debate and the discontent of the Coventry ribbon-weavers, the reduction of silk duties, tariff reform on wool and other raw materials, the debates on free trade principles, the close of the session

  • Chapter VIII — The Speculation Mania and the Panic of 1825 — a classic financial bubble and crash: speculation and overtrading in 1824–25, the exportation of gold, the joint-stock company mania (companies floated for every purpose — Latin American mining, steam laundries, canals, gas-lighting, even a company for “milk and eggs from cows and poultry”) — shares bid to absurd prices, the collapse beginning in the autumn of 1825, the panic and the crash of the Stock Exchange, the run on the banks (70 country banks failed), the Bank of England’s desperate measures — the suppression of small notes (under £5), the Scotch banks and their superior stability during the crisis, the position of the ministers, the immense suffering of the time, the defeat of the government’s financial proposals in the aftermath

  • Chapter IX — Riots, Scarcity, and Emigration — the social consequences of the 1825 panic: riots in manufacturing towns (the Sunderland seamen’s riot — troops firing on strikers, killing five), the Isle of Man potato tithe riots (the poor successfully resisting collection, forcing the bishop to yield), other outbreaks of popular violence, the alarm of scarcity, the opening of bonded corn to relieve distress, the Colonial Office and the beginnings of a systematic emigration policy, the Emigration Committee and the first experiments in assisted emigration to Canada, the reform of the poor laws urged

  • Chapter X — The Catholic Question — a comprehensive historical review of the Catholic question from the Reformation to the 1820s: the Treaty of Limerick and its violated guarantees, the penal laws under Henry VIII, Elizabeth, and the Stuarts, the 18th-century relaxations, Irish interest and Mr. Grattan, the coronation oath and the argument that it forbade Catholic emancipation, the disabilities of Catholics (excluded from Parliament, the higher offices of state, the universities), the state of opinion in 1824, the Catholic deputation to London, Mr. Canning’s speech in favour of relief, Mr. O’Connell and the New Catholic Association (the “Catholic rent” of a penny a month), the Duke of York’s speech against emancipation (declaring his unalterable opposition to the last hour of his life), Mr. Plunket’s measured advocacy, Catholics and Dissenters compared

  • Chapter XI — Parliamentary and Legal Reform — Chancery reform and the campaign against the “insufferable” delays of the Court of Chancery, John Williams leading the inquiry against the obstruction of Lord Chancellor Eldon, reports of the Chancery Commissioners, the reform of the criminal code continued by Peel (reduction of capital offences — the consolidation of the criminal law), jurors in India, the state of the national finances, Lord John Russell’s motion on the state of the country, the disenfranchisement of Grampound (a notoriously corrupt Cornish borough, its seats transferred to Yorkshire), the general election of 1823, the drought and harvest of that year, the irresistible spirit of reform

  • Chapter XII — Society and Culture (1820s) — the concluding chapter, a social and cultural survey. Capital punishment and banditti: the shocking frequency of executions (boys hanged alongside hardened criminals), the discovery of a Gloucestershire banditti gang living in a subterranean cavern, popular ignorance and the efforts of the education societies (British and Foreign School Society, National Society, the Church of England’s National schools), prosecutions for blasphemy (Richard Carlile and the republican publishers), the Society for the Diffusion of Useful Knowledge and the Penny Magazine, the encouragement of emigration. Public works: the Menai Suspension Bridge (Telford’s great work, opened 1826), gas-lighting spreading through the country, the Thames Tunnel under construction (Brunel’s project), the Stockton and Darlington Railway (1825) — the dawn of the railway age. North Polar expeditions and the search for the North-West Passage. Literature and the arts: the Edinburgh and Quarterly Reviews, the rise of the literary magazines, the death of the poets (Byron at Missolonghi 1824, Shelley drowned 1822, Keats at Rome 1821), the progress of science (Herschel, Davy, Jenner — their deaths noted), the Royal Society of Literature, artists lost (Fuseli), patrons of art (Sir George Beaumont), the death of Byron producing a reaction in English opinion. The transformation of English society since 1815
