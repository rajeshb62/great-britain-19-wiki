Marx and Engels — Manifesto of the Communist Party

Karl Marx (1818–1883) and Frederick Engels (1820–1895) — Manifesto of the Communist Party (German original 1848; this extract is an English text with the authorized Moore translation lineage and later prefaces). Commissioned at the November 1847 London Congress of the Communist League; manuscript sent to London for printing a few weeks before the February Revolution of 1848. First published in German; English first in Helen Macfarlane’s version in Harney’s Red Republican (1850); the authorized English translation by Samuel Moore, revised with Engels, London 1888 (William Reeves, 185 Fleet Street).

The extract is 2,270 lines / 18,899 words (121,106 bytes) from an Anna’s Archive plain-text dump (hash a07b587392459167b8c7d152b0c1a22a). It comprises successive prefaces (1872–1893), then the four chapters of the Manifesto proper with Engels’s notes from the 1888 English and related German editions. No separate modern scholarly foreword is present beyond the historical prefaces and notes in the file.

Content Summary

Prefaces

  • 1872 German Edition (Marx & Engels, London, 24 June 1872) — Origin of the Manifesto as programme of the Communist League (secret international workers’ association under pre-1848 conditions); commission at the November 1847 London Congress; manuscript to London shortly before the February French Revolution. Publication history: many German editions in Germany, England, and America; English first 1850 (Red Republican, Helen Macfarlane) and American translations 1871; French before the June 1848 insurrection and again in Le Socialiste (New York); Polish (London); Russian (Geneva, 1860s); Danish. General principles still correct; practical application depends on historical conditions; revolutionary measures at the end of Section II would be worded differently after modern industry’s strides and especially the Paris Commune (proletariat holding power two months)—“the working class cannot simply lay hold of the ready-made state machinery, and wield it for its own purposes” (Civil War in France, 1871). Criticism of socialist literature stops at 1847; Section IV on opposition parties is principle-correct but practice-antiquated. The Manifesto is a historical document they have no right to alter.

  • 1882 Russian Edition (Marx & Engels, London, 21 January 1882) — First Russian edition (Bakunin) early 1860s via the Kolokol printing office was a “literary curiosity”; no longer so. In 1847 Russia and the United States were missing from Section IV: Russia as reserve of European reaction, the United States absorbing surplus European proletarian forces; both as raw-material suppliers and markets for European industry. Now American agriculture shakes European landed property; American industry threatens Western European (especially English) industrial monopoly; a mass industrial proletariat and concentration of capital appear in the United States. The Tsar, once chief of European reaction, is “prisoner of war of the revolution in Gatchina”; Russia forms the vanguard of revolutionary action in Europe. Question of the Russian obshchina: if the Russian Revolution becomes the signal for a proletarian revolution in the West so that both complement each other, present Russian common ownership of land may serve as starting-point for communist development.

  • 1883 German Edition (Engels alone, London, 28 June 1883) — Signed alone after Marx’s death (14 March 1883; Highgate Cemetery). Basic thought of the Manifesto—economic production and the social structure arising from it as foundation of political and intellectual history; history (since dissolution of primeval communal land ownership) as history of class struggles; the proletariat can emancipate itself only by freeing the whole of society—belongs solely to Marx.

  • 1888 English Edition (Engels, London, 30 January 1888) — League history: German then international secret society; November 1847 commission; January 1848 German draft to London printer before 24 February; French before June 1848; Macfarlane English 1850; Danish and Polish. Defeat of the June 1848 Parisian insurrection drove proletarian aspirations into the background; propertied-class struggle resumed; Cologne Communist Trial (4 October–12 November 1852), fortress sentences, formal dissolution of the League; Manifesto seemed doomed to oblivion. International Working Men’s Association (1864) needed a broad programme acceptable to English trade unions, Proudhonists, and Lassalleans; Marx drew it; by the International’s break-up (1874) Proudhonism and Lassalleanism were dying and even English trade unions had advanced (Swansea president: “Continental socialism has lost its terror for us”). German text reprinted; 1872 New York English; authorized Moore translation revised jointly; Engels adds explanatory notes. Quotes 1872 preface on principles, Commune, and the Manifesto as unalterable historical document. Note equating the Manifesto’s basic thought, for history, with Darwin’s theory for biology; Engels’s independent approach shown in Condition of the Working Class in England; Marx had it worked out by spring 1845 Brussels.

  • 1890 German Edition (Engels) — Records further translations: second Russian (Vera Zasulich, Geneva 1882) with the Marx–Engels preface; Polish (Manifest Kommunistyczny, Geneva); Danish (Socialdemokratisk Bibliothek, Copenhagen 1885, incomplete/careless in places); French 1886 in Le Socialiste (Paris), best to date; Spanish from that French text in El Socialista (Madrid) and pamphlet form; Armenian manuscript offered at Constantinople but publisher refused Marx’s name. Authentic English 1888 (Moore). History of the Manifesto: early enthusiasm of scientific-socialism’s vanguard; background after June 1848; “excommunicated by law” after Cologne 1852; return with the workers’ movement and the International; May Day 1890 mass demonstrations for the eight-hour day as international proletarian solidarity (Paris Congress of the Second International, July 1889).

  • 1892 Polish Edition (Engels, London, 10 February 1892) — Demand for the Manifesto as index of large-scale industry’s development on the Continent. New Polish edition marks progress of Polish industry; Congress Poland as big industrial region of the Russian Empire, concentrated and hence both advantaged and a seedbed of socialist ideas. Polish independence necessary for harmonious collaboration of European nations; can be gained only by the young Polish proletariat; workers of the rest of Europe need Polish independence as much as Polish workers.

  • 1893 Italian Edition (Engels, London, 1 February 1893) — Manifesto’s publication coincided with 18 March 1848 (Milan and Berlin). Revolution of 1848 the work of the working class on the barricades; only Paris workers aimed at overthrowing the bourgeois regime, but conditions not yet ripe for social reconstruction; fruits reaped by the capitalist class. Bourgeois rule requires national independence; 1848 brought unity and autonomy to Italy, Germany, Hungary; Poland will follow. 1848 not socialist but paved the way by creating a numerous concentrated proletariat—“its own grave-diggers.” Without national autonomy and unity, international union of the proletariat is impossible. Italy as first capitalist nation; Dante as last poet of the Middle Ages and first of modern times; will Italy give the new Dante of the proletarian era?

Manifesto of the Communist Party

Opening: A spectre is haunting Europe—the spectre of Communism. All the powers of old Europe have entered into a holy alliance to exorcise it. It is high time that Communists should openly publish their views, aims, and tendencies, and meet the nursery tale of the Spectre with a manifesto of the party itself. Communists of various nationalities have assembled in London and sketched the following manifesto, to be published in English, French, German, Italian, Flemish, and Danish.

Chapter I. Bourgeois and Proletarians

The history of all hitherto existing society is the history of class struggles (Engels note: written history; since 1847, research on primeval communal forms—Haxthausen, Maurer, Morgan). Freeman and slave, patrician and plebeian, lord and serf, guild-master and journeyman; modern epoch simplifies antagonisms into two great hostile camps—bourgeoisie and proletariat (definitions in Engels’s 1888 note: owners of the means of social production employing wage labour; wage labourers reduced to selling labour power).

From the serfs of the Middle Ages sprang the chartered burghers; from these the first elements of the bourgeoisie. Discovery of America, rounding of the Cape, East Indian and Chinese markets, colonisation of America, trade with the colonies, increase of means of exchange and commodities, revolutionary impetus to feudal society. The feudal or guild system of industry no longer sufficed; manufacturing system took its place; markets kept growing; steam and machinery revolutionised industrial production; modern industry and the world market. The bourgeoisie has played a most revolutionary part: ended feudal, patriarchal, idyllic relations; left no other nexus than naked self-interest and callous “cash payment”; drowned religious fervour, chivalrous enthusiasm, philistine sentimentalism in the icy water of egotistical calculation; resolved personal worth into exchange value; set up free trade in place of chartered freedoms; stripped occupations of their haloes; converted the physician, the lawyer, the priest, the poet, the man of science into paid wage labourers; tore away the veil from the family and reduced it to a mere money relation.

Constant revolutionising of production; uninterrupted disturbance of all social conditions; everlasting uncertainty—“All that is solid melts into air, all that is holy is profaned.” Need of a constantly expanding market chases the bourgeoisie over the whole surface of the globe. Exploitation of the world market gives a cosmopolitan character to production and consumption; national industries destroyed or daily destroyed; raw material drawn from remotest zones; new wants requiring products of distant lands; intellectual creations become common property; national one-sidedness becomes more impossible. Country subjected to the towns; barbarian and semi-barbarian countries dependent on the civilised, the East on the West. Agglomeration of population, centralisation of means of production, concentration of property, political centralisation. In scarce one hundred years the bourgeoisie has created more massive productive forces than all preceding generations together: subjection of Nature’s forces, machinery, chemistry in industry and agriculture, steam-navigation, railways, electric telegraphs, clearing of continents, canalisation of rivers, whole populations conjured out of the ground.

Feudal relations of property became fetters and were burst asunder; free competition and bourgeois sway stepped into their place. A similar movement now: modern bourgeois society is like the sorcerer no longer able to control the powers of the nether world he has called up. Commercial crises periodically destroy products and productive forces; epidemic of over-production. The weapons with which the bourgeoisie felled feudalism are turned against the bourgeoisie itself. But it has also called into existence the men who are to wield those weapons—the modern working class, the proletarians.

In proportion as the bourgeoisie is developed, the proletariat is developed—a class of labourers who live only so long as they find work, and who find work only so long as their labour increases capital. These labourers, who must sell themselves piecemeal, are a commodity. Owing to the extensive use of machinery and to division of labour, the work of the proletarians has lost all individual character; the cost of production of a workman is restricted almost entirely to the means of subsistence; wages equal to that cost; mass of labourers crowded into the factory organised like soldiers. Distinctions of age and sex have no longer any distinctive social validity; all are instruments of labour. No sooner is the exploitation of the labourer by the manufacturer ended than he is set upon by the other portions of the bourgeoisie—landlord, shopkeeper, pawnbroker.

Lower strata of the middle class sink into the proletariat. The proletariat goes through various stages of development: at first struggle of individual labourers, then of a factory, then of an entire trade in one locality, against the individual bourgeois who exploits them; destroy imported wares, smash machinery, set factories ablaze—destroy the instruments of production to restore the vanished status of the workman of the Middle Ages. At this stage labourers still form an incoherent mass scattered over the country; their combination not yet the consequence of their own active union but of the union of the bourgeoisie. With industry the proletariat not only increases in number; it becomes concentrated in greater masses, its strength grows, it feels that strength more. Interests and conditions of life within the ranks of the proletariat more and more equalised. Collisions take on the character of collisions between two classes; workers form combinations (trades’ unions) against the bourgeois; clubs permanent; here and there the contest breaks into riots. Now and then the workers are victorious, but only for a time; the real fruit of their battles lies not in the immediate result but in the ever-expanding union of the workers. This union is helped on by improved means of communication. National struggles of the proletarians of different countries are on the point of combination in one national struggle between classes. Organisation of the proletarians into a class, and consequently into a political party, is continually being upset again by competition between the workers themselves, but it ever rises up again stronger, firmer, mightier. It compels legislative recognition of particular interests of the workers (e.g. the ten hours’ bill in England).

The bourgeoisie finds itself in constant battle—with the aristocracy, with portions of the bourgeoisie itself whose interests have become antagonistic to the progress of industry, with the bourgeoisie of foreign countries—and is compelled to appeal to the proletariat, thus dragging it into the political arena and furnishing it with its own elements of political and general education. Entire sections of the ruling class are precipitated into the proletariat; a portion of the bourgeois ideologists who have raised themselves to the level of comprehending theoretically the historical movement as a whole goes over to the proletariat. Of all classes that stand face to face with the bourgeoisie today, the proletariat alone is a really revolutionary class. The lower middle class—small manufacturer, shopkeeper, artisan, peasant—are conservative or reactionary, seeking to roll back the wheel of history; if revolutionary, only in view of their impending transfer into the proletariat. The “dangerous class,” the social scum (lumpenproletariat), may be swept into the movement but is more prepared for the part of a bribed tool of reactionary intrigue.

The proletarian is without property; modern industrial labour has stripped him of every trace of national character; law, morality, religion are to him so many bourgeois prejudices. All previous classes that got the upper hand sought to fortify their already acquired status; the proletarians cannot become masters of the productive forces of society except by abolishing their own previous mode of appropriation and thereby every other previous mode of appropriation. The proletarian movement is the self-conscious, independent movement of the immense majority in the interest of the immense majority. The essential condition for the existence and sway of the bourgeois class is the formation and augmentation of capital; the condition for capital is wage-labour; wage-labour rests on competition between the labourers. The advance of industry replaces the isolation of the labourers due to competition by their revolutionary combination due to association. What the bourgeoisie therefore produces, above all, are its own grave-diggers. Its fall and the victory of the proletariat are equally inevitable.

Chapter II. Proletarians and Communists

Relation of the Communists to the proletarians as a whole: Communists do not form a separate party opposed to other working-class parties; they have no interests separate and apart from those of the proletariat as a whole; they do not set up any sectarian principles. They are distinguished only by: (1) in the national struggles of the proletarians of the different countries, they point out and bring to the front the common interests of the entire proletariat, independently of all nationality; (2) in the various stages of development which the struggle of the working class against the bourgeoisie has to pass through, they always and everywhere represent the interests of the movement as a whole. Practically, the most advanced and resolute section of the working-class parties of every country; theoretically, they have over the great mass of the proletariat the advantage of clearly understanding the line of march, the conditions, and the ultimate general results of the proletarian movement. Immediate aim: formation of the proletariat into a class, overthrow of the bourgeois supremacy, conquest of political power by the proletariat.

Theoretical conclusions of the Communists are in no way based on ideas or principles invented or discovered by this or that would-be reformer; they merely express, in general terms, actual relations springing from an existing class struggle, from a historical movement going on under our very eyes. Abolition of existing property relations is not a distinctive feature of Communism; all property relations have been subject to historical change. The distinguishing feature is not the abolition of property generally, but the abolition of bourgeois property. Modern bourgeois private property is the final and most complete expression of the system of producing and appropriating products that is based on class antagonisms, on the exploitation of the many by the few. In this sense the theory of the Communists may be summed up in the single sentence: Abolition of private property.

Communism deprives no man of the power to appropriate the products of society; all that it does is to deprive him of the power to subjugate the labour of others by means of such appropriation. Objection that upon the abolition of private property all work will cease is answered: if that were so, bourgeois society ought long ago to have gone to the dogs through sheer idleness; those of its members who work acquire nothing, and those who acquire anything do not work. Culture under which the enormous majority have been stunted is, for the bourgeoisie, identical with the disappearance of all culture. Bourgeois notions of freedom, culture, law, &c., are but the outgrowth of bourgeois production and property. Charges concerning the family, education, and the “hallowed co-relation of parent and child” are answered by pointing to the practical absence of the family among the proletarians, to public prostitution, and to the education of children turned into a mere article of commerce. Bourgeois marriage is in reality a system of wives in common; the Communists might be reproached at most with desiring to introduce, in substitution for a hypocritically concealed, an openly legalised community of women—and abolition of the present system of production will abolish prostitution both public and private.

“The working men have no country.” National differences and antagonisms between peoples are daily more and more vanishing; supremacy of the proletariat will cause them to vanish still faster. United action of the leading civilised countries is one of the first conditions for the emancipation of the proletariat. Exploitation of one nation by another will be put an end to with the exploitation of one individual by another. Charges from a religious, philosophical, and ideological standpoint: man’s consciousness changes with every change in the conditions of his material existence; the ruling ideas of each age have ever been the ideas of its ruling class; the Communist revolution is the most radical rupture with traditional property relations and therefore with traditional ideas.

First step in the revolution: raise the proletariat to the position of ruling class, to win the battle of democracy. The proletariat will use its political supremacy to wrest, by degree, all capital from the bourgeoisie, to centralise all instruments of production in the hands of the State (the proletariat organised as the ruling class), and to increase the total productive forces as rapidly as possible. Despotic inroads on the rights of property unavoidable as means of entirely revolutionising the mode of production. In most advanced countries, generally applicable measures:

  1. Abolition of property in land and application of all rents of land to public purposes.
  2. A heavy progressive or graduated income tax.
  3. Abolition of all rights of inheritance.
  4. Confiscation of the property of all emigrants and rebels.
  5. Centralisation of credit in the hands of the state, by means of a national bank with State capital and an exclusive monopoly.
  6. Centralisation of the means of communication and transport in the hands of the State.
  7. Extension of factories and instruments of production owned by the State; the bringing into cultivation of waste-lands, and the improvement of the soil generally in accordance with a common plan.
  8. Equal liability of all to work. Establishment of industrial armies, especially for agriculture.
  9. Combination of agriculture with manufacturing industries; gradual abolition of all the distinction between town and country by a more equable distribution of the populace over the country.
  10. Free education for all children in public schools. Abolition of children’s factory labour in its present form. Combination of education with industrial production, &c., &c.

When class distinctions have disappeared and all production is concentrated in the hands of a vast association of the whole nation, the public power will lose its political character. Political power is merely the organised power of one class for oppressing another. If the proletariat, organised as a class, by revolution makes itself the ruling class and sweeps away the old conditions of production, it will have swept away the conditions for the existence of class antagonisms and of classes generally, and will thereby have abolished its own supremacy as a class. In place of the old bourgeois society, with its classes and class antagonisms, an association in which the free development of each is the condition for the free development of all.

Chapter III. Socialist and Communist Literature

1. Reactionary Socialism

  • A. Feudal Socialism — Aristocracies of France and England, after July 1830 and the English reform agitation, reduced to literary battle; feudal Socialism half lamentation, half lampoon; waves the proletarian alms-bag but shows feudal coats of arms; French Legitimists and “Young England”; in practice join coercive measures against the working class; barter truth, love, and honour for traffic in wool, beetroot-sugar, and potato spirits. Clerical Socialism hand in hand with Feudal Socialism.
  • B. Petty-Bourgeois Socialism — In countries where modern civilisation has become fully developed, a new petty bourgeoisie oscillates between proletariat and bourgeoisie (e.g. in France); Sismondi as head of this school in France and England. Dissection of contradictions in modern production; but either wants to restore old means of production and exchange, or to cramp modern means within the framework of the old property relations—both reactionary and Utopian (corporate guilds for manufacture; patriarchal relations in agriculture).
  • C. German or “True” Socialism — German philosophers and semi-philosophers took French socialist literature without the social conditions of France; French literature stripped of immediate revolutionary significance became pure literature representing the Nature of Man; German Socialism proclaimed the German nation the model nation and the German petty philistine the typical man; became a weapon against the bourgeoisie in the hands of German absolute governments, and an expression of reactionary interests of the German philistine.

2. Conservative or Bourgeois Socialism — Part of the bourgeoisie desirous of redressing social grievances to secure continued existence of bourgeois society: economists, philanthropists, humanitarians, organisers of charity, societies for prevention of cruelty to animals, temperance fanatics, hole-and-corner reformers; Proudhon’s Philosophie de la Misère as example. Desire existing society minus its revolutionary elements; a bourgeoisie without a proletariat. Practical form: depreciate every revolutionary movement by showing only change in material conditions (meaning administrative reforms, not abolition of bourgeois relations of production) can help. Free trade, protective duties, prison reform—all “for the benefit of the working class.” Summed up: the bourgeois is a bourgeois—for the benefit of the working class.

3. Critical-Utopian Socialism and Communism — Not the literature of every great modern revolution that voiced proletarian demands (Babeuf, &c.). The first direct attempts of the proletariat in times of universal excitement necessarily failed; the revolutionary literature that accompanied them was reactionary in content (universal asceticism, social levelling in its crudest form). Socialist and Communist systems properly so called (Saint-Simon, Fourier, Owen, &c.) appear in the first undeveloped period of struggle between proletariat and bourgeoisie. They see class antagonisms and action of the decomposing elements in the prevailing form of society, but the proletariat, as yet in its infancy, offers to them the spectacle of a class without any historical initiative. They want to improve the condition of every member of society, even the most favoured; hence appeal to society at large without distinction of class, and preferentially to the ruling class. They reject all political, and especially all revolutionary, action; wish to attain their ends by peaceful means and small experiments. Critical element: attack every principle of existing society; hence full of the most valuable materials for the enlightenment of the working class. Their positive proposals (abolition of the distinction between town and country, of the family, of the carrying on of industries for private account, of the wage system; proclamation of social harmony; conversion of the functions of the State into a mere superintendence of production) point solely to the disappearance of class antagonisms which were, as yet, only just cropping up. Significance is in inverse ratio to historical development; though the originators were revolutionary in many respects, their disciples form mere reactionary sects.

Chapter IV. Position of the Communists in Relation to the Various Existing Opposition Parties

Section II has made clear relations to existing working-class parties such as the Chartists in England and the Agrarian Reformers in America. Communists fight for attainment of the immediate aims and momentary interests of the working class, but in the movement of the present they also represent and take care of the future of that movement.

  • France — Ally with the Social-Democrats against the conservative and radical bourgeoisie, reserving the right to take up a critical position toward phases and illusions traditionally handed down from the great Revolution (Engels note: party of Ledru-Rollin, Louis Blanc, Réforme).
  • Switzerland — Support the Radicals, without losing sight of antagonistic elements (Democratic Socialists in the French sense; radical bourgeois).
  • Poland — Support the party that insists on an agrarian revolution as the prime condition for national emancipation (the party which fomented the Cracow insurrection of 1846).
  • Germany — Fight with the bourgeoisie whenever it acts in a revolutionary way against the absolute monarchy, the feudal squirearchy, and the petty bourgeoisie; never cease to instill into the working class the clearest possible recognition of the hostile antagonism between bourgeoisie and proletariat, so that after the fall of the reactionary classes the fight against the bourgeoisie itself may immediately begin. Attention chiefly to Germany, on the eve of a bourgeois revolution under more advanced conditions of European civilisation and with a much more developed proletariat than England in the seventeenth or France in the eighteenth century; the German bourgeois revolution will be but the prelude to an immediately following proletarian revolution.

Communists everywhere support every revolutionary movement against the existing social and political order of things. In all these movements they bring to the front, as the leading question in each, the property question. They labour everywhere for the union and agreement of the democratic parties of all countries. They disdain to conceal their views and aims. They openly declare that their ends can be attained only by the forcible overthrow of all existing social conditions. Let the ruling classes tremble at a Communistic revolution. The proletarians have nothing to lose but their chains. They have a world to win.

Working Men of All Countries, Unite!
