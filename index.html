The fundamental inquiry into the Corporation of the City of London: its limits and municipal territory, the wards and wardmote elections, the Lord Mayor, Court of Aldermen, Common Council, Sheriffs, courts, freemen and liverymen, property and revenue, the London Companies, the Southwark connexion, and the evidence and correspondence. Read source page

  • Royal Commission — Report of the Commissioners Appointed to Inquire into the Existing State of the Corporation of the City of London (1854). The Royal Commission into the Corporation of the City of London: its constitution, order, and government, jurisdictions, prisons and police, elections, freemen and liverymen, property, revenue, and expenditure, with the thirty-two recommendations for reform and the Minutes of Evidence and Appendix. Read source page

  • Justin McCarthy — A History of Our Own Times: From the Accession of Queen Victoria to the General Election of 1880. One-volume Burt edition covering Victoria’s accession through 1880; chapters I–XLVII cover 1837–1865 and chapters XXX–XLVII are especially useful for the post-Crimean period through Palmerston’s death. Read source page

  • Justin McCarthy — A History of Our Own Times, Vol. II. Belford, Clarke Vol. II, beginning with chapter XXX, “The Lorcha Arrow,” and carrying the narrative from the post-Crimean settlement through the later Victorian continuation; chapters XXX–XLVII cover 1856–1865. Read source page

  • Babbage, Charles — Reflections on the Decline of Science in England (1830). A polemic on the state of British science, criticising the Royal Society and calling for reform. Read source page

  • Buller, Charles — Responsible Government for Colonies (1840). The most sustained contemporary argument for applying the Westminster model of cabinet government to the British colonies, written in support of Lord Durham’s Report by his former secretary. Read source page

  • Carey, Henry C. — The Credit System in France, Great Britain, and the United States (1838). An economic analysis comparing banking, credit, and confidence across the three nations, drawing on data from the 1810s–1830s. Read source page

  • Gilbart, J. W. — The Laws of the Currency, as Exemplified in the Circulation of Country Bank Notes in England, since the Passing of the Act of 1844 (Journal of the Statistical Society of London, 1854). Statistical paper on English country-note circulation after Peel’s Bank Charter Act: seasonal “laws” for England, Scotland, and Ireland; extinction of issues; 1847 pressure; recommendations against a single amalgamated issue. Read source page

  • Imlac — Insecurity of the British Funds / Essay on Public Credit (1817). A post-war tract reprinting Hume’s essay on public credit with commentary on the national debt, the funding system, paper money, and the risk of national bankruptcy. Read source page

  • Hone, William — The Three Trials of William Hone (1818). Self-published report of the three Guildhall prosecutions (18–20 December 1817) for political parodies on the Catechism, Litany, and Athanasian Creed; ex-officio informations before Abbott and Ellenborough; three Not Guilty verdicts; Address, subscription meeting for liberty of the press and trial by jury, full parody texts, and (in this scan) later Queen Caroline–era bound pamphlets. Read source page

  • Hone, William — Pamphlets and Parodies on Political Subjects (Chidley collection, 1830). Nine Cruikshank-illustrated political squibs of 1820–22 reissued together: Political House that Jack Built, Queen’s Matrimonial Ladder, Miraculous Host, Caroline Form of Prayer, Non Mi Ricordo!, Political Showman, Man in the Moon, Right Divine of Kings to Govern Wrong!, and Slap at Slop (Bridge-Street Gang). Read source page

  • Hone, William — The Every-Day Book, Vol. I (1826). Day-by-day everlasting calendar of popular amusements, saints’ days, customs, and seasons (January–December), with 170 engravings; dedicated to Charles Lamb and Miss Lamb; preface and explanatory address; six indexes (subjects, saints, poetry, floral directory, correspondents, engravings). Read source page

  • Carlile, Richard — The Republican, Volume VIII (11 July–26 December 1823). Weekly freethought and republican journal from Dorchester Gaol; dedicated to Spaniards in Exile; Nos. 1–25 at 6d.; war with the Vice Society, materialism vs Methodism and Swedenborgianism, Dorset addresses, Elizabeth Fry, and Mechanics’ Institutes as temples of science. Read source page

  • Carlile / Hone / Cobbett — Harrison-bound radical miscellany. Hone’s Political House that Jack Built; Cobbett 1817 Weekly Political Register / Political Pamphlet (Vol. 32); Carlile’s The Republican No. 2, Vol. I (3 September 1819, 2d.) with letters to the Prince Regent and Sidmouth on the 16 August 1819 Manchester killings (the first Republican Peterloo locus in this wiki). Read source page

  • Engels, Friedrich — The Condition of the Working Class in England (1845). A contemporary investigation of the English working class during the Industrial Revolution, based on Engels’s two-year observation in Manchester and official reports. Covers the factory system, the great towns, Irish immigration, labour movements (Chartism, trade unions), the mining and agricultural proletariats, and the attitude of the bourgeoisie. Read source page

  • Marx, Karl, and Frederick Engels — Manifesto of the Communist Party (1848; this extract Moore/Engels English lineage with prefaces 1872–1893). Programme of the Communist League: bourgeoisie and proletarians, proletarians and Communists, socialist and communist literature, position of Communists to the various existing opposition parties; closing “Working Men of All Countries, Unite!”; later prefaces on the Paris Commune and international circulation. Read source page

  • James, William — Naval History of Great Britain, Vol. I: Introduction and the French Revolutionary War (1488–1796). Covers the history of naval architecture and ship classification, then the first four years of the French Revolutionary War: Lord Howe’s campaigns, the Glorious First of June, Hood at Toulon and Corsica, Bridport off Isle-Groix, Hotham off Genoa and Hyères, Cornwallis’s retreat, the capture of the Cape of Good Hope, and the war with Holland and Spain (1796). Read source page

  • James, William — Naval History of Great Britain, Vol. II: The Crisis Years (1797–1799). The mutinies at Spithead and the Nore, Jervis at Cape St. Vincent, Camperdown, Nelson at Santa Cruz and the Nile, the Bonaparte’s Egyptian expedition, Sir Sidney Smith at Acre, the Anglo-Russian expedition to Holland, and extensive light squadron and single-ship operations. Read source page

  • James, William — Naval History of Great Britain, Vol. III: From the Armed Neutrality to Trafalgar (1800–1805). The Baltic expedition and Copenhagen, the Peace of Amiens, Napoleon’s invasion preparations, Villeneuve’s chase across the Atlantic, the Battle of Trafalgar and Nelson’s death, the storm and its aftermath. Read source page

  • James, William — Naval History of Great Britain, Vol. IV: From the Declaration of War in 1793 to the Accession of George IV (1805–1809). A detailed chronological account of British naval operations from the aftermath of Trafalgar through the Basque Roads and Walcheren expeditions: Strachan’s action, Copenhagen, the Dardanelles, the Chesapeake affair, single-ship actions, and colonial campaigns across the Atlantic, Baltic, and East Indies. Read source page

  • James, William — Naval History of Great Britain, Vol. V: From the Declaration of War in 1793 to the Accession of George IV (1809–1813). A detailed chronological account of British naval operations: single-ship actions, fleet engagements, and colonial expeditions from the Napoleonic wars through the War of 1812, with annual abstracts of the Royal Navy. Read source page

  • James, William — Naval History of Great Britain, Vol. VI: From the Declaration of War in 1793 to the Accession of George IV (1813–1827). The final volume covering the end of the Napoleonic wars, the War of 1812 (Shannon and Chesapeake, the burning of Washington, Baltimore, Lake Champlain, New Orleans), and the post-war period: the bombardment of Algiers, the Burmese War, and the Battle of Navarino. Read source page

  • Clowes, Sir William Laird — The Royal Navy: A History, Vol. V (1803–1815). The whole naval struggle with Napoleon: the Trafalgar campaign, the blockade system, and the minor single-ship actions, covering civil administration, major fleet operations, and minor operations with appendices of ships taken and lost by each belligerent. Read source page

  • Clowes, Sir William Laird — The Royal Navy: A History, Vol. VI (1812–1856). The War of 1812 (by Theodore Roosevelt), the peacetime Navy 1816–1856 (Algiers, Navarino, St. Jean d’Acre, the Crimean War and Sebastopol), the transition from sail to steam, and Arctic and Antarctic voyages of discovery (by Sir Clements Markham). Read source page

  • Clowes, Sir William Laird — The Royal Navy: A History, Vol. VII (1857–1900). The late Victorian Navy from the Second China War and Indian Mutiny through the bombardment of Alexandria, the Sudan campaigns, and the Boer War; the transition to ironclads; Arctic, Antarctic, and the Challenger expedition (by Sir Clements Markham). Read source page

  • James, William — A Full and Correct Account of the Chief Naval Occurrences of the Late War Between Great Britain and the United States of America (1817). A contemporary British account of the naval War of 1812, written while details were still being gathered from officers across the United Kingdom. Covers every major naval action (Guerriere, Java, Macedonian, Shannon and Chesapeake, Essex, Lake Erie, President, Constitution vs. Cyane and Levant) with comparative force calculations, British and American official accounts compared, and an appendix of official documents. Read source page

  • Mayhew, Henry — The Morning Chronicle Survey of Labour and the Poor: The Metropolitan Districts (1850). A compilation of 14 of Mayhew’s Morning Chronicle letters (Letters XXVII–XL, Jan–Mar 1850) investigating vagrancy, casual wards, low lodging-houses, the boot and shoe trade, slop work, toy-makers, and doll manufacture in the London metropolitan districts. Read source page

  • Mayhew, Henry — London Labour and the London Poor, Vol. I: The London Street-Folk (1861–62). The first volume of Mayhew’s encyclopædic survey, covering the street-folk: wandering tribes, costermongers, street-sellers of fish, fruit, vegetables, game, flowers, green stuff, eatables and drinkables, stationery and literature, manufactured articles, and the women and children street-sellers. Read source page

  • Mayhew, Henry — London Labour and the London Poor, Vol. II: The London Street-Folk, Book the Second (1861–62). The second volume of the survey, continuing the Street-Folk subject: street-sellers of second-hand articles and live animals, street-buyers, the street Jews, street-finders and collectors, the sanitary condition of London’s streets (dust-yards, sewers, night-soil), chimney-sweepers, and crossing-sweepers. Read source page

  • Mayhew, Henry — London Labour and the London Poor, Vol. III: The London Street-Folk (1861–62). The third volume of the survey, covering street-folk trades and labour: destroyers of vermin (rat-killers, bug-destroyers), street-exhibitors (Punch, acrobats, conjurors), street-musicians, street-vocalists, street-artists, trained animals, garret-masters (cheap cabinet-makers), coal-heavers, ballast-men, lumpers, dock-labourers, cheap lodging-houses, London watermen, omnibus-drivers, cab-drivers, carmen, vagrants, and ticket-of-leave men. Read source page

  • Mayhew, Henry — London Labour and the London Poor, Vol. IV: Those That Will Not Work — Prostitutes, Thieves, Swindlers, Beggars and Vagrants (1861–62).
